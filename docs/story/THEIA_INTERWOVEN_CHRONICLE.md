# THEIA — THE INTERWOVEN CHRONICLE

Before there was a world, there was no sky to divide above from below, no distance to cross, and no moment by which one event could be measured against another. Neither the Physical Realm nor the Void existed. There were no realms at all, no established laws of nature, and no boundary separating what could exist from what could not. Yet two primordial essences endured beyond every condition that later beings would call reality.

**Aethyr** was the supreme creative will: not merely a god of life or light, but the potential for existence itself, the power by which something might arise where nothing had been. **Nhal** was its opposite: not a creature of darkness dwelling in some already formed abyss, but the supreme will to negate, to undo whatever might be made, until even the possibility of its existence was extinguished. Their power was older and more fundamental than the gods who would one day emerge. Where Aethyr sought to bring forth creation, Nhal sought to unmake it.

Their struggle had no battlefield, because space did not yet exist. It had no beginning that could be recorded, because time had not yet begun. Later minds might describe it as lasting eons, but even that word borrows a measure from a universe that had not been born. Every expression of Aethyr's creative purpose met Nhal's opposing will; every act of unmaking was resisted by the possibility of becoming. Neither could overcome the other. Their contest settled into an immeasurable stalemate, and no creation could be assured of enduring while its destruction remained within Nhal's reach.

In that impasse Aethyr conceived a different kind of victory. To defeat Nhal outright had proved impossible. But perhaps Nhal did not need to be destroyed. If creation could be given a realm of its own, and Nhal confined to another realm from which it could not freely reach what Aethyr made, existence might finally be allowed to unfold. The answer was not greater force in the old struggle; it was to establish the very conditions under which that struggle could no longer proceed as before.

The cost was Aethyr itself.

Aethyr understood that creating such a separation would require an act beyond ordinary creation: the breaking of its own primordial unity. The power that had resisted Nhal for an immeasurable existence would be spent in a single deliberate sacrifice. Aethyr would not remain whole to govern the result, to witness its future, or to defend each new life personally. Yet if the plan succeeded, what came afterward would have something Aethyr had never been able to guarantee—**the chance to exist without being immediately unmade**.

And so Aethyr chose.

In an instant beyond the measure of any clock, Aethyr shattered itself.

This was the **First Sundering**. The eruption of primordial energy did not merely fill an empty universe; it **brought two distinct realms into existence**. One was the **Physical Realm**, in which space, time, matter and the fundamental laws of magic could arise and endure. The other was the **Void**, not a second world of mountains and cities, but a realm of negation beyond the conditions of ordinary physical existence.

The force of the shattering drove Nhal into the newly formed Void. Aethyr's own shattered energies passed into the Physical Realm. In its final creative act, Aethyr established a **Veil between the two realms**—a fundamental separation intended to prevent Nhal from reaching across and unmaking the existence now taking shape.

It was not a prison built around an existing abyss. The prison, the world it protected, and the boundary between them were consequences of the same sacrifice. **Aethyr had changed the terms of the primordial conflict by creating the separation that neither essence had previously possessed.**

Nhal survived. Its nature was primordial and could not be extinguished merely by being cast beyond the new physical order. But survival was not freedom. Nhal was confined beyond the Veil, unable simply to reach into the Physical Realm and erase whatever emerged there. Aethyr's purpose had been achieved, at a price no later god could fully comprehend.

The Veil had denied Nhal the power to intervene, **not the ability to observe**. From its confinement Nhal could witness the Physical Realm unfold: the Seven awakening, their differing ambitions, the emergence of mortal races and the increasingly intricate workings of magic. It could learn from what it perceived, but it could not reach across, change a mortal's thoughts, alter a spell, send a warning or touch the smallest stone. Its perception was not omniscience; hidden motives and future choices remained uncertain. For ages it watched Aethyr's fragments build a world it could not unmake. This long enforced observation would later give Nhal knowledge of the ambitions it might exploit, should any means of influence ever become possible.

Aethyr, as a single consciousness, was gone.

Within the Physical Realm, seven great concentrations of its shattered essence awakened as independent beings. They inherited extraordinary power, but not the whole mind, memory or intention of the one who had sacrificed itself. They did not know the long struggle, the revelation that had ended it, or the reason the Veil existed. None was appointed to rule the others. These seven would become known as **the Seven**.

And so creation began with a paradox that would haunt every later age: the first act that made lasting life possible was an act of self-destruction, undertaken to prevent a still-living enemy from destroying all that might follow. The gods would inherit the world made by that choice without understanding its price. Nhal, beyond the Veil, would remember what they had forgotten.

**Aethyr chose sacrifice so that creation could finally have a future.**

In the ages that followed, the Seven felt a strange familiarity with one another but could not explain it. No one was their king. They discovered their powers gradually, learned magical laws rather than inventing them, and developed independent personalities, ambitions and loyalties.

The seven did not awaken together. They emerged into the young Physical Realm at different moments and came to know themselves before they knew one another. No god was appointed sovereign over the others; no voice explained that they were fragments of Aethyr. Their awakenings were separated by stretches of solitude in which each began to understand the world independently. What mattered was that relationships formed through experience rather than being inherited fully made.

Among the earliest and most consequential was the bond between **Guardian and Warrior**. It did not begin in agreement. The young realm was still turbulent, its first enduring formations threatened by the forces that shaped them. Guardian regarded their fragility as a reason to intervene; Warrior questioned whether constant shelter could produce anything capable of standing on its own. Guardian answered that a thing destroyed before it had a chance to grow could never learn strength. Warrior argued that strength granted entirely by another's intervention was not strength discovered for oneself. Neither wanted pointless destruction. Neither was prepared to surrender the principle at stake.

They returned to the disagreement, observed the consequences of their choices and slowly learned to respect what the other understood. Guardian came to recognize the dignity of resilience and independent will. Warrior came to see that protection could make strength possible rather than diminish it. Disagreement became conversation, conversation became cooperation, and cooperation became the closest friendship among the Seven. They would not always agree—especially when honour and sacrifice later came into conflict—but they learned to trust that the other acted from a serious conviction.

Elsewhere, **Seeker** investigated the patterns underlying the newborn realm. The existence of matter and energy did not explain why particular forms endured or why a magical arrangement responded to intention. Seeker learned to distinguish reproducible law from coincidence and to value an unanswered question more than an elegant but untested certainty. **Shaper**, working independently, approached the same world from the opposite direction. Where Seeker wished to understand a phenomenon, Shaper wished to make something with it. Shaper's first works were experiments in form, construction and transformation, each one an argument that reality could become more than it had been.

Their meeting began with an anomaly. Seeker encountered one of Shaper's formations and found that it remained stable despite violating an accepted model. Shaper did not know the full reason it worked; Seeker had not imagined that arrangement. Seeker proposed an adjustment and Shaper gave it form. A structure that had survived by inspired trial became something they could understand, refine and reproduce. Then Shaper tried a variation Seeker had not anticipated and exposed another principle. Each discovered that the other's gift could carry their own further than solitary work.

The collaboration grew into affection and intellectual trust. Seeker admired Shaper's courage to attempt the seemingly impossible. Shaper admired Seeker's ability to turn invention into dependable knowledge. Their investigations transformed the young Physical Realm: stable structures, carefully governed energies and increasingly sophisticated patterns became possible. Keeper would later recognize that the stability of such works depended on rules neither partner always respected; Judge would see that powerful creators needed responsibilities as well as freedom. Yet these concerns had not yet broken the extraordinary confidence of the early age.

The first truly transformative question emerged from Seeker and Shaper's work: **could the principles that sustained a magical form also permit an independent living consciousness?** Not merely a moving construct, nor a servant echoing a maker's intentions, but a being able to experience the world, choose, learn and become something its creator had never planned.

Seeker and Shaper made the foundational discovery that independent mortal life was possible. But possibility was not proof of safety. They could model complex self-sustaining patterns and describe the conditions under which individuality might arise, yet they could not confidently establish what would separate a free consciousness from an extension of divine will, or how such a being could endure without constant intervention. **They chose not to attempt mortal creation yet.** This was a deliberate pause because their knowledge remained incomplete, not a failure of imagination. Seeker believed further understanding was owed to any life they might bring forth. Shaper accepted the pause but felt the frustration of seeing an extraordinary possibility left unrealized.

Their findings reached Guardian and Warrior, who saw in them questions different from those the two researchers had asked. Guardian wanted life that could flourish for its own sake, even if its maker could not predict what it would become. Warrior wanted life that could choose, strive and overcome difficulty through its own will. Their old disagreement—protection against the danger of overprotection—became the source of two complementary approaches to the unresolved problem. Rather than wait for a complete theoretical account, they took Seeker and Shaper's research further through their own understanding of living independence and will.

**Guardian created the elves, the first of the mortal races.** They were not instruments fashioned to worship or obey. Their capacity for life, relationship and adaptation gave them a future their creator could nurture but not wholly command. **Warrior created the orcs**, likewise free to grow beyond their maker's expectations, with the potential for endurance, courage, communal honour and the strength to face adversity. Their creations followed close enough in the earliest history that each god could witness the other's achievement. Neither regarded the other people as a failed version of its own. For Guardian and Warrior, the difference between elves and orcs confirmed the lesson their friendship had taught them: independent life could flourish through more than one set of virtues.

The new races changed the other gods as profoundly as they changed the world. Their lives revealed that the creation of a person was not the completion of a work but the beginning of a history. They made choices, formed bonds and altered their surroundings. Even their divine makers were surprised. Guardian learned the pain of watching a beloved creation risk harm. Warrior discovered that honour freely chosen by mortals meant more than honour simply imposed. Seeker found a new field of inquiry in the unpredictable development of mortal minds. Shaper watched beings transform the world around them and saw the possibility of creators who were themselves creations.

**Shaper then created the dwarves.** Inspired by the capacity of elves and orcs to act independently, Shaper envisioned people who would not merely inhabit a formed world but transform it through craft, construction and enduring works. Their aptitude for making was not a command to reproduce divine designs. It was an invitation to surpass them. When dwarves began inventing forms and techniques Shaper had not taught, the god experienced an extraordinary pride. Mortal admiration for Shaper's visible contributions would grow from these achievements, later becoming one source of Ascendant's envy.

Seeker did not create a separate one of the original six mortal races. Instead, Seeker became increasingly fascinated by mortal discovery itself. The elves, orcs and dwarves could observe, test and challenge even divine explanations. Seeker's role as investigator and teacher would later make the god a principal patron of halflings and Vaelari, though neither people had been created by Seeker. The distinction between **creator** and **patron** began to matter long before the modern religions took shape.

As mortals formed relationships and communities, **Judge** confronted questions that had scarcely existed before free races appeared. What law could govern beings who could refuse their makers? How should competing claims be reconciled? What obligations did power create? Judge created **humans**, whose adaptability and capacity to form intricate institutions reflected those questions. Their later principal devotion to Guardian would show that an origin did not determine a people's permanent faith.

**Keeper** observed another aspect of mortality. A person could be free and still fragile. A community could flourish and still collapse when its sustaining patterns failed. Keeper studied how living systems could remain stable through change without being held motionless by a divine hand. From this concern came **halflings**, whose inherited magical resilience would later express itself in sophisticated guild traditions and engineering. Like humans, halflings would eventually choose a principal patron other than their original creator: Seeker.

By this point **five mortal races existed: elves, orcs, dwarves, humans and halflings**. Their early communities were developing identities, knowledge and relationships independent of their gods. The five creators and Seeker had shared discoveries, disagreements, celebrations and the first anxieties of responsibility. This was the divine family and its living legacy into which Ascendant would enter.

**Ascendant had not only just awakened.** He had spent a long period independently exploring the realm, developing an understanding of adaptation, amplification and synthesis, and shaping a philosophy that emphasized the potential to go beyond an original design. When he finally found the others, he encountered equals who nevertheless possessed a history together that he could not share. Their friendships were established. Their work was visible in five living races. The other gods did not have to reject him deliberately for their ease with one another to feel like exclusion.

He admired much of what they had made. He also wondered whether they had left room for anything truly new. Seeker and Shaper's celebrated collaboration suggested a place for insight and invention, but Shaper was already beloved for the dwarves and other great works. Guardian and Warrior had proved that life could arise from different philosophies. Judge and Keeper had each contributed a people. Ascendant had extraordinary power, but no comparable legacy and no people who knew him as their maker.

His desire to contribute could still have become an uncomplicated source of creativity. Instead, recognition slowly became entangled with identity. He wanted to demonstrate that the world could not merely be preserved, understood or shaped: it could be **improved beyond the limits its first makers had accepted**. Keeper possessed knowledge of magical stability that Ascendant's independent experiments could not fully reproduce. Their future collaboration in creating the trolls therefore offered both a practical partnership and a chance for a lasting legacy. The later Kel'vari project would carry that same desire to a far more dangerous scale.

Ascendant's creative gifts were genuine, but recognition had become entangled with his sense of worth. Each new achievement offered the possibility of belonging, and each comparison with the others made that belonging feel more uncertain.

Keeper and Ascendant had not been close friends before their collaboration. Ascendant had come late to the divine community, discovering five mortal races already alive and the other gods' achievements already celebrated. Keeper possessed knowledge Ascendant needed: the understanding of how to give living magical structures stability without extinguishing their independence. Ascendant offered a complementary gift, the capacity to amplify and adapt what already existed. Together they began creating the trolls, the sixth mortal people.

Keeper wished to give them a stable magical constitution and the ordinary capacity to endure the changing world. Ascendant contributed an adaptable nature, but privately wanted something more personal from this creation. He had seen elves honour Guardian, orcs revere Warrior and dwarves celebrate Shaper. The new people might finally be a living testament to his own significance. And so he considered a subtle alteration to the sensitivity to primordial energy that each created race inherited through its ancestry.

Through the creation process, **each mortal race inherited traces of Aethyr's primordial energy**, the same fundamental power from which the Seven themselves had emerged. As the gods shaped their creations, that energy manifested in different ways, giving rise to distinct racial affinities and magical potential—not fragments of the gods' personal essences. Over time, studying these manifestations would lead to the discovery and understanding of the **six fundamental fields of magic**. **Ascendant secretly amplified the trolls' sensitivity to this primordial energy and its divine resonance**, hoping they would feel the presence of divinity more keenly and, in time, choose to honour him above the others. He did not give them the ability to heal any wound, survive any blow or evolve new powers when threatened. The alteration concerned their **spiritual receptivity**, not their combat strength. It offered no guarantee of direct conversation with a god, superior spellcasting, exceptional magical reserves or obedience.

The trolls emerged as free mortal beings. They could be injured, fall ill, age and die, as other races could. Their heightened sensitivity to Aethyr's primordial energy could express itself through experiences that their descendants interpreted as spiritual, through devotion to ritual, and through an unusually rich religious imagination. But it could not choose their beliefs for them. A troll could question a divine teaching, reject a priest's interpretation or follow a different patron. A people created with heightened awareness was not a people created without free will.

Keeper eventually recognized that Ascendant had altered the agreed design. The immediate danger was not an army of invulnerable beings; it was the attempt of one creator to shape the religious inclinations of independent lives for personal recognition. Keeper had consented to a stable, resilient people, not to an undisclosed amplification of their sensitivity to the gods. Removing the alteration after the trolls existed risked interfering with living beings who had committed no wrong. Keeper chose to preserve them, but the decision could not restore trust.

Ascendant defended the work: the trolls remained free, and their heightened spiritual awareness could be a genuine gift. Keeper did not deny either point. What Keeper could not accept was that Ascendant had decided, alone, to change the conditions under which those beings would develop their beliefs. The distinction mattered. **Ascendant had not forced the trolls to worship him, but he had tried to influence the path by which they might come to do so.**

The irony would unfold across generations. Troll religious traditions flourished, but devotion did not converge upon Ascendant. Some communities revered the promise of change and growth associated with him. Others increasingly honoured Keeper's restraint, stability and guardianship. Ascendant had helped create a people unusually attentive to divine questions, only to discover that their independence included the freedom to prefer his collaborator. His desire for recognition had produced an achievement he could neither claim exclusively nor control.

The discovery troubled the other gods as well. They admired the lives Keeper and Ascendant had made, yet the concealed alteration raised questions none could easily set aside: whether a creator could change an agreed design without consent, and how the freedom of a people could be protected from the ambitions of those who made them.

The consequences for Keeper and Ascendant were enduring. Keeper became more wary of undisclosed modifications to living magical structures, especially those that might influence the freedom of their inhabitants. Ascendant increasingly believed that his finest achievements would be judged by the manner of their making rather than their results.

Mortals were never confined to their creators' intentions. The orcs developed scholars as well as champions; dwarves became healers as well as masons; elves could choose lives that required no magical training. The traces of Aethyr's primordial energy inherited by mortal bloodlines expressed themselves differently across families and generations. In most, the inheritance was subtle. In rare lineages it could produce unusual aptitude, but neither ancestry nor divine patronage removed the need for training, discipline and choice.

The six peoples spread through the lands that became their ancestral homes. Human settlements followed rivers across inland plains, where dependable harvests made permanent institutions possible. Dwarven communities worked the mountains, opening mines and underground passages before expanding the great defensive and civic structures for which they became renowned. Orc Great Clans established communities across the grasslands, combining martial traditions with permanent settlements, sacred places and councils of their own. Halflings adapted underground valleys into networks of dwellings, waterways, workshops and mechanisms. Elves cultivated settlements within the primeval forests, developing traditions of stewardship alongside their understanding of living magic. Trolls established strongholds in rugged highlands, where chronicles, stonework and protective wards preserved the memory of earlier generations.

These were beginnings, not rigid boundaries. Traders crossed the distances between peoples; scholars travelled to places where unfamiliar methods might answer their questions. A dwarven engineer could learn from a halfling regulator without becoming halfling, and a human settlement might adopt techniques first developed by elves. Geography shaped customs, but contact and migration ensured that no people possessed a single unchanging way of life.

The gods observed these developments with different kinds of pride. Shaper saw mortals extend the possibilities of craft beyond anything directly taught to them. Seeker valued the questions they asked, especially when their experiments challenged accepted explanations. Guardian protected communities beyond the elves, while Judge watched human institutions develop interpretations of law that were not simply repetitions of divine instruction. Their appearances remained rare enough to matter. Much more commonly, mortals solved problems through the knowledge and relationships they built for themselves.

As communities grew, their uses of magic became more deliberate. Practitioners distinguished **six disciplines: Elements & Energy, Matter, Life, Form, Mind and Passage**. No discipline belonged exclusively to a particular race. Inherited affinity could make one field easier to apprehend, but sustained skill required practice and understanding. A working drew upon the practitioner's finite reserves, shaped by intention and precision. Those reserves recovered through rest, ambient energy and disciplined meditation; exhausting them imposed real limits. More complex workings required understanding several disciplines and the relationships between them. A gathering of untrained practitioners could not simply combine their strength into the skill of a master.

These limits encouraged cooperation. A settlement seeking protection might employ a specialist in Form to devise a stable pattern, another in Matter to prepare its supporting structure, and trained ward-keepers to maintain the finished work. Passage experts could establish connections between distant centres, but the undertaking required careful design, substantial resources and continuing maintenance. Life practitioners refined methods of healing while debating the limits of intervention; engineers discovered ways to renew charged objects and preserve complex workings. Extraordinary resurrection, stable matter created from nothing and far-ranging passages remained rare achievements, not ordinary conveniences.

The need to preserve such expertise gradually changed the nature of scholarship. Teachers who had once trained a handful of apprentices began exchanging records with distant colleagues. Schools grew around successful methods, disputed theories and shared ethical obligations. From these traditions emerged **three or four great independent magical orders**, alongside many smaller institutions. They were not divided one-to-one among the six disciplines. Their identities arose from differing philosophies, specialties and public responsibilities, and their members could challenge divine teaching as well as one another.

The orders became places where knowledge survived beyond an individual practitioner. Libraries preserved failed experiments alongside successful ones, allowing later researchers to recognise errors that might otherwise be repeated. Visiting scholars carried methods between cities. Councils debated the ownership of discoveries, the risks of experimental workings and the duties owed to communities that depended upon them. Divine founders, rare appearances and trusted intermediaries influenced their development, but the orders possessed their own authority and could disagree openly with the gods.

Their work increasingly entered daily life. A halfling regulator might maintain the flow of a dwarven defensive system. Elven and human healers could compare treatments across institutions, while Passage engineers joined markets and archives separated by great distances. A city ward created by exceptional practitioners could be maintained by specialists who had never met its designers. Such arrangements allowed communities to benefit from achievements far beyond the abilities of any one resident.

This was the **Age of Wonders**: not a time of limitless magic, but an age in which accumulated knowledge, patient engineering and cooperation made extraordinary achievements dependable. Conventional labour and magical craft remained interwoven. Fields still required cultivation, buildings needed repair, and every complex system depended upon people trained to sustain it.

The prosperity also changed what communities expected from the world around them. A city supplied through Passage routes could let older roads fall into disuse. A settlement protected for generations by a ward might retain fewer conventional defences. An intricate structure could continue operating long after the death of its designer, while the theory needed to rebuild it passed into the care of an increasingly narrow circle of specialists. The achievements of the age were real, and so was the dependence they created.

The greatest danger of the Age of Wonders did not begin as a council of seven gods agreeing to fashion a new people. It began with a private wound in the life of one god.

**Ascendant had never forgotten the trolls.** Their creation with Keeper had been a genuine achievement, and the trolls had become a free people with elaborate spiritual traditions. But Ascendant's concealed amplification of their sensitivity to divine essence had not produced the devotion he desired. Many honoured Keeper. Others honoured both creators, or drew their own conclusions about the gods. Ascendant had attempted to cultivate recognition without abolishing free will; their freedom had answered him in a way he could not control.

He had also learned something about Keeper. Beneath that god's caution lay an extraordinary command of how living magical patterns could endure without collapsing. Their estrangement was real, but Keeper did not wish every future exchange to be poisoned by the first betrayal. In the years that followed, Keeper cautiously permitted Ascendant to examine **limited portions of advanced stability research**. It was an attempt to restore a scholarly relationship under conditions both could understand. The permitted work concerned complex relationships among divine essence, living autonomy and the six disciplines. Certain incomplete experiments, whose effects Keeper could not explain or safely contain, remained restricted.

Ascendant accepted the arrangement. At first the exchange was honest. His insights into adaptation genuinely improved some of Keeper's theoretical models, and Keeper's methods corrected failures in Ascendant's own. But Ascendant noticed absences in the research: references without their underlying proofs, experiments whose conclusions were withheld, and patterns whose permitted forms implied more powerful variants. Keeper had drawn a boundary around knowledge for which no reliable safeguard yet existed. Ascendant began to regard that boundary as the very obstacle that had always prevented his genius from being recognized.

**Using the research Keeper had willingly granted him access to, Ascendant devised his own methods to reconstruct the gaps in the knowledge Keeper had deliberately withheld.** He then extended those recovered principles through his own mastery of amplification and synthesis. He knew he had exceeded the agreement. He did not tell Keeper. He was acting from conviction, wounded pride and the desire to create something undeniably his own.

His ambition had a precise object. The six original mortal races possessed distinct inherited affinities and could all learn magic through training, finite reserves, discipline and effort. Ascendant believed it possible to create the Kel'vari, whose consciousness could apprehend the relationships among magical disciplines with an ease other mortals rarely achieved. Such beings would not possess limitless energy or immunity to death. They would still have to learn, practice, eat, build, argue and live. But they might see structures in Form and Passage that even accomplished scholars overlooked, and advance the arts of the Physical Realm far beyond their makers' expectations.

A second quality arose from the same radical design. The six original species could not ordinarily have children across species boundaries. **Ascendant's Kel'vari alone would be compatible with any of the six.** Whether he foresaw all the social consequences is not established; the compatibility expressed his central philosophy that creation should be able to cross limitations its predecessors accepted.

Ascendant worked in secrecy, without the other gods contributing to the act of creation. He tested patterns, discarded failed structures and developed his own methods to combine the six magical fields with a precision previously unimagined in mortals. Many trials failed. Others succeeded because of his persistence and ingenuity. **Nhal neither guided these experiments nor contributed knowledge to the Kel'vari's creation.** The race's extraordinary aptitude was Ascendant's achievement, born of his determination to outshine the other gods. The unintended consequence was a possibility so remote that even Ascendant did not perceive it: in the hands of sufficiently gifted, trained and ambitious descendants, an exceptionally precise combination of all six fields might one day resonate with the Veil. This was not a flaw implanted by Nhal, an inevitable trait of every member of the race, or an open breach. It was an unrealized possibility within a creation whose potential exceeded its creator's foresight.

Beyond the Veil, Nhal watched. It had witnessed the birth of the first six races and the gods' growing relationships; now it saw Ascendant create beings capable of bringing the six expressions of Aethyr's primordial energy together at a depth mortals had never reached. Nhal recognized the significance because it remembered the primordial power that had formed its prison. But **recognition was not intervention**. It could not whisper to Ascendant, improve his results or change the Kel'vari's design. It could only hope that one day these new mortals would discover a configuration capable of disturbing the boundary.

Then the first of the **Kel'vari** awakened. The living consciousness was its own. Ascendant could not command its choices simply by having made it. More followed. They learned and formed attachments, developed preferences and disagreements, and began a history that did not belong to Ascendant alone. **He was their sole divine creator**, but he was not the author of every act they would undertake. At last he possessed a creation whose divine origin no other god could claim as shared labour.

He revealed them only after their independence and unusual aptitude could no longer be dismissed as speculation. Seeker was astonished by their capacity to understand magical relationships. Shaper admired the scale of Ascendant's accomplishment, even as the speed of its achievement unsettled old assumptions. Guardian saw free persons entitled to protection. Warrior respected their agency but warned that rulers might seek to possess their gifts. Judge questioned the secrecy of a creation whose consequences could affect every people. **Keeper recognized elements of research Ascendant had been forbidden to extend** and knew their agreement had been broken again.

Yet the other gods could not simply condemn the Kel'vari for their creator's conduct. They were alive, independent and, at first, flourishing. Keeper could identify unauthorized methods without proving that the new lives were themselves dangerous. Seeker insisted that a discovery could be examined without punishing its discoverers. Guardian refused to allow the people to become hostages in a dispute among gods. Ascendant heard the reservations as yet another refusal to acknowledge what he had accomplished.

The Kel'vari did not remain a collection of individuals gathered around their creator. As generations passed, they established families, settlements and institutions of their own. Their extraordinary capacity to recognise relationships between magical fields gave them unusual opportunities, but it did not supply food, shelter, governance or the labour upon which any lasting society depended. Farmers, builders, artisans, healers and civic officials were as necessary to their survival as the scholars whose discoveries attracted the attention of other peoples.

Their settlements developed into cities whose institutions reflected both their gifts and their disagreements. Some centres grew around archives and teaching halls; others became important through craft, cultivation, protective research or connections to the wider Passage network. Their ancestral language, **Oth'vaeri**, carried a distinct melodic cadence, with apostrophes marking meaningful sound boundaries in formal names. Shared language and ancestry did not prevent the cities from developing different customs, priorities and political traditions.

The Kel'vari's growing expertise brought them into close contact with the six older races. Visiting scholars exchanged methods with the great magical orders, while merchants and craftspeople carried practical discoveries between settlements. Kel'vari research contributed to improvements in healing, construction, defensive wards and Passage engineering. In return, their institutions drew upon the accumulated experience of civilizations that had existed before their own. They were unusually gifted at understanding complex relationships, but their achievements still depended upon training, cooperation and access to knowledge.

The cities learned that independence and shared responsibility could not always be separated. A local council might govern land, resources and disputes within its own territory, yet a Passage connection joining several cities required common standards and continuing cooperation. Research undertaken in one centre could affect the safety of distant communities. Disagreements over who should approve experiments, maintain shared works or control access to discoveries encouraged the development of broader institutions.

Over time, these arrangements became a **federation of largely autonomous Kel'vari cities**. Civic councils retained authority over local affairs. Scholarly assemblies coordinated research and the exchange of knowledge, while a federal assembly considered shared infrastructure and undertakings whose consequences extended beyond any one city. The federation did not remove rivalry or political ambition, but it provided a means of pursuing achievements that no individual centre could sustain alone.

For Ascendant, the civilization's growth was a source of pride. The people he had created had surpassed the limits he believed the older gods had accepted. Yet their independence meant their achievements belonged to them. Their scholars challenged inherited assumptions, their councils made decisions without seeking divine approval for every undertaking, and their ambitions did not always match those of their creator.

Among the questions that increasingly occupied Kel'vari scholars was the relationship between the six magical fields. Their studies suggested that the disciplines were distinct expressions of a deeper unity, though the principles connecting them remained difficult to reproduce. As their methods improved, the desire to understand that unity became associated with an even greater ambition: establishing a lasting means of communicating with Ascendant.

Such communication could not be achieved merely by possessing unusual aptitude. The gods' appearances were rare, and even the greatest mortal workings remained constrained by the laws of the Physical Realm. Kel'vari scholars therefore approached the undertaking through research, testing and debate. What began as a question of magical theory gradually became a subject of federation-wide interest, bringing together institutions whose cooperation would be essential to any attempt on such a scale.

As generations passed, Kel'vari researchers explored the apparent unity beneath the six magical fields. In one exceptionally difficult experiment they brought all six into a fleeting configuration and observed an unexpected result they could study but not fully explain. Unknown to them, that configuration had briefly **resonated with the Veil**, giving Nhal its first minute opportunity to influence a magical working in the Physical Realm.

Encouraged by their results, the researchers continued combining the six fields in increasingly complex experiments. Whenever they reproduced the particular alignment, the Veil resonated again. Nhal could not command minds, control all magic or speak openly as a god, but through those fleeting opportunities it could favour possible outcomes, help unstable patterns settle and encourage discoveries that seemed to justify further investigation.

The research eventually supported a publicly authorised undertaking: a permanent connection with Ascendant. The Kel'vari federation and its scholarly authorities approved the legitimate objective after debate over cost, safety and the limits of existing knowledge. The undertaking became known as **the First Anchor**.

Six principal scholars guided the project. **Vaer'uun** led resonance and communication research; **Thes'var** designed its complex structures; **Su'vareth** coordinated restricted records and external relationships; **Oma'vira** studied consciousness and perception; **Khor'vun** directed protective workings and containment; and **Oth'varen** independently verified the stability of the combined six fields. They were celebrated colleagues, and Oth'varen counted the other five among his closest friends.

Over years of repeated resonance, five of the scholars came to suspect an intelligence behind their extraordinary results and willingly pursued it. Nhal cultivated their ambitions and gradually distorted their judgement, subtly guiding the project towards an objective known only to itself. Vaer'uun interpreted discoveries as confirmation of a greater purpose. Thes'var incorporated concealed structural alterations. Su'vareth restricted records and concealed discrepancies. Oma'vira encouraged confidence in what she regarded as a revelation. Khor'vun adjusted safeguards to accommodate the altered workings. Their legitimate reputations and divided institutional oversight allowed the deception to continue.

Oth'varen noticed discrepancies between approved designs and operational configurations. Other Kel'vari investigators also uncovered missing records, inconsistent results and evidence of unauthorised alterations. They preserved copies and demanded further scrutiny, though they could neither identify Nhal nor demonstrate the ultimate danger. Oth'varen increasingly feared that his friends were following an influence they did not understand.

As the First Anchor neared completion, the five presented their final findings to the scholarly assemblies and federal authorities. Years of successful experiments, independently celebrated discoveries and the extraordinary reputation of the project's leading researchers gave the assembly confidence in their work.

Concerns had been raised by Oth'varen and a small number of investigators, but the evidence remained incomplete. Discrepancies in the research records and unexplained alterations had been attributed to the complexity of the undertaking, while the five carefully concealed the true extent of their modifications.

The final authorisation was granted. Across the Kel'vari federation, anticipation grew for what promised to be the greatest achievement of their civilization: a lasting connection with Ascendant.

Oth'varen continued his examination. In the activation chamber, with the six principal scholars present, he recognised that the actual configuration was directed towards an unknown destination rather than the promised stable channel to Ascendant. He confronted his five colleagues and pleaded with them to stop. He appealed to their shared work, their friendship and the lives depending on their judgement. They refused. They believed the hidden intelligence had led them to a discovery greater than the original project, and their years of exposure had made them increasingly able to justify what they concealed.

The First Anchor activated.

For a fleeting moment, the six magical fields converged in a configuration of extraordinary precision. The immense structure responded as its creators had anticipated, drawing together energies that no mortal working had ever sustained on such a scale.

Then something changed.

The convergence shifted beyond the boundaries of its intended design. The six fields, brought together through the concealed alterations, began to resonate with something the scholars had never understood. The Anchor reached beyond the Physical Realm.

And **Aethyr's Veil ruptured.**

Through the breach surged a primordial force unlike anything the Kel'vari had encountered. The carefully balanced magical fields convulsed as the power of unmaking entered the working, overwhelming its protective structures and spreading through the vast network of energies sustaining the Anchor.

Within the central chamber, the six scholars stood at the heart of the rupture.

Beyond the Veil, Nhal recognised the opportunity it had cultivated through years of subtle influence. The five who had willingly pursued its guidance were exposed to the immense primordial energies pouring through the breach. Nhal seized upon them, deliberately directing those energies into their living forms.

Vaer'uun, Thes'var, Su'vareth, Oma'vira and Khor'vun were transformed. Their bodies and magical structures were altered beyond the limits of ordinary mortal existence. They gained extraordinary longevity, immense magical capacity and resilience far beyond what any Kel'vari could naturally survive. Yet they had not become gods. Their transformation did not render them invulnerable or incapable of death.

Nhal's attention then turned to the sixth.

Oth'varen had resisted the hidden influence behind the Anchor's research. He had recognised the manipulation and desperately attempted to prevent the activation. Now Nhal sought to claim him as well.

The primordial energies entered his body, altering his physical form and magical structures just as they had transformed his colleagues. But where the five had willingly embraced the influence that guided them, Oth'varen resisted. The power threatened to overwhelm him. His perceptions expanded, his body changed, and within the transformation he felt the same terrible invitation that had drawn the others towards Nhal.

For a moment, he understood the temptation. The power offered possibilities beyond anything he had imagined, an understanding of magic that seemed to transcend the limitations of mortal existence. But beneath that promise, he recognised the same influence that had manipulated their research.

He refused it.

Drawing upon everything he understood about the stability of magical structures, Oth'varen fought to preserve the independence of his own consciousness. He could not prevent the primordial energies from transforming him, but he resisted their attempt to claim his will. The struggle nearly destroyed him. Yet his resistance held.

Like the five, Oth'varen emerged fundamentally changed. His mortal body possessed extraordinary longevity, resilience and magical capacity. But Nhal had failed to secure his allegiance. The corruption remained within him, a persistent influence that would test his resolve throughout his long existence.

**All six had been transformed. Only five had surrendered to Nhal.**

The rupture continued to widen.

The energies flowing through the First Anchor overwhelmed its protective structures. The six magical fields fell out of balance, and the containment wards surrounding the central chamber failed in succession.

**A tremendous discharge of primordial energy erupted from the heart of the First Anchor.**

The shockwave shattered the chamber and devastated the surrounding research complex. Towers fell, laboratories collapsed and the conduits linking the complex to its supporting structures were torn apart. Yet at the centre of the devastation, the First Anchor remained. Its outer mechanisms were ruined, but its core continued to resonate with the breach.

All six transformed scholars were caught in the discharge. Their extraordinary resilience allowed them to survive forces that would have killed ordinary mortals, but they were injured and scattered. Some disappeared into the collapsing galleries; others were thrown into the surrounding devastation. Oth'varen lost sight of his former colleagues as the chamber fell around him. Their precise destinations remained unknown.

When the discharge subsided, the research complex was gone. The six were separated by ruined structures, unstable passages and a landscape beginning to break apart.

None knew which of the others had survived.

Yet the First Anchor continued to operate.

The breach remained open, and the primordial energies flowing through it spread beyond the ruined complex.

**The Kel'vari homeland was the first to suffer.**

Cities were struck by forces their scholars could neither predict nor contain. Buildings collapsed as the magical structures sustaining them failed. Protective wards twisted into dangerous configurations, and great Passage connections broke apart or became unstable. Across the homeland, mountains fractured, valleys collapsed and immense stretches of land were torn apart.

The catastrophe spread beyond the Kel'vari's borders.

The interconnected magical infrastructure of the ancient world carried disturbances far beyond the original breach. Passage networks failed, protective structures collapsed, and systems upon which entire cities depended ceased to function.

Yet the destruction was not confined to magic.

Immense forces tore through the Physical Realm, reshaping the land itself. Great fractures opened across the ancient world. Regions that had once been joined were separated by widening seas, while mountains rose or collapsed and entire coastlines were transformed.

**The continents of the ancient world were breaking apart.**

Civilizations that had flourished through centuries of cooperation found themselves divided by distances that had not previously existed. Ancient roads ended at shattered coastlines. Cities lost access to their surrounding settlements, agricultural regions and distant trading partners.

The devastation was uneven.

Some ancestral cities were destroyed outright. Others survived the immediate catastrophe but lost the infrastructure, resources or knowledge upon which their continued existence depended. Communities that had once relied upon the Passage network for food, trade, protection and scholarship were suddenly isolated.

Across Theia, survivors struggled to understand what had happened. The Kel'vari, whose extraordinary discoveries had once been celebrated throughout the ancient world, now found their homeland devastated and their greatest achievement associated with a catastrophe of unimaginable scale.

But the Kel'vari had not been extinguished.

Some survived among the ruins of their homeland, sheltered by ancient wards, fortified structures or the few places that had escaped the worst destruction. Others had been travelling, studying or living among the six older races when the catastrophe struck.

Those beyond the homeland faced growing suspicion.

As reports of the disaster spread, rulers and communities searched for explanations and someone to hold responsible. Some demanded access to surviving Kel'vari research, believing it might explain the destruction or provide the means to repair it. Others regarded the Kel'vari themselves as dangerous and sought to drive them from their lands.

Among the survivors were scholars who had questioned the First Anchor's research before its activation. They carried records of unexplained experimental results, concealed modifications and discrepancies between the approved designs and the workings that had ultimately been constructed.

The catastrophe had confirmed the danger they feared, but it had not revealed the full nature of the intelligence behind the manipulation. They did not know its name or primordial origin. Nor did they understand the full extent of what had been unleashed.

What they knew was that the First Anchor had been deliberately altered, that their warnings had gone unheeded, and that the resulting catastrophe had devastated the world.

They began seeking allies among the scholars, witnesses and protectors of the six older races, whose own civilizations were struggling with the consequences of the disaster. Their evidence became increasingly important as the gods and mortal peoples attempted to understand the catastrophe and determine who bore responsibility.

The devastation left the gods confronting a catastrophe unlike anything they had previously witnessed. Across Theia, their mortal creations suffered amid ruined cities, fractured lands and the collapse of magical structures that had sustained civilizations for generations.

As the scale of the destruction became clear, attention turned towards the Kel'vari and the research that had brought about the disaster.

Keeper recognised elements of the forbidden stability principles Ascendant had used in creating the Kel'vari. The discovery deepened his suspicions, and he demanded access to the research behind the First Anchor, determined to understand how such a catastrophic working had been constructed.

Ascendant resisted.

He had watched his greatest creation suffer devastation on an unimaginable scale. His people were being blamed for a disaster most had played no part in causing, and he feared that surrendering their knowledge would expose the survivors to further persecution.

To Ascendant, Keeper's demands seemed less an attempt to uncover the truth than another effort to condemn the achievement he had never fully accepted.

The other gods were divided over how to respond.

Judge demanded accountability for those responsible, but insisted that judgement must rest upon evidence. Guardian opposed the persecution of innocent Kel'vari and sought to protect the displaced. Warrior condemned the destruction and demanded that those responsible answer for it, while refusing to treat an entire people as guilty.

Seeker argued that the First Anchor's workings must be understood before their knowledge was suppressed or destroyed. Shaper, witnessing the collapse of the ancient world's infrastructure, feared that indiscriminately dismantling advanced magical structures would condemn even more mortals to suffering.

None of these positions was without merit. Yet each god increasingly regarded the others' priorities as a threat to the people and principles they had sworn to protect.

Their disagreements deepened as the surviving Kel'vari research was examined.

Records were incomplete. Experimental results contradicted established theory, and evidence emerged that the First Anchor's construction had differed from its approved design.

Among those who possessed such evidence was Oth'varen.

Having survived the destruction of the Anchor complex, he sought out the Kel'vari investigators who had questioned the project before its activation. Together, they gathered surviving records, reconstructed the concealed alterations and attempted to establish how their colleagues had been led towards the disastrous working.

Oth'varen knew more than any of them about the final moments of the activation. He had witnessed the transformation of his five colleagues and experienced the same primordial influence attempting to claim him.

Yet even he could not explain its true nature.

He knew that an intelligence had manipulated their research and that the five had willingly pursued it. He did not know its name, its origin or the full extent of its purpose.

The rupture of the Veil had changed more than the physical world. Before the First Anchor's activation, Nhal could influence only particular magical experiments through fleeting resonances. With the breach open, its influence reached those who had willingly followed it with greater clarity.

The five transformed scholars experienced this most strongly. What had once appeared as unexpected insight now took the form of thoughts not wholly their own, impressions that arrived with sudden meaning and, at times, whispers that seemed to speak from beyond their minds. **Nhal had found a means to communicate with its chosen followers.**

The guidance was neither constant nor absolute control. Through the corruption within them, Nhal offered direction, fragments of knowledge and interpretations that reinforced convictions already distorted by years of influence. The five increasingly understood even the destruction of their homeland through the explanations it provided, treating the catastrophe as an incomplete achievement rather than a betrayal.

Other willing followers received weaker impressions, particularly near corrupted structures or during forbidden workings. Their experiences varied, but the communications helped them recover research, conceal evidence and exploit the gods' growing distrust. Nhal did not direct every action. Its followers remained capable of choices, even as their judgement and ambitions grew more closely aligned with its purpose.

Oth'varen also sensed the intelligence through the corruption that had reached him. Sometimes its presence came as a faint whisper or an invitation to stop resisting, particularly when he drew deeply upon his altered power. He never accepted it as an authority. The five, by contrast, increasingly treated the guiding presence as inseparable from their own purpose.

As the investigation continued, those responsible for the First Anchor's secret alterations began working to prevent the truth from emerging.

The surviving followers of the hidden intelligence exploited the growing distrust among the gods. They circulated selected records alongside forged evidence, mixing genuine accounts of Ascendant's concealed methods with false claims that he had deliberately designed the Kel'vari as instruments of destruction.

Other documents suggested that Keeper had known more about the dangers of the research than he had admitted, while conflicting testimony cast doubt upon the intentions of those demanding access to the surviving magical knowledge.

The accusations found willing audiences.

Ascendant, already distrustful of Keeper's motives, withheld further details of his creation of the Kel'vari. Keeper restricted access to dangerous stability principles, fearing that their disclosure might enable another catastrophic working.

Seeker challenged those restrictions, arguing that concealed evidence could neither be tested nor trusted. Judge's attempts to establish responsibility were frustrated by contradictory accounts, while Guardian and Warrior increasingly found themselves protecting communities threatened by reprisals and the seizure of their knowledge.

Each act of concealment strengthened suspicion. Each demand for disclosure seemed to threaten another god's responsibilities.

What had begun as a disagreement over the cause of the catastrophe became a struggle over who could be trusted with the knowledge and power necessary to prevent another.

Mortal rulers, academies and magical orders were drawn into the conflict. Some supported the gods whose principles they shared. Others sought protection for their people, access to surviving infrastructure or control of knowledge that might determine whether their civilizations recovered.

The first attempts to seize forbidden research provoked resistance. Resistance brought harsher measures, and the resulting confrontations deepened the divisions among gods and mortals alike.

**The Divine Schism had become the Divine War.**

Amid the spreading conflict, Oth'varen and the surviving Kel'vari investigators recognised that their evidence could no longer be entrusted to any single divine authority, kingdom or magical institution.

They had already witnessed how respected scholars could be manipulated and how dangerous knowledge could be concealed beneath apparently legitimate research. Now they saw the same pattern of secrecy, accusation and mistrust spreading through the wider world.

They began seeking trusted allies among scholars, witnesses and protectors of the six older races.

Some had already questioned the accounts used to justify the war. Others had protected Kel'vari refugees, preserved records of the catastrophe or resisted attempts to seize dangerous magical knowledge.

Together, they established **the Guardians**.

From their founding, the Guardians were a multiracial organisation, united not by ancestry or allegiance to a particular god, but by a shared purpose: to preserve evidence of the manipulation behind the First Anchor, investigate the unknown intelligence responsible and prevent another catastrophe.

They established hidden repositories and independent cells throughout the surviving civilizations, including within the devastated Kel'vari homeland.

Their members sought to safeguard knowledge without allowing it to be exploited, protect surviving witnesses and preserve the truth against those who would destroy or distort it.

Oth'varen became one of their principal founders and their most important living witness to the First Anchor's activation.

But the Guardians were not his personal order. Their strength came from the different peoples and disciplines brought together by a common mission.

As the Divine War spread, their work became increasingly dangerous.

The followers of the hidden intelligence continued manipulating the conflict, while the gods' own decisions brought further destruction to a world already struggling to survive.

And despite everything Oth'varen had witnessed, the true nature of the power behind the First Anchor remained unknown.

The Divine War brought further destruction to a world already struggling to recover from the Second Sundering.

Surviving Passage routes became strategic objectives, fought over by forces seeking to move armies and supplies through the fractured continents. Fortified cities were besieged for their remaining protective structures, while magical institutions were attacked for the knowledge and artifacts they possessed.

As the war continued, the loss of skilled practitioners and irreplaceable records made the surviving infrastructure increasingly difficult to maintain. Entire communities were forced to abandon settlements that had survived the original catastrophe, while others became isolated as the last dependable routes between them were destroyed.

The Guardians attempted to preserve what knowledge and evidence they could, but their hidden repositories and independent cells could not prevent the wider destruction. Meanwhile, followers of the unknown intelligence continued exploiting the conflict, recovering fragments of the First Anchor's research and pursuing experiments whose true purpose remained concealed.

Amid the devastation, Keeper began noticing disturbances that could not be explained by warfare or the failure of damaged magical structures.

Some protective workings collapsed despite remaining structurally sound. Others behaved unpredictably only when particular magical configurations were attempted. Accounts from distant regions described similar anomalies, even where no direct connection between the affected structures could be established.

Keeper began comparing these incidents with surviving records of the First Anchor's research.

He recognised a troubling similarity. Certain configurations appeared to produce results that favoured further instability or encouraged repeated experimentation, much as the unexplained discoveries preceding the First Anchor's activation had done.

At first, he considered whether the anomalies reflected an undiscovered interaction between the six magical fields. Yet the pattern persisted across different workings and circumstances, and some disturbances appeared to respond to the actions of those investigating them.

The evidence increasingly suggested something beyond ordinary magical instability.

Keeper extended his investigation towards the devastated Kel'vari homeland, where the most severe disturbances continued to originate.

There, studying the corruption surrounding the First Anchor, he recognised that the rupture had created more than a passage for destructive energies.

**Something beyond the Physical Realm was exerting influence through it.**

Keeper could not identify the intelligence or reconstruct the primordial history of Aethyr and Nhal. But he recognised deliberate behaviour in the disturbances, and understood that the breach offered an opportunity for that influence to grow.

The possibility that it might eventually establish a lasting presence within the Physical Realm became his greatest concern.

As Keeper investigated the damage surrounding the First Anchor, a terrible possibility emerged. The breach could not simply be abandoned, nor could the Anchor be safely destroyed. It had become entangled with the rupture in the Veil, and removing it risked tearing away what little still constrained the intrusion. The damaged region would have to be isolated from the rest of the Physical Realm.

Keeper began developing a means of containment. An immense magical boundary, later called **the Shroud**, might preserve what remained beyond the epicentre. But no ordinary spell, nor even the power of a single god, could sustain it. The containment would require a contribution from **all Seven**, their combined divine essences forming the foundation of a barrier capable of resisting the pressure from beyond creation.

As Keeper refined the workings, the true cost became clear. The Seven would not merely expend their power to establish the Shroud. Their essences would have to remain bound to it, continuously sustaining the containment. Once the ritual took hold, none could simply withdraw and resume their former lives.

**Keeper knew the price with certainty.**

Keeper also understood that the others might never willingly accept such a fate. They were already divided by suspicion, grief and war. Some would question the evidence, others would search for alternatives, and still others might refuse to sacrifice their freedom without knowing whether the threat could be contained by less terrible means. Yet every delay risked allowing the breach to grow beyond their ability to restrain it.

Keeper made a deliberate choice. Rather than reveal the permanent nature of the Binding, Keeper would present the containment as a great but temporary expenditure of divine power. Each god would surrender a small portion of their essence to establish the Shroud, accepting exhaustion, weakness or a long recovery as the price of protecting creation. **Keeper knew this was not the truth.** The contributions would not be temporary, and the essences surrendered would become the Shroud's enduring foundation.

When Keeper finally brought the proposal before the other six gods, the warning was met with resistance. Judge demanded proof of the intelligence behind the catastrophe and questioned the authority to impose such a sacrifice. Seeker wanted to reproduce the observations and understand the workings before committing divine power. Shaper feared the proposed containment would condemn what remained of the Kel'vari homeland. Warrior refused to abandon the innocent lives still caught within the devastation. Ascendant, already suspicious of Keeper's judgment and secrecy, resisted the suggestion that his people's greatest achievement had opened a danger beyond their understanding.

Guardian, however, came to accept Keeper's evidence and the urgency of the threat. Guardian's support did not erase the others' doubts, but it gave the proposal a voice they trusted. The debate continued as the war consumed more of the ancient world. Each god weighed the danger against their own principles, the fate of their creations and the consequences of doing nothing.

Eventually, the Seven reached an agreement. Their trust was wounded, and their reasons for accepting the plan were not identical, but they recognized that the breach threatened everything they had made. **They consented to contribute what they believed was a limited portion of their power**, trusting Keeper's assurance that their sacrifice would end once the Shroud had been established.

The final containment would come to be remembered as **The Binding**.

The Seven joined their power in one last act of cooperation. The boundary rose around the devastated homeland. The breach was contained. The spread of Nhal's influence was checked.

Then they tried to withdraw.

They could not.

The structure that held the Shroud was not merely a thing the Seven had built. Their own continuing essences had become its living foundation. They remained conscious and distinct, but bound to a task that would outlast their freedom. Keeper could no longer hide the truth: this had been the intended outcome all along.

**The Binding saved creation through a sacrifice the other gods had not knowingly chosen.**

For Warrior, the deception violated the honour of a freely given promise. For Judge, it violated the conditions under which agreement had been obtained. Seeker would search the structure for a means of release; Shaper would confront the terrible fact that the makers themselves had become the mechanism of their creation. Guardian could recognize the need to protect mortal lives and still be appalled that their right to choose had been taken. Ascendant, whose own earlier concealment had broken Keeper's trust, now faced a betrayal committed on a far greater scale.

The Seven remained bound, and the knowledge that their consent had been obtained through concealment deepened the divisions The Binding had been meant to overcome.

### The Immediate Aftermath of The Binding

The Binding had contained the breach, but the world beyond the Shroud remained devastated.

The Second Sundering had fractured the continents, and the Divine War had brought further destruction to civilizations already struggling to survive. The gods who had once guided their mortal creations were now bound within the great magical barrier surrounding the ruined Kel'vari homeland.

Across Theia, survivors faced a world profoundly changed. Cities that had endured the catastrophe were separated from their former territories. The great Passage network that had connected distant peoples was largely broken, and much of the knowledge needed to restore it had been lost.

Yet not every connection had failed.

A small number of ancient Passage gates remained operational, though at greatly reduced capacity. Where vast numbers of travellers and supplies had once moved between cities, only individuals, small groups or limited consignments could now pass.

These surviving routes provided rare connections between otherwise isolated regions, but their limited capacity made them incapable of restoring the trade and movement upon which the ancient world had depended.

#### Within the Shroud

The Kel'vari homeland lay enclosed within the barrier, its ancient cities shattered and its landscape scarred by the forces unleashed through the First Anchor.

The Shroud restrained the spread of corruption beyond its boundaries, but it did not cleanse the land within.

Around the First Anchor, primordial influence continued to distort magical structures and the surrounding environment. Regions of the homeland remained dangerous or inaccessible, and many surviving settlements were separated by corrupted territory and ruined infrastructure.

The Kel'vari who had survived the catastrophe gathered wherever protection remained possible.

Some found shelter within fortified research complexes, ancient defensive structures or settlements whose protective wards had survived. Others occupied damaged cities or established small refuges around sources of water, cultivable land and surviving magical infrastructure.

Their extraordinary knowledge offered advantages, but it could not replace destroyed food supplies, lost craftspeople or the countless ordinary activities required to sustain a community.

The Guardians maintained cells within the devastated homeland, protecting survivors, recovering evidence and attempting to prevent the dangerous research that had caused the catastrophe from being repeated.

Their work was increasingly difficult. The Shroud had restricted access to the wider world, while corrupted territories and unstable passages made movement between surviving communities hazardous.

Not all Kel'vari within the Shroud were aligned with the Unmade. Most were ordinary survivors, struggling to preserve their families and what remained of their civilization.

#### Beyond the Shroud

Outside the containment, the six older races faced the consequences of the Second Sundering and Divine War without the direct guidance of their creators.

The human communities of the inland plains began restoring their settlements and agricultural lands. The dwarves retained their ancient mountain and subterranean capital, though its surviving infrastructure required extensive repair.

Across the grasslands, the orc Great Clans gathered displaced families and defended the territories that remained habitable. Halfling communities struggled with damaged passages and failing systems within their ancestral underground valleys.

The elves and trolls likewise confronted the destruction of their ancient world. Their civilizations remained culturally connected, though religious and political disagreements were becoming increasingly difficult to reconcile.

The ancestral troll highland capital had survived. Elsewhere, settlements and institutions faced different degrees of destruction, isolation and displacement.

Among the populations beyond the Shroud were Kel'vari who had been travelling, studying or living among the older races when the catastrophe occurred, together with those who had escaped their homeland before The Binding.

Their circumstances varied.

Some found protection among former colleagues and trusted communities. Others faced suspicion or persecution from those who associated their people with the destruction of the ancient world.

The Guardians' cells beyond the Shroud sought to protect vulnerable Kel'vari, preserve surviving evidence and maintain contact with their counterparts inside the containment wherever possible.

The followers of the hidden intelligence had also been scattered.

Some remained within the Shroud, while others had been operating among distant institutions when the catastrophe occurred. Those outside the barrier retained fragments of research, concealed loyalties and whatever contacts they had established before the war.

But the surviving Unmade faced a crisis that none of their preparations had anticipated.

#### The Silence of Nhal

The Binding did more than restrain the corruption spreading from the First Anchor. By enclosing the breach within the Shroud, the Seven disrupted the channels through which the intelligence beyond the Veil had communicated with its followers.

For the five transformed scholars, the change was immediate. The whispers and guiding impressions that had accompanied them through the Divine War ceased. No further instructions came. The presence they had learned to trust was suddenly silent.

They attempted to recover the connection through surviving Anchor workings and familiar configurations. Nothing answered. Their transformation, extraordinary power and distorted convictions remained, but the voice that had reinforced them was gone.

**The voice was gone, but its influence had become part of who they were.**

Across the Unmade, within and beyond the Shroud, dependable communication ceased. Corruption still pressed upon the land around the First Anchor, but pressure was not instruction. Their laboratories and records had already been devastated by the Sundering and war, leaving them with fragments of knowledge and remembered guidance whose source no longer answered.

Oth'varen felt the silence too. For him it brought relief rather than abandonment, although the corruption within him remained and he knew that the intelligence had not been destroyed.

For some followers the silence brought uncertainty; others believed the gods had separated them from their patron. None could know how long it would last.

### The First Years of Silence

The five transformed scholars, however, had survived.

Scattered by the destruction of the First Anchor complex, they had endured the Divine War and the devastation of their homeland. In the years following The Binding, they began gathering the remaining followers of the intelligence they had served.

Their extraordinary longevity, magical power and knowledge of the original research placed them beyond the authority of ordinary Unmade practitioners.

Yet the corruption that had transformed them had also become deeply rooted in their thoughts and ambitions.

They did not interpret the destruction of their civilization as a reason to abandon their pursuit. Instead, they saw the catastrophe as evidence that their greatest undertaking had been incomplete.

The loss of Nhal's guidance made their continued work more difficult, but it did not diminish their conviction.

From the surviving followers and fragments of their former research, they established **the Corrupted Conclave**.

Vaer'uun assumed the role of **the First Voice**, interpreting the remembered intentions of the intelligence they served.

Thes'var became **the Architect**, determined to recover the lost workings of the First Anchor.

Su'vareth, **the Veiled Hand**, began restoring covert connections with followers beyond the Shroud.

Oma'vira, **the Harbinger**, preserved devotion and recruited new adherents.

Khor'vun, **the Warden**, secured the remaining strongholds and protected the approaches to the First Anchor.

Together, the five assumed leadership of the Unmade.

Their authority did not come from a new instruction from Nhal. It arose from their surviving knowledge, extraordinary abilities and shared determination to continue the work they had begun.

#### The First Reunion

Several years after The Binding, Oth'varen followed traces of familiar but altered magical workmanship to a ruined Kel'vari research sanctuary. Its protective patterns had been distorted, yet beneath the new arrangements he recognised the precision of the scholar who had once worked beside him. Thes'var's designs remained unmistakable.

Oth'varen had not known which of his five former colleagues had survived the discharge that destroyed the First Anchor complex. He had searched for evidence through the war and the first years of containment, fearing what their survival might mean. Within the sanctuary he found all five alive, their altered forms still bearing the consequences of the breach. They had gathered surviving followers and established authority over the Unmade. Their long exposure to Nhal's influence had not faded with the loss of its voice.

The reunion confronted him with the distance between the people he remembered and those who stood before him. Vaer'uun, now the First Voice, regarded their transformation as evidence that they had reached beyond the gods' limitations. Thes'var, the Architect, treated the catastrophe as proof that the Anchor's construction had been insufficient, not that its purpose was wrong. Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand, justified the concealment of their research as protection from those who would have suppressed it. Oma'vira, the Harbinger, spoke of their transformation as a revelation, while Khor'vun, the Warden, saw the defence of the five and their followers as the duty that remained to him.

Oth'varen had once trusted each of them for different reasons. He had argued with Thes'var over the stability of their designs, relied upon Khor'vun's safeguards and believed Vaer'uun capable of recognising the limits of a discovery. Their answers revealed that those very strengths had been bent towards Nhal's purpose. The destruction of their homeland had not restored their judgement. They had instead learned to interpret it as an incomplete achievement, a sacrifice justified by what they still believed could be attained.

When they invited him to rejoin them, the corruption that had entered Oth'varen during the breach responded to their combined presence. He felt the attraction of the power they had embraced, not as a fresh command from Nhal, whose reliable communication had ceased, but as a resonance among beings transformed by the same primordial force. He drew upon his understanding of magical stability to resist it. The effort caused him pain, and he recognised how vulnerable he remained even after years of refusing the influence.

His refusal ended the encounter. The sanctuary's protective workings turned against him, and Khor'vun barred his departure. Oth'varen disrupted the wards while Thes'var attempted to restore them, breaking their containment long enough to escape into the ruins. He did not defeat the five, nor could he bring himself to destroy them. But he had seen enough to understand that the danger they represented had survived the catastrophe and The Binding.

He returned to the Guardians knowing that their task extended beyond preserving the evidence of the First Anchor. The five still sought to continue their work, and their corrupted convictions made another disaster possible. For Oth'varen, that knowledge carried the grief of an old friendship as well as the responsibility of preventing its consequences.

### The First Generations — Survival Becomes Inheritance

Children born after The Binding inherited a world of broken passages, separated lands and absent gods. For their parents these were fresh wounds; for their descendants they became the conditions of everyday life.

#### Within the Shroud

In the generations following The Binding, the surviving Kel'vari struggled to rebuild their shattered civilization. The corruption spreading across their homeland, together with the destruction of ancient roads and Passage networks, left many communities isolated from one another.

Without the means to restore their former federation, survivors established independent settlements around the few locations where ancient infrastructure, protective wards and essential resources remained intact.

Over time, these refuges developed into **distinct Kel'vari enclaves**, each shaped by the surviving institutions and resources upon which its people depended.

Among the most significant was **Veyr'athun**, a fortified enclave established around an ancient centre of defensive research. Its surviving protective workings provided shelter against the corruption surrounding it.

**Oth'irun** emerged around one of the civilization's great archives, where scholars and custodians dedicated themselves to preserving the knowledge and records that had survived the catastrophe.

In the sheltered lands of **Oru'sava**, ancient cultivation and restorative workings allowed its inhabitants to sustain agriculture and provide for communities whose ancestral farmlands had been lost.

**Thuma'rei**, once a major centre of Passage research and transportation, became an important refuge through its surviving gates, maintaining limited connections between otherwise isolated regions.

At **Soru'vaeth**, a former centre of material research and reconstruction, survivors began recovering resources, artifacts and knowledge from the ruins of their ancestral civilization.

These five enclaves were among the most significant surviving Kel'vari settlements, but they were not the only ones. Smaller refuges remained scattered throughout the Shroud, some maintaining limited contact with their neighbours while others became increasingly isolated.

Although the enclaves shared a common ancestry and the memory of their lost civilization, **they remained independent communities rather than reforming a single Kel'vari kingdom**.

As generations passed, their different circumstances shaped distinct traditions and priorities. Archive custodians feared repeating forbidden research, while ward-keepers needed to investigate failing defences. Passage custodians could move only limited supplies and travellers. Reclamation parties sometimes returned with indispensable tools and sometimes disturbed dangers that had lain untouched. Families worked, learned and aged within these refuges, preserving fragments of a civilization no enclave could reproduce in full.

Not every corrupted inhabitant served the Unmade. Some transformed Kel'vari and ancient constructs continued duties they barely understood, defending deserted gates or tending workings that had become dangerous. Others willingly followed the Unmade or were exploited by them. Guardian cells protected particular communities and repositories, but securing one refuge or route could expose another.

As the enclaves struggled to preserve their independence, the Unmade strengthened their remaining strongholds. **Khor'vun, the Warden**, secured routes towards the First Anchor, while **Thes'var, the Architect**, recovered research from ruined laboratories. The teachings of **Oma'vira, the Harbinger**, reached new generations of followers; **Vaer'uun, the First Voice**, enforced his interpretation of their silent patron's purpose. Through **Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand**, contacts beyond the Shroud became more organised, though none of these efforts restored Nhal's voice.

Oth'varen continued to aid the Guardians and protect evidence of the catastrophe. His reunion with the five had shown him that their ambitions survived. He remained a living witness to their transformation, though he could not explain the primordial intelligence's full nature, and the corruption within his own altered body still demanded resistance.

#### Beyond the Shroud

Human communities restored farms and institutions across the inland plains. The dwarves repaired their surviving mountain and subterranean capital, relying upon human food and secure routes while offering engineering, fortification and craft in return. Repeated cooperation created the foundations of a lasting treaty.

Across the grasslands, the orc Great Clans defended their people and sheltered halflings displaced from their underground valleys. The partnership that followed was neither conquest nor subordination: halfling engineering, supply systems and guild organisation complemented the protection and territory offered by the orcs.

Elven communities disputed the responsibilities attached to magical knowledge. Among trolls, the traditions honouring Keeper and Ascendant developed increasingly distinct religious authorities. These differences deepened through institutions, decisions and generations rather than dividing either people in a single moment.

Kel'vari refugees established families among the six older races. **Only the Kel'vari could have children with members of any of those six species**, which could not ordinarily reproduce across species boundaries. Their descendants were not thereby immortal or extraordinarily powerful. Some families preserved their ancestry; others concealed it or gradually lost the records. Guardian cells sought to protect vulnerable descendants and maintain limited contact with the Shroud. Unmade adherents beyond the barrier likewise preserved hidden networks and fragments of research, without the capacity to move armies through the surviving gates.

### The Age of Reconstruction — New Peoples and New Powers

The first generations had built refuges. Their descendants faced a different task: deciding what kind of world those refuges would become. A protected road could bind communities together, but it could also give one settlement power over another. A restored archive could teach a new generation, or reignite an argument over knowledge that had once brought catastrophe. The arrangements made to survive were becoming laws, borders and institutions.

Human settlements spread again across the inland plains. As harvests became more dependable, councils and local rulers coordinated roads, defence and the disputes of growing towns. A renewed monarchy gathered these communities around a postwar racial capital, while the dwarves rebuilt the ancient mountain metropolis that had endured the Divine War. Neither people could prosper alone: human grain and routes supported dwarven holds, while dwarven engineering secured bridges, fortifications and vital works. Their cooperation deepened into a royal treaty without extinguishing either people's independence.

On the grasslands, the orc Great Clans faced their own questions of shared authority. Protection of displaced kin had brought them into lasting partnership with the halflings, whose guilds organised supplies and whose engineers helped settlements survive. As the halflings established a surface capital and new trading centres, their interests extended beyond the immediate refuge that had first united them. Orc leaders and halfling institutions increasingly found that agreements over roads, protection and exchange could endure where temporary obligations could not.

Among the elves, rebuilding reopened arguments the catastrophe had never settled. Scholars who sought to recover the ancient world's knowledge faced those who believed its unrestrained use had contributed to disaster. At first these disputes concerned the control of archives, the restoration of wards and the authority to approve experiments. They gradually divided communities over the obligations of magical power itself. Their original capital had been lost, leaving survivors to disagree over which traditions should guide the future.

Those who became the **Aelari** built a woodland monarchy in the primeval forest, placing greater trust in Guardian's principles of protection, Life and restraint. Those who became the **Vaelari** gathered around a mountain-lake capital under arcane leadership, honouring Seeker and the pursuit of knowledge. Their choices arose from a shared history, but each increasingly understood the other's path as a danger to what remained of that inheritance. Separation became hostility, sustained by competing institutions and memories.

The trolls also carried an old disagreement into the reconstruction. Traditions honouring Keeper's stability and Ascendant's promise of change had long existed side by side. As religious authorities gained influence over sacred places, customs and political decisions, those differences became harder to reconcile. The **Korrath** retained the ancestral highland capital and its elders' councils, chiefly honouring Keeper. The people who became the **Vorrath** were exiled amid the conflict and established a sacred storm-coast capital under religious leadership, chiefly honouring Ascendant. The circumstances of that exile remained contested in the histories their descendants preserved.

The growing divisions drew the older partnerships into wider commitments. The human–dwarf treaty offered a foundation upon which the Aelari and Korrath could join in mutual protection. Together they formed **Covenant**, an alliance of four sovereign civilizations. Its hereditary High Ruler came from a separate branch of the human royal dynasty; its multicultural coastal alliance capital was distinct from the human kingdom's inland capital. The arrangement allowed common defence while preserving separate laws, traditions and rulers.

The orc–halfling partnership likewise broadened as the Vaelari and Vorrath sought allies whose interests and grievances differed from those of Covenant. Their union became **Dominion**, also comprising four sovereign civilizations. Authority over the alliance was vested in a High Ruler chosen through trials involving champions nominated by all four member peoples. Its multicultural highland capital stood apart from the orc racial capital. These political forms emerged through negotiations and competing needs, not because the older races had always been destined to divide into two camps.

Within the Shroud, Kel'vari enclaves followed a different course. Distance, corruption and fragile passages continued to prevent a reunited federation. A settlement that preserved cultivation could not simply share its abundance with every isolated refuge; an archive that survived might possess knowledge another enclave had lost. The Guardians protected particular communities and repositories, but the loss of couriers and the increasing difficulty of contact meant that knowledge travelled less reliably with every generation.

The five original Conclave founders endured while their followers aged and died. Their unnatural longevity allowed them to maintain ambitions across lifetimes, but their power could not restore the guidance lost at The Binding. Beyond the Shroud, Kel'vari descendants increasingly identified with their adopted societies. Much of the old world's scholarship survived only in fragments. Of the three or four great magical orders of the Age of Wonders, **one secretly endured**, while new academies, guild colleges and religious schools developed practices suited to a changed world.

### The Long Age of Silence — Memory Becomes Secrecy

The first Unmade to lose Nhal's voice had expected the silence to end. They remembered the whispers that had followed the First Anchor's rupture, the impressions that seemed to answer questions before they were spoken and the guidance that had given scattered followers a shared direction through the Divine War. Their successors knew those experiences through testimony. Later generations knew them through records, ritual and the insistence of the five transformed scholars who had lived through them.

The silence lasted longer than the lifetimes of ordinary followers. A generation passed, then another, without a reliable answer from beyond the Veil. What had begun as an interruption became a condition around which the Unmade organised their existence. In time, they called those centuries **the Age of Silence**. The name did not mean that the Shroud had become peaceful. The corruption surrounding the First Anchor remained dangerous, and the five founders still possessed formidable power. It meant that the voice which had once guided their undertaking no longer spoke.

The Conclave had to decide how an organisation built around obedience to an absent intelligence could continue. **Vaer'uun, the First Voice**, preserved the authority of the instructions he remembered and insisted that their purpose could be understood even without fresh guidance. **Oma'vira, the Harbinger**, sustained devotion among followers who had never heard Nhal themselves. She gathered accounts, shaped teachings around them and treated doubt as a test of conviction. The memories on which those teachings rested were real, but repetition and interpretation changed how later adherents understood them.

**Thes'var, the Architect**, pursued another answer to the silence. He recovered fragments of the Anchor's research, tested surviving structures and sought the configurations that had once allowed the hidden intelligence to reach them. Failure did not persuade him to abandon the work; he regarded each incomplete reconstruction as evidence of knowledge still missing. **Khor'vun, the Warden**, defended the strongholds, routes and materials on which such experiments depended. He protected the organisation against threats from the corrupted land as well as from surviving Kel'vari and Guardians who opposed it. **Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand**, cultivated connections beyond the Shroud, where distance and political change required patient recruitment rather than direct force.

The five did not always agree about what their absent patron intended. Some followers believed Nhal had withdrawn from them; others regarded the Shroud as an obstacle that must eventually be broken. The Conclave's founders could not appeal to a fresh command to settle every disagreement. Yet their shared corruption, extraordinary longevity and knowledge of the original research kept the Unmade from dissolving. Their followers changed, their interpretations multiplied, and their purpose endured.

The Conclave's long research programme also changed during these centuries. **Thes'var, the Architect**, first attempted to recreate the six-field alignments of the First Anchor. Most workings failed, but a few produced faint, repeatable resonances with the original breach even beyond the Shroud. These **Minor Resonators** disturbed local magical conditions without restoring Nhal's voice. Their value was proof that the damage in the Veil could still be influenced from a distance.

Successive generations improved the designs, preserved measurements and established concealed laboratories. **Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand**, obtained materials and protected distant contacts; **Khor'vun, the Warden**, secured sites; **Oma'vira, the Harbinger**, maintained devotion through repeated failure; and **Vaer'uun, the First Voice**, interpreted progress as fidelity to Nhal's remembered purpose. The work was the Conclave's own, pursued without new instructions from its patron.

Eventually the Architect developed **Regional Anchors**, larger and more stable workings that linked the resonances of multiple installations. Their combined effects extended beyond individual sites, redistributing the accessibility of existing primordial energy and placing a faint cumulative strain upon the Shroud. They created no new magic. Some installations remained active for generations; others failed, fell dormant or were abandoned. Disturbances appeared as isolated anomalies to outside observers. The network remained too weak to restore communication with Nhal, but its foundations had been laid.

Within the Shroud, the Kel'vari enclaves faced a very different passage of time. The survivors who had first sheltered behind functioning wards gave way to children who had never seen the federation's cities intact. A ward-keeper might teach a successor the sequence required to maintain a protective structure while lacking the lost instruments needed to explain its complete design. An archivist might preserve a damaged record for decades, only for its meaning to become disputed after the last reader trained in the old methods died. Families cultivated protected ground, repaired shelters and negotiated over supplies whose movement depended on unreliable routes.

These were living communities, not relics frozen at the moment of catastrophe. Their inhabitants formed attachments, argued over responsibilities and made decisions that shaped later generations. Some communities guarded knowledge jealously because a mistake could endanger everyone behind their walls. Others risked expeditions into corrupted territory to recover resources their neighbours could no longer provide. The five major enclaves retained distinct purposes, while smaller refuges sometimes disappeared or survived with little contact beyond their immediate surroundings.

The Guardians had been founded to preserve evidence and protect those endangered by the First Anchor's manipulation. In the earliest generations, secrecy had served that purpose. A hidden repository could keep forbidden research out of the hands of the Unmade; a concealed refuge could shelter Kel'vari who faced persecution; a courier who knew only part of a route could not betray the whole network if captured.

Over centuries, those same precautions carried a cost. Couriers died before passing on their knowledge. Routes became unusable. A cell might receive instructions to protect a sealed archive but no longer possess the testimony explaining what it contained. Other cells lost contact with their counterparts inside the Shroud and came to regard reports of surviving enclaves as unreliable traditions. The organisation did not choose ignorance in a single decision. **Its protective secrecy gradually became an obstacle to its own memory.**

A few custodians retained the truth. Oth'varen remained among them, carrying firsthand knowledge of the Anchor and the five scholars who had betrayed their people. His extraordinary lifespan did not make the Guardians a single, perfectly informed institution. His continued existence and identity were themselves closely guarded, and no one witness could personally sustain every hidden route, refuge and archive across the world. He watched an order founded to prevent concealed knowledge from causing another catastrophe struggle with the consequences of concealment.

Beyond the Shroud, the Veiled Hand's networks developed the opposite advantage. They could survive without preserving a complete account of their origins. A contact within an academy might pass forbidden research to a successor; a guild intermediary could arrange a shipment without knowing its destination; an officer might justify a concealed operation as service to the alliance they publicly supported. Some agents knowingly served the Unmade. Others were manipulated through ambitions and obligations that required no understanding of Nhal. The remaining low-capacity Passage gates permitted limited covert journeys, but not armies or renewed direct communication.

During the same centuries, Covenant and Dominion grew into powers whose political identities were shaped by the choices of their ancestors. The human–dwarf treaty and orc–halfling partnership had begun in the necessities of survival; the elven division and Vorrath exile carried unresolved grievances into the alliances that followed. Successive rulers inherited claims to territory, sacred places, trade routes and ancient artifacts. Their peoples increasingly interpreted the divine age through separate traditions, even as much of the evidence that might have reconciled those accounts disappeared.

By the end of the long silence, the world contained two increasingly hostile alliances, a hidden order whose members knew unequal portions of its own history, and an Unmade conspiracy whose founders had outlived generations of followers. None of these developments required Nhal to speak. They were the accumulated consequences of choices made while its voice was absent.

The Architect's next ambition was to coordinate the scattered Regional Anchors through more powerful central workings. Early attempts at **Major Anchors** demanded rare artifacts, elaborate protections and cooperation among distant cells. Some remained incomplete or dormant. The Conclave had not yet restored Nhal's voice, and no single installation could accomplish that goal alone.

### The Modern Age — War and the Shattered Delegation

By the modern age, Covenant and Dominion remained hostile alliances of four sovereign civilizations each. The seas separating their continents did not prevent conflict. Warships supplied landing bases, garrisons, resource camps and research outposts on enemy shores. No ordinary public peace or cross-alliance cooperation united them. The Guardians and Unmade operated covertly within both societies.

Covenant's hereditary High Ruler balanced the human monarchy, independent dwarf holds, Aelari royalty and Korrath elders. Dominion's trial-chosen High Ruler answered to orc Great Clans, halfling guilds, Vaelari arcane institutions and Vorrath religious authorities. **Queen Seralyth**, **First Archon Selrion** and **First Elder Dromak** were among the established leaders whose peoples carried these histories into modern government.

Generations of conflict had taken their toll upon Covenant and Dominion.

Along their contested frontiers, settlements had been abandoned, trade routes disrupted and resources diverted towards the demands of war. Maintaining fortified outposts upon distant enemy shores placed an increasing burden upon both alliances, while repeated campaigns brought victories that seldom endured.

For the rulers of both powers, the cost of continued conflict was becoming increasingly difficult to justify.

Yet generations of hostility had left little room for trust. Old grievances remained unresolved, and neither alliance was willing to appear weak before its enemies or its own people.

Despite these obstacles, influential voices within both alliances began advocating for an end to the fighting.

What began as cautious exchanges through intermediaries gradually developed into the possibility of formal negotiations.

The prospect was extraordinary.

For the first time in generations, representatives of Covenant and Dominion were preparing to meet with the authority to explore terms that might bring their long conflict to an end.

The delegations faced considerable opposition. Military commanders questioned the intentions of their enemies, political leaders feared concessions that might weaken their standing, and communities that had suffered through generations of warfare struggled to imagine reconciliation.

Nevertheless, preparations continued.

For the Unmade, the prospect of peace presented a serious threat.

The continuing war had provided their hidden networks with opportunities that would be far more difficult to exploit in a stable world. Missing supplies could be attributed to enemy raids, forbidden research justified as military necessity, and covert operations concealed beneath the suspicion that surrounded every unexplained incident.

Peace threatened to remove that protection.

Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand, recognised the danger. Through agents embedded within the political, military and commercial institutions of both alliances, she began preparing an operation intended not merely to disrupt the negotiations, but to destroy the fragile trust upon which they depended.

The Unmade exploited existing suspicions, arranged the movement of agents and materials, and ensured that carefully selected evidence would implicate both alliances in the violence to come.

When the delegations finally assembled, the attack struck.

**The gathering was devastated, and the hope of peace collapsed with it.**

Among those lost was someone deeply cherished by the orc leader, whose death transformed an already difficult political decision into a profoundly personal tragedy.

The human monarchy suffered an equally devastating loss. The human ruler's most trusted adviser, a figure whose counsel had helped make the negotiations possible, was killed in the attack.

For both leaders, the destruction of the delegation was no longer simply an assault upon their alliances. It was a personal betrayal, made all the more bitter by evidence that appeared to implicate the very people with whom they had sought peace.

In the confusion that followed, evidence emerged suggesting that Covenant had orchestrated the attack. Other accounts implicated Dominion. Witnesses offered contradictory testimony, while documents and materials recovered from the site appeared to confirm the accusations each side was already inclined to believe.

The surviving representatives returned to their peoples carrying different accounts of the same catastrophe.

Covenant condemned what it regarded as an act of Dominion treachery.

Dominion answered with accusations of betrayal by Covenant.

The negotiations were abandoned.

Across both alliances, outrage overwhelmed those who had advocated peace. Leaders who had supported the gathering found their judgement questioned, while military authorities demanded retaliation.

The event became known as **the Shattered Delegation**.

Its consequences spread rapidly.

Armies mobilised, coastal garrisons were reinforced and expeditions pushed deeper into contested territories. Resources that might have supported recovery were once again committed to warfare.

The fragile possibility of reconciliation had been replaced by renewed hostility, strengthened by the conviction on both sides that their enemies had deliberately betrayed an offer of peace.

Behind the conflicting accusations lay the work of the Unmade.

Agents of Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand, had orchestrated the attack and planted evidence designed to ensure that neither alliance would accept the other's account. Their purpose had never been to secure victory for Covenant or Dominion, but to preserve the conflict that allowed their hidden operations to flourish.

The Guardians began investigating the inconsistencies surrounding the attack.

Certain details suggested deliberate manipulation, and fragments of evidence pointed towards individuals whose activities extended across both alliances. Yet the Guardians' own secrecy complicated their efforts. Revealing what they knew risked exposing cells embedded within hostile institutions, while incomplete accusations might provoke further reprisals.

Unable to establish the full truth publicly, they continued their investigation from the shadows.

For Covenant and Dominion, the Shattered Delegation became another enduring grievance, reinforcing generations of suspicion.

For the Unmade, it was a calculated success.

**Two alliances had come closer to peace than they had in generations. Through a single act of manipulation, the Unmade had ensured that their war would continue.**

### The Awakening — The Network Reaches Its Threshold

The Conclave's centuries of experimentation had followed a difficult progression. Minor Resonators established that distant workings could resonate with the original breach. Regional Anchors extended those effects across connected installations. Now the first successful **Major Anchors** could synchronise several Regional Anchors, amplifying the influence of a network rather than a single site.

The first Major Anchor did not awaken the world. Its importance lay in allowing the Conclave to align more of the installations it had built over generations. As further nodes were completed and brought into resonance, their combined pressure upon the Shroud grew. The same workings altered the distribution and accessibility of primordial energy already present in the Physical Realm. They did not create magic, and Nhal was not the source of the energy becoming more readily available.

The earliest effects seemed unrelated. A ward began holding a pattern more easily, while another became dangerously unstable. A healer sustained a working that had previously exceeded their reserves. Dormant instruments responded to new practitioners, and ancient structures produced unfamiliar disturbances. Some communities benefited, others suffered, and scholars disagreed over the cause. Those near active Anchors often faced greater instability, but the cumulative network affected regions far beyond any individual device.

The Guardians investigated incidents without immediately recognising their shared origin. Centuries of compartmentalised records and lost couriers made comparisons difficult. Meanwhile, the Conclave measured the effects more carefully. Its five founders had heard Nhal before The Binding, but they were wary of mistaking chance, corrupted patterns or a rival cell's deception for a returning voice.

Eventually enough Major Anchors and supporting Regional Anchors operated in alignment to cross a critical threshold. The cumulative resonance strained the containment sufficiently for a narrow channel of influence to reach corrupted workings again.

**After generations of silence, Nhal began to communicate again.**

At first the responses came as impressions, brief whispers and knowledge that seemed to arrive without an ordinary source. They were selective and intermittent, not an unrestricted voice or command over every mind. The founders tested independent results until they recognised the same guiding presence they had known during the Divine War. Nhal remained confined beyond the Veil, but the network built without its guidance had restored a limited connection.

The return transformed the Conclave's operations. **Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand**, coordinated agents and recovered caches across both alliances. **Thes'var, the Architect**, could refine the network with new guidance. The other founders mobilised followers and protected installations as the Unmade sought to extend the system further. Their objective was to strain the Shroud and eventually permit more than whispers to pass through the breach.

The Guardians began to recognise that the lesser Anchors were not all equally dangerous to disable. A Minor Resonator could sometimes be destroyed directly. A Regional Anchor might need its supporting wards and conduits disconnected first. A Major Anchor could influence multiple linked installations, so careless destruction risked unpredictable effects. Stopping one node might protect a community without ending the wider awakening or healing the original breach.

Yet the same discovery suggested another possibility. The Anchors operated through arrangements of the six magical fields, not through an energy that belonged to Nhal. Their redistribution of accessible magic was not necessarily inseparable from the harmful resonance directed against the Shroud. **In principle, their configurations might be inverted to reinforce the containment rather than weaken it.**

This was not a simple polarity switch. Inversion would require deep understanding of the interdependent fields and the safe reconstruction of supporting structures. A Minor Resonator might be reconfigured locally; a Regional Anchor would require coordinated adjustment of its connections; a Major Anchor might demand control over several linked installations. **Oth'varen's expertise in stabilising six-field workings** and the Guardians' preserved evidence could prove vital, but a complete method had not yet been established.

If successful, inverted Anchors might preserve some of the awakening's beneficial redistribution of primordial energy while reducing corruption and strengthening the Shroud. They might even lessen the burden borne by the Seven, though neither their release nor a permanent replacement for their divine essences was assured. Whether inversion could be achieved safely, and what its ultimate costs might be, remained for later discovery.

The Conclave had spent centuries creating a network to weaken Nhal's prison. Its own achievement might yet become a means of strengthening that prison—but only if others learned to change the workings' purpose before the network was driven further towards rupture.

### Theia at the Threshold

Across the two alliances, the awakening entered lives already shaped by local obligations and inherited conflict. Human frontiers faced betrayal and disputed authority; ancient dwarf mechanisms stirred beneath surviving halls. Aelari sanctuaries struggled with unstable wards, while Vaelari researchers pursued unfamiliar discoveries. Korrath wardstones failed, Vorrath pilgrims disputed strange signs, orc Great Clans contested claims of honour, and halfling engineers reopened sealed ancestral works.

These troubles did not all originate with Nhal. Each people possessed its own history. Yet some incidents concealed Unmade operations, while others drew Guardian investigators towards connections that ordinary rulers could not see. A stolen artifact could travel through a military supply route; a legitimate scholar might unknowingly aid an infiltrator; a ruined Passage chamber might preserve evidence that altered the meaning of a familiar dispute.

Covenant and Dominion continued to act separately and to regard one another as enemies. Their investigations could uncover different fragments of the same conspiracy without producing a public alliance. The Guardians remained cautious about revealing themselves, and the surviving Kel'vari enclaves were still largely unknown beyond the Shroud.

Within that barrier, the First Anchor continued to sustain the wound that the Seven had bound themselves to contain. Beyond it, the descendants of their creations faced a renewed threat whose origins lay in the trust broken during the Age of Wonders. The Corrupted Conclave had endured through the silence. Now its purpose was moving again.

The world had begun to awaken, and the old catastrophe was no longer merely a matter of history.

# The Human Chronicle — A Road Out of Millhaven

*First-person narrative adaptation of the canonical Human 1–80 campaign. Literary draft, not a separate canon authority. The canonical campaign governs events and outcomes; the quest manifest governs objective order and evidence; the level-range dialogue scripts provide the characters' first-pass voices. Narrative transitions, atmosphere and the unnamed protagonist's private thoughts are WORKING prose. This opening installment covers the beginning of the Millhaven storyline; later chapters remain to be written.*

## Book I — The Crown and the Betrayal

### Chapter One — Every Absence

I came to Millhaven looking for work. At the time, that seemed an ordinary enough reason to enter a village. I had no commission, no letter bearing a royal seal, and no particular talent for knowing when a stranger was telling the truth. I knew only that the road had carried me there and that people needed help.

Elara was not impressed by my arrival. She looked up from the work in front of her, measured me with a glance, and asked what I could do.

“Whatever needs doing,” I said.

“That's what everyone says before they know what needs doing.”

She told me to find three people: Darian, who kept the road watch; Anwen, who tended the healing house; and Brenna, who handled the caravans. I had expected a task. Elara gave me questions.

“Speak to all three,” she said. “Then come back and tell me what you found.”

“Why does the village need another pair of hands?”

“Because a small place notices every absence. And we've had one too many lately.”

I carried those words with me through Millhaven. I found Darian concerned about the western bend, where predators had been moving close to the road. Anwen had patients waiting for supplies that had not arrived. Brenna's next caravan was overdue. Each spoke of a different problem, but the road ran through all of them.

When I returned, Elara did not ask which problem seemed most important. She asked what each person had actually told me. I repeated their accounts as carefully as I could.

“Good,” she said at last. “Start with Darian's patrol. If the road is unsafe, every other problem gets worse.”

Darian took me to the western bend. There was little to see at first: disturbed earth, the movement of brush, a stretch of road that looked no different from any other. He showed me where the ground had been pressed down and how the tracks continued toward the place where a worker had failed to return.

“Watch where you're standing, too,” he warned. “A beast won't wait for you to turn around.”

We found the worker sheltering away from the road, frightened but alive. I helped bring them past the dangerous stretch while Darian kept watch. By the time we reached safety, I understood why he had insisted on finding the person rather than simply counting the predators. A patrol could report an empty road and still leave someone stranded beside it.

Anwen's parcel was waiting at a roadside store, sealed and kept out of the damp. I brought it back to the healing house, expecting her to send me on my way. Instead she asked me to set it on the table and look at a patient whose wound had begun to close more quickly than she expected.

“I thought I'd be laid up for weeks,” the patient said. “It barely aches now.”

Anwen examined the healing tissue with the guarded attention of someone who did not want hope to outrun evidence.

“The wound's closing faster than I expected,” she said quietly. “I can't explain it yet, and I won't pretend I can.”

I could not explain it either. I remembered the moment because she refused to make more of it than she knew. There would be many people, in the years that followed, who spoke with far greater certainty about things they understood far less.

Brenna had no patience for mysteries without witnesses. She gave me the caravan's last confirmed stop and told me to look for the missing wagon.

“If you find anyone alive, listen before you act.”

The wagon had been abandoned. Ordinary goods lay scattered, but other fittings stood empty where selected crates had been secured. The damage did not tell me who had done it. A surviving teamster, hidden nearby after the attack, told me more.

“They took the crates with the marked seals,” the teamster said. “Left the rest.”

“Did you see who led them?”

“Not clearly. One of them said the buyer wouldn't pay for substitutions.”

I went back over the wagon before leaving. The empty places mattered as much as the scattered cargo. Whoever had taken the crates had wanted something specific. That was not proof of a royal plot or a great enemy beyond the hills. It was proof that this had not been a random theft.

Brenna listened without interrupting. When I finished, she asked me to repeat the teamster's words.

“So they knew what they wanted,” she said. “Elara will want to hear this.”

I had imagined that helping a village would mean fixing what was broken and then moving on. But a missing caravan did not end with a recovered survivor, any more than a safe road meant the next journey would be safe. There were still frightened workers, delayed medicine, missing crates and people whose livelihoods depended on the next wagon arriving.

I told Elara what we had found. She asked where the evidence came from, what I had seen myself, and what the teamster could only remember hearing. I was beginning to understand the difference.

The village still needed its fences mended, its seed counted and its carts repaired. Those things did not become less important because someone had stolen marked cargo. I helped where I could. At a broken pasture fence I learned how quickly an ordinary hazard could become a danger to a neighbour. In the seed stores, a duplicate requisition proved to be a matter for patient counting, not a sword. A wheel assembly needed iron and work, not a heroic speech.

Each small task brought me back to the same lesson: people lived here, and they would still live here after I had gone.

Anwen continued to watch her patients without declaring a cause for their unexpected recovery. Brenna kept asking after the missing cargo. Darian watched the road. Elara held their separate concerns together without pretending they were all one story.

I did not yet know where the marked crates had gone. I did not know why anyone would pay for those particular goods, or what kind of authority might lie behind a seal.

I knew the teamster was alive. I knew the parcel had reached the healing house. I knew the road was dangerous, and that somebody had chosen what to take from the wagon.

It was not much to carry beyond Millhaven.

It was enough to begin.


### Chapter Two — The Weight of a Seal

The first time I saw a royal seal, it was not attached to a proclamation or carried by a herald in polished colours. It lay in the palm of a frightened teamster, a fragment of damaged metal and wax he had pried from the wreck of a wagon because he could not bear to return with nothing.

The morning had been wet. Rain gathered along the eaves of Brenna's storehouse, and every wheel that crossed the yard left a dark furrow in the mud. I had spent the previous day repairing a cart with Heddan, who taught me that iron could be perfectly sound and still be fitted badly enough to fail. He had let me hold a wheel steady while he worked, then made me try the same repair myself.

“That's the trouble with doing a thing properly,” he said when I finally finished. “You have to know what it's meant to carry.”

I remembered that as Brenna set the broken seal beside the teamster's account. It was a small thing to bear the weight of so many questions.

Elara came before midday. She did not touch the fragment immediately. She asked Brenna where it had been found, who had handled it, and whether the teamster had described the marked crates before seeing it again.

“You think he's lying?” I asked.

“I think memory is a poor place to store something that matters,” she said. “Especially when someone is frightened.”

The teamster had been frightened. I had seen it in the way he watched the road even after we brought him home. I also knew he had told us the same detail twice: the thieves had refused substitutes. Someone had ordered particular goods, and whoever collected them knew the difference.

Elara unfolded a rough account of Iven Rusk, a buyer who moved stolen cargo through the hills. He was not a nobleman, nor a great commander. He was the sort of person who could make a living between other people's needs and other people's dishonesty.

“The teamster's account gives us a place to start,” she said. “Find out what Rusk took and who paid for it. Bring back evidence, not a body.”

“And if he attacks?”

“Defend yourself. But I want the name behind the theft.”

I left Millhaven before the rain had quite stopped.

The path into the hills was narrower than the caravan road. In places the grass had been pressed flat by boots; in others, water had washed away every mark. I followed signs that might have belonged to smugglers or shepherds, and I disliked how readily I began to prefer the first explanation. Once I had been given a suspect, the whole landscape seemed eager to confirm him.

Rusk's camp stood where the ground rose enough to overlook a winding approach. I watched it from a distance until I could distinguish ordinary stores from crates bearing the marks Brenna had described. The guards had weapons, but their attention wandered toward the fire and the damp bundles piled beneath canvas. These were people doing a job. That did not make the job harmless.

I approached when I could no longer learn anything by watching.

Rusk came forward with the expression of a man who had already decided what sort of visitor I was.

“You came a long way for someone else's boxes,” he said. “Whoever sent you should have stayed home.”

“Someone was left to die beside a broken wagon.”

“That wasn't my instruction.”

“Whose instruction was it?”

His gaze moved toward the covered crates, then back to me.

“You think I know every name behind a payment?”

“I think you know who paid you.”

A guard shifted at my side. I turned before he could come closer, and the movement was enough to make everyone suddenly aware of the weapons between us. The confrontation was brief and ugly, without any of the clean courage I had imagined in stories. When it ended, I was breathing too hard, and Rusk was looking at the ground rather than at me.

“I moved what they asked me to move,” he said. “I didn't ask why. The payment was good.”

That was an answer, though not the one I wanted.

Among the cargo I found the selected crates and an instruction bearing the remains of an official-looking seal. The document was damaged, but its meaning survived: specific crates requested, no substitutions permitted. I copied the legible words and kept the original from further harm. I could not make the missing parts appear by wishing for them.

On the walk home I considered what I had almost done. I had been angry at Rusk for the teamster's fear, angry at his guards for standing between me and the stolen goods, and angry that a person could speak so casually about a payment that had nearly cost another person their life. Anger had made the questions seem simple. The answers were not.

Elara listened to my account with the same attention she had given the first report from the road. When I finished, she laid the recovered instruction beside the seal.

“A buyer who knew the cargo,” she said, “and a seal that looks official. Heddan can tell us something about the workmanship.”

“Do you think someone at court ordered it?”

“I think someone wants us to think an official order exists. That's not the same thing.”

Heddan examined the fitting at his bench. He turned it in the light, compared it with legitimate workshop stock and asked me to hold two pieces side by side. Their shapes were similar. Their histories were not.

“The fitting resembles stock used by officials,” he said. “That's all I can prove. Someone could have copied it.”

“So the order is royal?”

He gave me a look I remembered better than the words that followed.

“I didn't say that. That's exactly the mistake I'm warning you against.”

It would have been easier if the metal had confessed. Instead Heddan wrapped it carefully and told me to take both fitting and shipment record to Stoneford, where a proper records office could examine them.

“One without the other tells half a story.”

That evening I returned to Anwen's healing house with a delivery from the village stores. She was making notes beside the patient whose wound had healed too quickly. I asked whether she had discovered why.

“No,” she said.

“Doesn't it trouble you?”

“Of course. But if I write down what I hoped happened instead of what I saw, the next person who needs my notes will be worse off.”

Her ledger was open beside the lamp. Names, dates, supplies, observations. Nothing grand. Yet the care with which she separated one kind of knowledge from another seemed more useful than all the certainty Rusk had offered.

I slept badly. In my dreams I was holding a seal against a door, trying to decide whether it was a key or a warning.

***

Brenna's caravan left for Stoneford under a grey sky. I travelled with the drivers, partly to protect the recovered evidence and partly because Brenna had insisted that I see the regional warehouse for myself.

“We travel together,” the caravan leader told me. “Stay close enough to hear the signal if trouble comes.”

There were long stretches when nothing happened. The wheels complained over stones. Drivers called to one another across the road. At a crossing we waited while the wagons were brought through in order, and I watched people make small decisions that kept the journey from becoming a disaster. Someone checked a fastening before it failed. Someone shared water with a tired worker. Someone noticed a rut deep enough to turn a loaded cart.

By then I had begun to understand that safety was rarely a single act. It was a hundred ordinary acts performed before danger became visible.

Stoneford was larger than Millhaven and less inclined to trust a stranger carrying damaged paperwork. At the warehouse a clerk looked first at Brenna's introduction and then at the recovered records.

“We'll need the original dates,” the clerk said.

A local correspondent working with Mara Thorne met me among stacks of ledgers. The correspondent set three books on the table: Perrin's stock book, a courier docket and the warehouse tally.

“One shipment appears in all three,” the correspondent explained. “But the dates and quantities don't agree. Find the contradiction.”

I examined the entries until the figures began to swim. The stock book showed one movement; the docket another. The warehouse tally recorded the same goods under a date that should have made their journey impossible.

“Could be a copying mistake,” I said.

“Could be. That's why we compare all three.”

I read them again, this time setting aside what I thought the records ought to say. Two agreed on the dispatch date. The third placed receipt after the goods had already been signed out.

“There,” I said. “They left before the warehouse claims to have received them.”

The correspondent leaned forward and checked the entries.

“Now we have something that can be independently checked.”

The discovery did not feel like victory. It felt like finding a loose stone beneath a wall and realising I could not yet see how far the weakness ran.

Perrin had handled the transfer paperwork after Edric's death. That fact mattered because it gave us someone to question, not because it made him guilty. The correspondent obtained the proper regional authority before we approached him. I waited through the formalities with the recovered instruction folded safely away, trying not to imagine what Perrin would say.

He was not the villain I had prepared myself to meet. He looked like a clerk who had spent too many hours remembering orders he had been paid to copy.

“I copied the order as it was given,” he told us. “The office had changed hands after Edric died. I didn't choose who signed it.”

“Who authorised the payment?”

“The seal was on the instruction. The name wasn't mine to question.”

“Then why keep a copy?”

He looked at the document between us.

“Because I knew someone would ask later. I didn't expect it to be you.”

I wanted to ask why he had not spoken sooner. I wanted to tell him what the teamster had endured, and how close the theft had come to becoming a death. But the question before us was what he knew, what he had preserved, and what could be proved. He answered under the reeve's authority. His account did not repair the damaged record, but it gave the record a history.

When the interview ended, the correspondent said the evidence belonged before someone who could examine the royal trail properly.

“Elara can introduce you to Mara Thorne. She'll know what to do with a record like this.”

The return to Millhaven felt longer than the journey out. I had crossed the same roads, seen the same fields, and heard the same wheels, but now every wagon seemed to carry an invisible chain of signatures and decisions. I had once thought a seal made an order trustworthy. I was learning that it only made the question of trust more urgent.

Elara received me without ceremony. I laid out the authenticated copy, the comparison of the three books and Perrin's authorised testimony. She listened until I had finished.

“You brought the instruction back,” she said, “and Perrin answered under the reeve's authority. That's something Mara can examine, not just another rumour about Edric's office.”

“Will she believe us?”

“She'll test the record. That's why I'm sending you to her.”

She prepared an introduction and placed it beside the copies. The original remained with its lawful custodian. I found myself relieved by that small restraint. Nothing about the investigation had given me the right to own everything I touched.

“Take this to Mara,” Elara said. “Tell her what you saw, what the witnesses said and what the records show. Don't improve the story for her.”

I looked toward the village road. Darian would still be watching it. Brenna would still be counting arrivals. Anwen would still be writing what she could prove in her ledger. Millhaven had not become less ordinary because the evidence now pointed beyond it.

“Do you think I'll come back?” I asked.

Elara regarded me for a moment.

“That's not something I can promise you.”

I folded the introduction and put it away.

The road to Valecrown waited beyond Stoneford, and somewhere along it the names behind the payments would have to become more than shadows on damaged paper. I had no idea how far those names might reach.

For the first time, I was afraid not only of what I might find, but of how easily I might misunderstand it.

I went anyway.


### Chapter Three — The City of Other People's Certainties

Before I left Stoneford behind, I learned how easily a village's troubles could become a city's footnotes.

At Greyfall Quarry, a shift bell had rung without the workers returning. Joss showed me the lower workface on a rough sketch, his finger tracing the route from the bell to the cut where predators had begun to gather. He had already counted the missing twice. When I asked why he still carried the tally, he said that a count was the only promise he could make to the families waiting at home.

We found the workers sheltering beyond the lower passage. I remember the sound of their voices before I remember the fight: one calling for quiet, another insisting someone should try the way out. The cave predators came at us in the narrow space where turning was difficult. When it was over, I helped bring the workers back through the cut and recovered the shift tally from a tool chest.

Joss checked every name before he thanked me.

“The quarry needs a safer route,” he said, “not a conspiracy invented to explain every broken shift.”

I had come to Greyfall with a mind full of stolen crates and false dates. His words embarrassed me. I had nearly begun to see the same hidden hand behind every misfortune.

At Stoneford's damaged bridge, two people were equally certain their need should come first. The miller spoke of flour that could not reach households; the merchant spoke of the repair materials without which there would be no safe crossing at all. The reeve made me inspect the supports before listening to either argument. I could feel the bridge move beneath my boots. It was easier to promise fairness from the bank than while standing over the water.

I helped set the work in an order that could be defended to both sides. The choice did not make either need disappear. When the crossing reopened, the reeve said, “Neither claim was forgotten. That's what a useful decision looks like.”

Brenna's warehouse watch brought a different lesson. Missing tools had frightened honest workers into suspecting one another. We examined the loss records, watched the perimeter and intercepted thieves who wanted the tools for their value, not for any grand design. Brenna was relieved, though not pleased.

“Not every wrong in Stoneford belongs to the same enemy,” she told me.

Then a recovered caravan segment needed bringing home by the longer road. I walked with the drivers past the obstruction that had closed their old route. When one wagon stopped, the others waited at an agreed safe place rather than pushing forward and leaving it behind. By the time we reached Millhaven, the survivors were exhausted, the supplies accounted for, and no one was interested in hearing a heroic version of the journey.

Elara listened to the report.

“The road changed,” she said, “but the people still needed to make it back.”

I thought of those journeys later, when the roads beyond Millhaven began carrying me toward decisions made in halls I had never seen. A repaired bridge could be inspected. A returned worker could speak. A captured thief could be questioned. But how was I to measure a king's grief, or a court's loyalty, or the harm a confident accusation might do?

I left with Elara's introduction to Mara Thorne, copies of the records and the uneasy conviction that I understood less than I had when I first entered Millhaven.

***

Valecrown announced itself through traffic before it appeared as a city. Wagons gathered at the approaches, their drivers watching one another for the first chance to move. People with letters and seals stood beside people carrying sacks or tools. Every queue seemed to contain another queue within it, and every answer depended upon someone elsewhere deciding that a question had been asked in the proper form.

I had imagined a capital as a place where uncertainty was finally settled. Instead I found that it had been sorted into offices.

Mara Thorne received me in a room that smelled of ink, worn bindings and the faint dampness of documents brought in from the road. She did not ask whether I had found the conspirators. She asked me to put the evidence down in the order I had obtained it.

“You brought a sealed case from Stoneford,” she said. “That earns you a hearing, not a conclusion. Tell me which parts you witnessed and which came from someone else.”

I began with the broken wagon, the empty fittings and the teamster. I told her what Rusk had admitted, what the damaged instruction said, and how Heddan had refused to identify its issuer from the metalwork alone. I described the three books and the impossible sequence of receipt and dispatch.

She stopped me whenever I moved too quickly from one fact to the next.

“You saw the empty fittings,” she said. “Did you see the marked crates taken?”

“No. The teamster did.”

“Then that is his account, not yours.”

I felt the old impatience rise in me. “Does it matter, if his account matches what we recovered?”

“It matters precisely because it matches. If we cannot tell the difference between an independent witness and a witness repeating our conclusion, the agreement proves less than you think.”

She had me repeat the dates without consulting the copies. When I stumbled, she returned them to me.

“A story that survives another set of eyes is worth keeping,” she said. “We will check the courier and warehouse testimony independently. After that, I can request the records we are not yet permitted to see.”

I had thought Elara was sending me to someone who could name the guilty. Mara was teaching me how not to name them too soon.

Lysa, who guarded the authorised office registers, had even less patience for speculation. She placed a volume before me and told me to copy the dates exactly as they appeared.

“These registers survived a change of office and three different clerks,” she said. “Don't tidy them to fit a theory.”

The entries concerned the reorganisation that followed Edric's death. Names shifted between columns; offices passed into new hands; duties were reassigned with a speed that could be read as efficiency or haste, depending on what a reader already believed.

“Why were the offices reorganised?” I asked.

“After Edric died, people wanted order restored quickly. Whether they achieved it is another question.”

She authenticated the copy when I finished. I asked what the dates meant.

“What they mean is Mara's question, not mine.”

Outside the records room I watched a clerk try to restore a damaged public index while petitioners argued over which queue had been theirs first. I helped sort the surviving entries and match them to their proper references. The work was dull, painstaking and oddly reassuring. In the capital, too, there were people trying to make sure someone could find a document when it mattered.

Not all of Valecrown's shortages came from deception. At the barracks, the quartermaster showed me ration counts that had fallen behind genuine need. Soldiers who had spent the day protecting the city were waiting for bread. The problem required honest accounting and another supply arrangement, not the discovery of a villain. I found myself glad of the distinction. If every problem was corruption, there would be no room left for ordinary mistakes—or ordinary repair.

Mara later arranged an interview with a witness whose account touched the disputed movements of men and records. Before we entered, she warned me not to ask questions that carried their answers inside them.

I had done exactly that with Perrin, I realised, when I asked who had authorised a payment before establishing what he had seen. He had answered anyway. Another witness might not.

I listened more carefully this time. The testimony did not give us everything we wanted. Its value lay in the parts that could be checked against other records without teaching the witness what to remember.

By the time Mara told me the king would grant an audience, I had learned to dread the phrase *we know*. It had begun to sound less like a conclusion and more like a responsibility.

***

Aldren did not look like the king I had imagined on the road.

I had expected confidence, perhaps anger, perhaps a ruler too occupied with the war to care about the paperwork of a dead adviser. What I saw was a man who had learned to keep grief from interrupting the business of his court, but not from entering it.

“Edric believed there was still a path to peace,” he told me. “I sent him to find it. He never came home.”

There was no ceremony in the admission. He spoke as though the words had worn a channel through him.

“You trusted him,” I said.

“With my life.” His gaze did not leave mine. “I have never been certain whose hand struck first.”

The Shattered Delegation had taken lives on both sides. I knew enough of the war to understand why the deaths had become proof of betrayal to people who had wanted proof already. But Aldren's uncertainty was harder to bear than certainty would have been. He had sent Edric to a meeting that might have changed the future, and the man had not returned.

Cassian stood within the king's circle of confidence. He spoke carefully, without the nervous haste I had seen in lesser officials when their records were challenged. His suggestion that the inquiry examine military correspondence seemed reasonable. It offered a route forward without asking Aldren to condemn anyone he trusted.

Aldren authorised the continuing inquiry and told me to accept Cassian's assistance. Then he warned me that doubt could do as much damage as a false accusation.

I left the audience with a warrant and a new unease. The court had given me access, but it had also given me a direction in which to look. I wondered whether I would have recognised the difference if Mara had not taught me to ask.

Garrick was not pleased to see me.

“I opposed the talks in council,” he said before I had finished explaining why I had come. “I did not murder Edric.”

His anger was plain. So was his refusal to soften his position on the negotiations merely to make himself more likeable.

“Can your records prove that?” I asked.

“They can show where I was and what orders I gave. They won't prove I liked the negotiations.”

I examined the preserved logs. Their dates and instructions could be tested. They did not turn Garrick into an advocate for peace. They did something more important: they made it impossible to treat his opposition as sufficient evidence of participation in Edric's killing.

I told him what I had found.

“Then tell Mara,” he said. “Even if it makes an easier story harder to tell.”

I had spent much of my journey trying to discover who had done wrong. Now I was being asked to preserve a truth about someone whose views I did not share. It should not have felt difficult. It did.

Mara received the findings without triumph.

“An accusation that fails a proper test must be allowed to fail,” she said.

We set the military logs beside the reorganisation dates, the payment instruction and the witness accounts. Each had its own origin. Each could be challenged. Together they formed a pattern, but there were still gaps through which an accusation might fall.

The next comparison concerned the time an authorisation had been recorded, the format in which it appeared, and a witness whose account had not been drawn from the same office. Mara made me check them separately.

I wanted the answer to be immediate. I wanted Edric's death to acquire a single name and a single cause, something I could carry back to Aldren and Elara without qualification.

Instead I found that the more closely I examined the records, the more deliberate some of their inconsistencies appeared. An office had changed hands. A request seemed to have existed before it could properly have been made. The shape of the record was familiar, but its place in the sequence was wrong.

Mara stood beside me as I laid the final copy down.

“Do we have enough?” I asked.

“Enough to keep looking,” she said. “Not enough to accuse.”

I stared at the pages. Somewhere between Millhaven and Valecrown I had begun to believe that every careful answer must lead to a final one. Mara's words reminded me of the teamster hiding beside the road, the healed wound Anwen could not explain, and Heddan refusing to make a royal order out of a familiar piece of metal.

There was still a missing part of the story. Someone had paid for it to remain missing.

And for the first time since I left home, I understood that finding it might place me against people the king trusted more than he trusted me.


### Chapter Four — The Price of an Office

The first lie I recognised in Valecrown was not spoken aloud.

It sat in a register, beneath a date that should not have existed. A request had been approved before the office claimed to have received it. The signature was properly formed. The paper had passed through the right hands. Every outward sign suggested the machinery of government had done precisely what it was meant to do, except that time itself refused to agree.

Mara stood at the opposite side of the table while I compared the authorisation with the original office format.

“Someone has the order of events wrong,” she said, “or wants us to.”

“Could Cassian explain it?”

“Perhaps. First we need the witness and the original format. Don't accuse a man because the paperwork is strange.”

It was infuriating advice because I knew she was right. By then I had learned that I could make a convincing case against almost anyone if I began by deciding what their records ought to mean. Mara made me begin again whenever she caught me doing it.

The witness had seen part of the transfer. Their memory was clear in places and uncertain in others. I asked what had happened before I asked who might have been responsible. When they hesitated, I waited. Silence felt longer in a room where everyone expected an answer.

Afterward Mara read the account without changing a word.

“That stands on its own,” she said. “Whether it supports our theory is a separate matter.”

We compared it with the office format and the recorded authorisation time. Three independent details contradicted the official account. It was enough to follow the payments, though not yet enough to pronounce a verdict.

The court continued around us as though nothing had changed. Clerks carried bundles between rooms. Petitioners argued over appointments. In the public records hall I helped Lysa restore an index whose damaged entries had sent people from one office to another for days. At the barracks I saw a quartermaster struggling to feed soldiers with supplies that had been honestly delayed. Neither problem concealed a grand design. The people suffering from them would not have cared whether their inconvenience was important to my investigation.

I thought of that when Garrick asked me to inspect the western approach.

“Raiders know the road is poorly watched,” he said. “Whatever you think of my politics, those roads still need protecting.”

I had not expected to accept work from him. I still disliked the certainty with which he spoke about the failed peace. Yet the people travelling that road had no part in our disagreement. I went with the patrol, helped repel the raiders and returned with a clearer account of where the watch was thin.

Garrick studied the report.

“A road doesn't ask which side of a court argument you're on,” he said.

He had opposed the delegation. He had not thereby confessed to killing Edric. That distinction was becoming the measure of whether I deserved the authority people were beginning to lend me.

***

The payment trail led to an intermediary who had survived long enough to be questioned. Mara insisted on securing the person and the independently stored records together. One without the other would leave room for the story to be reshaped by whoever spoke loudest.

We found the records first. They showed money moving through an office whose authority had changed after Edric's death. Some payments were ordinary enough to vanish among legitimate entries. Others carried references that connected the office to the compromised security arrangements surrounding the delegation.

The intermediary's account did not make the documents more dramatic. It made them harder to dismiss.

When we returned, Mara spread the evidence across her table. There was no satisfaction in her face.

“Are you saying Cassian ordered it?” I asked.

“I'm saying the evidence now points toward him. I won't go further until we can prove the chain.”

We checked it again. Dates, payments, authority, testimony. The links held. The records no longer supported a theory built only on Cassian's opportunity or his closeness to Aldren. They established a connection between his authority, the compromised security and the intended killing.

I looked at the seal on the final instruction and remembered Heddan holding a similar fitting at his bench.

*I didn't say that.*

He had been right to refuse the easy conclusion. It made this harder one possible.

“Aldren must see this privately,” Mara said.

I dreaded that meeting more than I had dreaded Rusk's camp.

A king could order an arrest. I did not know what a friend could bear.

***

Aldren listened without interrupting as Mara presented the records. Cassian stood before him, still composed, still able to speak in the measured language of duty and wartime necessity.

For a moment I wanted the king to reject everything. It would have been easier to watch anger than the slow change in his face as each independent account was placed beside the next.

“Cassian,” Aldren said at last, “these orders bear your authority. Mara has brought independent testimony. I am ordering your detention until this can be heard properly.”

The room seemed to contract around the words.

I watched Cassian's eyes move toward the guards.

“He may try to run,” I said.

“Then the guards will stop him,” Aldren replied. “No one will turn my court into a place of execution.”

The order was lawful and unmistakable. It was not enough to keep Cassian in the room.

The escape happened quickly. A movement toward an inner passage, a shouted warning, the confusion of guards trying to obey an arrest order without turning the audience chamber into a killing ground. By the time we reached the concealed entrance, Cassian had disappeared below the court.

Aldren stood very still when Mara told him.

“He escaped through a concealed passage,” she said.

The king looked at the evidence on the table, then at the doorway through which his trusted adviser had fled.

“Bring him back alive,” he said.

It was not mercy in place of justice. It was a refusal to let the man who had betrayed his trust choose the manner in which the truth would end.

***

The passage beneath Valecrown had been built for movement without witnesses. Its corridors branched through storage rooms and service exits, narrow places where a person could vanish into the ordinary labour of a city. Cassian knew them. Mara knew enough to fear what a hurried pursuit might cost.

“Before we follow,” she told me, “clear the civilian exits and copy the evidence. If the pursuit goes badly, the truth must survive us.”

I wanted to argue. Every moment we spent securing an exit was another moment Cassian could use to escape.

Mara saw the thought before I spoke it.

“We don't risk innocent lives for a faster arrest.”

So we checked the exits. We warned the people whose work brought them near the underground route. We lodged an independent copy of the dispatches beyond the reach of anyone waiting below. The preparations were slow, practical and humiliating to my impatience.

Only when the civilians were safe and the copies secured did Mara allow the pursuit to begin.

The air changed as we descended. It smelled of wet stone, old timber and the stale heat of lamps burning where daylight never reached. I could hear water somewhere beyond the walls. Every sound seemed to return from a direction different from the one in which it began.

Sorn commanded the guards holding the passage. He did not offer a confession or a speech about the fate of kingdoms. He had orders, armed defenders and ground chosen to favour them. We fought through the narrow approaches, using each cleared chamber to recover our breath before pressing on.

I remember the fear more clearly than the blows. I remember wondering whether the next turn would bring a dead end, whether a guard would strike from behind, whether Cassian had already escaped through some exit we had failed to find. The thought that I might kill him in the struggle frightened me almost as much as the thought that he would get away.

Sorn fell. We secured the dispatch copy he had guarded. Beyond him, Cassian had nowhere left to run.

He faced us with the same controlled expression he had worn in Aldren's court, though there was less room now for courtesy.

“You've followed papers a long way,” he said.

“People died because of what those papers conceal.”

“People die in wars.”

I thought of Edric, whom I had never met, and of the king who had sent him to seek peace. I thought of the teamster beside the broken wagon. Their lives were not equal in rank, but neither could be made into a line of acceptable losses by the person who had helped cause them.

“You will answer in court,” I said.

Cassian moved as though he might force another way through. I stopped him. When the struggle ended, he was alive.

Mara took custody of the recovered dispatches. She checked that Cassian could be moved safely, then stood for a moment looking at the passage we had crossed.

“Edric's death will not be explained away by a convenient corpse,” she said.

I had once thought bringing a guilty man down would feel like the end of something. Instead I felt the weight of having kept him alive, and the greater weight of what his testimony might yet reveal.

We brought him back to Valecrown under guard.

***

Aldren received the news without celebration.

Cassian had been captured alive. His authority was removed, and lawful custody had been established. The evidence against him would not disappear into a rumour of betrayal or an unrecorded death beneath the palace.

“You brought Cassian back alive,” Aldren said. “That matters. I cannot undo what happened to Edric, but I can make sure the inquiry continues.”

“Where does the trail lead?”

“The shipping references extend beyond our borders. This warrant permits you to pursue the evidence with the consent of our allies.”

He placed the commission before me. It was less grand than I expected. There was no promise that the next city would open its archives, no authority to command another people, no guarantee that the truth beyond Valecrown would be easier to bear.

I found that I trusted the warrant more for what it did not claim.

“Report to Elara,” Aldren said. “Tell her what became of the case she sent you to investigate.”

On the road home I passed the same crossings and fields that had once seemed to mark the whole of my world. I could not look at them without remembering the people who had kept them working while I was away. The bridge was open. Caravans moved again. Nothing about Cassian's capture had repaired a fence or delivered medicine, and yet I understood now how easily the decisions of distant offices could reach into every one of those ordinary lives.

Elara listened as I told her what had happened. She asked whether Cassian had been brought back alive and whether the king had removed him from office.

“Yes,” I said. “The inquiry continues under a limited commission. Edric's death is not the end of the evidence.”

“Good. People here deserved an answer, not a convenient story.”

She read the commission carefully.

“The work may take you farther,” she said. “Bring back what can be proved.”

We returned recovered goods to the households that had waited for them, careful not to expose witnesses whose accounts had helped make the arrest possible. Some families wanted to know every name. Others were simply relieved to have their possessions back. I learned to answer without turning protected testimony into village gossip.

Darian asked me to join a patrol along the reopened road. He had adjusted the watch since the first time we walked the western bend together. The routes were better covered; the workers knew where to report danger. He seemed almost embarrassed when I noticed.

“Mostly I've learned how much I didn't know,” he said.

When the patrol ended, he looked toward Millhaven.

“The watch has it from here. You don't have to keep solving our problems for us.”

Brenna was loading another wagon when I found her. She needed supplies sent to the farms and the healing house. Both deliveries mattered; she wanted help deciding which should leave first. I chose the healing house, remembering Anwen's ledger and the patients who could not wait for an explanation before they needed care.

“Anwen will appreciate it,” Brenna said. “The farmers will get their share on the next cart.”

She lifted a crate into place and smiled at the sight of the work still waiting.

“A royal investigation ended, and we're still loading wagons. That's how I like it.”

That evening I stood outside Millhaven and watched the road darken. I had left the village believing that courage meant going toward danger. I returned knowing that sometimes it meant refusing to strike when striking would be easier, or admitting that a record did not prove what I wanted it to prove.

Edric was still dead. Aldren had lost a friend and discovered a betrayal. Garrick remained opposed to the peace Edric had sought, but he had not been responsible for the murder. The Dominion remained an enemy in a war that would not be settled by Cassian's arrest.

And I had a commission that led beyond the borders of the place I knew.

I thought of the first task Elara had given me: speak to three people, listen carefully and come back with their accounts.

It had seemed a small beginning.

Now, as the last light left the road, I wondered how many more people I would have to listen to before I understood the shape of what we faced.

I went inside to prepare for the journey.


## Book II — Shadows of Conflict

### Chapter Five — Four Peoples, One Harbour

The commission Aldren had given me was folded so often that its edges began to soften. I had imagined a king's warrant would make the next part of my journey easier. In truth, it made my obligations harder to ignore. The document named what I was permitted to investigate, but it did not turn the roads ahead into royal property, or the people beyond them into subjects of Aldren's court.

Mara met me before I left for Tidehold. Cassian's recovered ledger lay between us, open at two shipping references.

“Tidehold,” she said, touching the first. Then the second. “Twice. I won't pretend a Human warrant gives us rights in an allied harbour.”

“Who has authority there?”

“The port council. Start with customs. Ask permission.”

I had heard that word so often by then that it was beginning to sound like the hinge upon which every locked door turned. I also knew what happened when someone decided a worthy purpose was sufficient permission to do anything.

The road carried me away from Millhaven's familiar fields and toward a coast that announced itself long before I could see the water. Salt settled on my lips. The air changed. The traffic thickened into a procession of carts, travellers and guarded cargo, all drawn toward the same place.

Tidehold was not like Valecrown. In the Human capital, the offices of government had seemed to radiate from the king's authority, even when they argued over its limits. Tidehold belonged to an alliance. Its docks and streets were used by four peoples whose obligations overlapped without becoming identical.

I watched a dispute over unloading space end not when one side yielded to the loudest voice, but when the workers found the official empowered to settle that particular claim. It took longer than shouting. It appeared to last longer, too.

At customs, I presented the case from Stoneford, the records from Valecrown and Aldren's commission. The clerk examined the seal without bowing to it. I felt a foolish sting of embarrassment before I remembered that this was precisely what Mara had told me to expect.

The harbour records confirmed the disputed shipping dates. Confirmation gave me another question, not a right to search wherever I pleased.

Corven received the request in an alliance chamber where the noise of the harbour was faintly audible through the walls.

“Tidehold serves four peoples, not one crown,” he said. “Tell me what you need and what you intend to do with it.”

“I need access to the cargo records.”

“Then you will receive limited access, with a clerk present. You will not seize a warehouse on a Human king's word.”

He spoke without hostility. That made the limit feel firmer. I had come expecting to prove that I was trustworthy; he wanted to know whether I understood whose trust I was asking for.

“I understand,” I said.

“Good. Respect the terms, and the next request will be easier to hear.”

The inspection led me to Ysra and the provision depot, where a shortage had become an argument among people who all believed they had done their work properly. The manifests said goods had arrived. The storehouses disagreed.

Ysra spread the original tallies across a scarred table. She had the exhausted patience of someone who had counted the same crates three times and still been told the problem was her arithmetic.

“Someone changed the requisitions after they were signed,” she said.

“Could it be an accounting error?”

“It could. That's why we'll compare the original tally before we accuse a dockworker.”

We checked the figures. I had grown accustomed to finding lies in documents, but I tried not to be disappointed when some discrepancies turned out to be ordinary mistakes. A number copied into the wrong column was still a problem; it simply did not require an enemy to explain it.

The altered requisitions were different. They selected particular cargo. Once again, someone had known what to remove.

Ysra closed the last ledger with a sharp movement of her hand.

“Whoever did this knew which goods to take.”

I thought of the teamster's voice beside the broken wagon: *The buyer wouldn't pay for substitutions.*

The same care had followed the cargo across more than one jurisdiction. It had crossed borders more easily than I had.

***

Northwatch Landing lay beyond the harbour, reached by ship rather than a convenient road. Nessa met the departing supply party and looked me over as though trying to decide which part of my equipment would fail first.

“Northwatch is a military landing, not a sightseeing stop,” she said. “You can travel with us if you follow orders at the shore.”

“What should I expect?”

“Hostile patrols, bad weather, and soldiers who don't know your name. Stay alert.”

The ship left Tidehold behind in slow degrees. I watched the harbour shrink until its separate quays became a single line against the coast. Out on the water, the distinctions that mattered so much in council chambers were harder to see. The wind did not care which people had signed the cargo manifest. The sea did not care whose king had authorised my journey.

I found that comforting until the weather turned.

The crossing was not a great storm, only enough wind and rough water to make every loose object a hazard and every step uncertain. The sailors moved with the confidence of people who had learned to respect conditions they could not command. I held where I was told to hold and tried not to think about how little use a royal commission would be if the ship foundered.

At Northwatch, Nessa made me report to the quartermaster before leaving the defences. Beyond the landing lay a hostile coast. Dominion patrols held positions inland, and Covenant soldiers watched the approaches with the particular fatigue of people who knew a quiet morning could become a battle by afternoon.

I found Darian among the support unit.

For a moment I saw him as he had been at Millhaven's western bend, pointing out fresh tracks in the dirt. He recognised me before I spoke and smiled, though the expression faded when he explained why he had sent for help.

“A supply party missed its signal,” he said. “I know what it's like to be the one who doesn't come back when expected.”

“We'll find them.”

“Find them alive if you can. Don't turn a rescue into a hunt.”

We took the coastal route, keeping to the ground that offered the safest passage around the contested positions. It was not a secret road, and it did not make us invisible. More than once we waited while distant patrols passed, listening to the sounds of armed people who had every reason to regard us as enemies.

The missing party had become stranded away from its planned return. Its members were alive, but frightened and short of safe options. I remember one of them trying to apologise for the delay before Darian stopped them.

“Save the account for when we're back,” he said. “Right now, we're getting you home.”

We returned with the survivors and their military route report. Darian read it carefully, then sent word that the watch procedures needed changing.

I had thought that seeing him at Northwatch would make the world feel smaller. Instead it reminded me how far the concerns of Millhaven could travel. A missing worker at a western bend and a stranded supply party on a hostile coast were not the same event. But the obligation to bring someone home had survived the distance between them.

***

Nessa wanted reconnaissance of the Dominion positions beyond the landing. She showed me the military posts associated with Rukhan's coastal defence and marked the boundaries we were not to cross.

“The Dominion is defending its coast,” she said. “I want their patrol routes, not a pile of bodies. Keep clear of civilian dwellings.”

“Why not attack the lookout?”

“Because an observation mission doesn't become a raid just because you have a weapon.”

We approached by a guarded route, stopping where we could observe without entering the homes beyond the military line. I watched the defenders change positions and signal to one another. They were disciplined. They understood the terrain. They were not waiting to be exposed as secretly friendly, nor were they monsters whose existence excused anything done to them.

I had been raised on stories in which the enemy was always most dangerous when hidden and least human when seen. Watching ordinary soldiers obey their watch rotations did not make the war less real. It made its costs harder to simplify.

Nessa studied the reconnaissance when I returned.

“Their defences are disciplined,” she said. “That tells us something.”

She did not say it told us they were innocent. She did not say it made an attack impossible. She used the report for what it was: knowledge of a real opponent, gathered without turning a scouting assignment into an assault on civilians.

The expedition clerk brought the next discrepancy while I was still thinking about those patrols.

A military depot had reported cargo lost in an attack. Yet the records placed the disappearance before the attack had occurred.

I almost laughed. The same impossibility again, dressed in another uniform.

“We need a copy from inside,” the clerk said, “not another rumour.”

“You want me to enter their depot?”

“Quietly, if possible. Their guards will treat you as an enemy. No one there is expecting a friendly visitor.”

I went because the records mattered, not because the danger made the task noble. The depot was guarded and its stores were military property. I kept to the evidence we had come to verify, copied the accounting and withdrew without mistaking a successful intrusion for ownership of the place.

The dates were unmistakable. A private consignment had been removed before the reported attack. The comparison with Nemi's serial records gave the loss a more precise shape. Neither army could honestly explain that earlier removal by pointing to the later fighting.

The clerk checked the copy twice.

“Someone took the cargo before either army could blame the other.”

I looked toward the coast, where the lines of the war ran through roads, watch posts and settlements that had existed before I came to investigate them. The Dominion's attack was real. The Covenant's defence was real. And beneath those events someone had been moving selected materials with an attention that did not belong to the ordinary disorder of battle.

In Millhaven I had followed the absence of a few crates. At Tidehold I had followed their paperwork. At Northwatch I had followed the marks they left in military stores.

Now I had a question that no ledger alone could answer.

What were those materials meant to become?

The answer waited farther along the coast, at a signal house where a surveyor had been recording disturbances that ordinary supply records could not explain.

I set out with the copied evidence secured against the weather, aware that I was crossing into a kind of inquiry for which I had no training.

I still believed a record should be tested before it was trusted.

I was about to learn how difficult that rule could become when the thing being measured was itself unfamiliar.


### Chapter Six — The Silent Signal

The signal house stood above a stretch of coast where the wind seemed to find every crack in the stone.

I reached it carrying copies of a depot ledger, a record of missing cargo and questions that had grown less ordinary with every mile. At Northwatch, I had watched soldiers defend roads and stores against an enemy they could name. Here the trouble had no such shape. It lay in a set of readings taken from instruments whose needles moved when nothing visible disturbed them.

Sera was working beside a narrow window, comparing figures written on two separate sheets. She looked up only when I placed the evidence on the table.

“You've brought equipment records that don't belong in an ordinary supply dispute,” she said. “This station has seen a similar disturbance.”

“What is it?”

“A resonance anomaly. That's a description, not an explanation.”

She made room for the papers. I had grown accustomed to being asked where evidence came from; Sera wanted to know how it had been measured. She questioned the conditions under which the instruments had been used, whether anyone had repeated the readings and whether the equipment had behaved the same way before the disturbance began.

I could answer some of those questions. Others made me painfully aware that I had carried a report across a hostile coast without knowing how to judge its most important numbers.

“We need measurements before conclusions,” she said.

Outside, the wind worried at the shutters. Inside, a small device rested within a protective housing. Its construction did not match the ordinary equipment I had seen in the depots. I could recognise the workmanship only in the broadest sense: metal joined to metal, fittings made to carry a load, surfaces shaped for a purpose. Whatever purpose this one served, it was not to hold a wagon wheel together.

Sera showed me a preliminary note from Brynja in Kar Durn. The Dwarven engineer had not examined the device itself; she had been sent descriptions of its housing and the measurements available so far. Her response identified precautions and limits, not a final explanation.

We moved to an observation position overlooking the apparatus. One of the supports had become unsafe. Sera would not permit further readings until it was secured, though every delay made her more restless.

I helped steady the position and checked the access route while she reset the instruments. When the next readings came, she compared them with Brynja's note, then with the figures I had brought from the coast.

“The readings match more closely than I expected,” she said.

“Does that tell us what it is?”

“It tells us the measurements deserve another witness. I want the Surveyor to see them.”

She said the title as though it required no explanation. I had heard of the Grey Surveyor in scattered references, always at the edge of someone else's investigation. I knew only that the person behind the name was associated with difficult measurements and warnings that other people sometimes resented.

I expected another official with a seal and a claim of authority.

The Grey Surveyor arrived without offering either.

He examined the apparatus from a distance before approaching the records. His attention moved first to the instruments, then to the structure holding them, and finally to the space between the two. I had the peculiar impression that he was studying not what the device did, but the conditions that allowed it to do anything at all.

Sera explained our observations. He asked her to repeat the limits of the measurement, not the most dramatic result.

When she finished, he looked at me.

“You have seen it operate?”

“Only from the observation position.”

“And you have not opened the housing?”

“No.”

“Good.”

It was not praise. It sounded like relief carefully kept out of his voice.

He did not tell me what he thought the device truly was. He spoke instead of isolation, load, and the danger of disturbing a working whose behaviour had not been fully understood. I found the caution frustrating. Yet the apparatus stood before us, and I could not deny that it had already done something we were struggling to describe.

Sera had recovered an unstable sample. She wanted it preserved because the material might answer questions the instruments could not. She also knew that keeping it could endanger the people responsible for it.

“We can preserve it under strict isolation,” she told me, “or destroy it after copying its readings. Neither choice is without cost.”

“What would you do?”

“I would preserve the evidence if I could guarantee safety. I cannot guarantee that.”

The Grey Surveyor listened without intervening. When I asked whether the measurements would survive the destruction of the sample, he explained the difference between the physical material and the record of its behaviour. We could retain the latter. We could not pretend it was identical to keeping the former.

I chose to have the sample destroyed safely after its measurements were copied.

I watched the procedure from behind the agreed boundary. Nothing spectacular happened. There was no burst of light to make the choice feel important, no sign that the world approved. When it was over, the sealed record remained and the sample did not.

Sera wrote down my decision.

“We have the measurements either way,” she said. “I won't pretend the risk was simple.”

I wondered whether I would have made the same choice if I had understood more. The uncertainty stayed with me. I had learned to preserve evidence whenever possible; now I was learning that preservation could itself become a danger.

***

Brynja's instructions arrived as a set of carefully bounded procedures. Separate mechanical isolators were to be fitted. A physical cutoff had to remain accessible. No one was to treat a stable reading as proof that the apparatus would remain stable under every condition.

Sera laid the instructions out on the worktable.

“This isn't permission to improvise,” she said. “Fit the isolators exactly as instructed.”

“Why hasn't Brynja come herself?”

“She's responsible for work in her own hold. We can ask her to examine the originals when this site is secure.”

I thought of Heddan at his bench in Millhaven, explaining how a fitting could be sound and still fail when used for the wrong load. The scale of the problem had changed. The need to respect what a structure could bear had not.

The Grey Surveyor reviewed the shutdown sequence. He insisted that we know where to retreat if the readings exceeded their limits, and that the cutoff could be reached without passing through the most dangerous part of the installation.

I asked whether the plan would work.

“It is a plan for limiting what we do not yet control,” he said. “That is not the same as certainty.”

We tested the supports, checked the isolators and walked the retreat route. I remember being impatient with the repetitions. Later, when the signal house shook beneath us, I would have given much to repeat them once more.

Halvek controlled the apparatus deeper within the installation. Sera believed he was deliberately altering its resonance. We had to stop him and disable the Minor Resonator, but we could not destroy the essential observations or abandon people held within reach of the danger.

The route inward passed through chambers where the sound of the device seemed to gather in the stone. At times I felt a pressure behind my ears, as though a distant noise were almost becoming a voice. I told myself that fear was finding patterns in the hum of machinery. I had no evidence for anything else.

We met defenders before we reached Halvek. Some were armed and purposeful. Others moved with an unnatural urgency that made me hesitate before deciding how to respond. I had been told that the apparatus could alter behaviour; I had not been told that every person near it had chosen to be there.

I fought to open the route, not to prove that everything inside was an enemy. Where people could be brought clear, we brought them clear. Where the structure threatened to fail, we withdrew and approached by the prepared way.

Halvek did not surrender the apparatus. He fought to keep control of it, and the struggle carried us into the chamber where the Resonator's housing stood among its supports and instruments.

The noise changed as the device responded. The readings climbed. For one terrible moment I could not tell whether the shaking came from the machinery or from my own hands.

Sera called the limits aloud.

“Use the physical cutoff,” she said. “Retreat if the instruments exceed them. There is no glory in an uncontrolled collapse.”

I reached the prepared mechanism and followed the sequence we had rehearsed. The isolators took the load separately. The cutoff answered. The signal faltered, recovered for an instant, then fell silent.

The sudden absence of the sound was more frightening than the noise had been.

I stood waiting for it to return.

It did not.

Halvek had been defeated. The Minor Resonator was disabled. The essential measurements survived, along with those captives we had been able to bring to safety.

Sera checked the instruments before she spoke.

“What we recovered matters more than the machinery we broke.”

I wanted to believe that meant the danger was over. Instead I looked at the empty space where the signal had been and wondered how many other places might contain something built to do the same work.

The Grey Surveyor examined the final readings. He did not celebrate. He asked that the record include every limit we had encountered, every point at which our understanding had failed, and the fact that a controlled shutdown here proved nothing about the safety of other installations.

When I left the chamber, I felt as though I had crossed a threshold I could not name.

I had come to Northwatch following stolen cargo. I had found a machine that could disturb something deeper than a supply chain. I did not know who had conceived it, how far the design had travelled or what purpose its makers ultimately served.

I knew only that someone had built it deliberately.

***

The coast did not fall quiet because the signal had stopped.

At Northwatch, Nessa was already organising a defence. Dominion forces were attacking the landing, and the wounded needed to be moved before the fighting reached the supply stores.

I asked whether the attackers were connected to the apparatus.

“We have no evidence of that,” she said. “Sometimes an enemy patrol is simply an enemy patrol.”

The words brought me back to the road outside Millhaven. I had been tempted then to make every misfortune part of the same plot. The temptation was stronger now that I had seen a device I could barely understand.

But the Dominion attack was real on its own terms. Its soldiers fought for positions and supplies. Covenant defenders fought to keep the landing. Neither battle nor injury became less real because another hidden operation had taken place nearby.

I helped carry the wounded clear, secured supplies and held the agreed defensive position until the immediate danger passed. When the evacuation was complete, Nessa looked across the damaged landing.

“We can grieve the losses without inventing a hidden hand behind them,” she said.

I had seen enough to know how difficult that would be.

The report returned with me to Tidehold. Mara compared the recovered measurements with the cargo records, then had the technical evidence authenticated separately. The procurement trail pointed toward an Aelari material reference, but she refused to turn the reference into an accusation against the Aelari.

“That's a lead,” she said, “not a verdict.”

“Where do we go next?”

“The engineering contract passed through Kar Durn. Start with the people who made the original drawings.”

I thought of Brynja's instructions, precise enough to save us and limited enough to admit what she did not know. If the apparatus had been built from altered specifications, the original engineer might be the only person able to show where the change began.

Before leaving again, I returned to Millhaven.

Anwen had been comparing her patients' recovery records with observations from Oren. The unusual healing pattern I had seen months earlier had not vanished. It had been recorded carefully, and a local ward needed to be made safe while the investigation continued.

“Is it related to the signal station?” I asked.

“We don't know,” she said. “Healing isn't evidence of a conspiracy, and I won't frighten patients with guesses.”

I helped secure the ward and carry the observations into order. There was a real benefit in what Anwen had seen. There was also deliberate interference at the signal house. I could not yet explain the relationship between them, and I refused to make one up merely because both involved things I did not understand.

When the records were filed, Anwen closed her ledger.

“Whatever this is, it deserves careful study.”

Outside, Millhaven continued its work. Wagons arrived. Darian's patrol passed along the road. Brenna counted supplies. The village had survived my absence without waiting for me to explain the world.

I had followed a theft to a court, a court to a harbour, a harbour to a hostile coast, and a hostile coast to a machine whose silence had frightened me more than its noise.

Now the trail led into the mountains, toward the people who had designed the fittings that made such machinery possible.

I set out for Kar Durn carrying the measurements, the authenticated procurement records and the memory of the Grey Surveyor's careful questions.

There was something comforting in the thought that a structure, however strange, must still have been made by someone.

There was something terrible in it, too.


### Chapter Seven — No Fault in the Drawing

I had imagined the mountains as a boundary, a place where the road would simply rise until the rest of the world fell away beneath it. Instead they seemed to gather the world into themselves. Roads followed narrow cuts between stone shoulders; caravans moved at the pace of their heaviest wagons; every bridge and retaining wall bore evidence of someone having calculated what the mountain might permit.

I travelled with the engineering records wrapped against the weather. The papers were dry when I checked them, but the edges had begun to fray from my handling. I had read Brynja's preliminary safety instructions so often that I could almost recite them. They had helped us shut down the signal at Northwatch. Now I wanted to know whether their author could tell us who had altered the design and why.

Garrick had given me the original contract docket before I left.

“Brynja's name appears on the contract,” he had said. “That doesn't make her responsible for every alteration made after it left her workshop.”

“Will she speak to an outsider?”

“Bring the original docket and ask. Dwarven engineers tend to respect a well-kept record.”

I remembered his words when Kar Durn first appeared ahead of me. It was a city shaped by labour as much as by stone: approaches strengthened against falling rock, entrances measured for heavy loads, and the persistent sound of work carried through passages where I could not see its source. I had come from a harbour shared by four peoples and a capital governed by a Human king. Here I was entering a hold whose people had their own duties, laws and reasons to distrust a stranger with questions about their craft.

I was directed through the proper offices before I reached Brynja. By then I had learned not to resent the delay. Permission was not a ritual performed to inconvenience me. It was the way a community decided who could handle work that might injure its people.

Brynja received the docket with little ceremony. She laid my copied specification beside her original drawings and looked at them for a long time.

“That drawing is mine,” she said at last. She touched a line of measurements on the revised sheet. “The changed measurements are not.”

I leaned closer. The alteration was so small that I would have missed it even after the signal house. The lines still met. The fittings still appeared capable of bearing their assigned load.

“Someone copied my seal onto a revision I never approved,” she continued.

“Can you prove the difference?”

“Of course.”

She opened a bound workshop record and showed me her calculations, the dated inspection marks and the original arrangement. It took her several explanations before I understood that the altered specification had not simply weakened a component. It had changed the conditions under which the component would be expected to work. A structure might survive ordinary use and still behave disastrously when placed within a different system.

“Whoever altered it understood enough to make the change look ordinary,” she said.

I thought of Rusk's insistence upon particular crates, the impossible dates in Stoneford, and the signal house whose strange workings had been assembled from things that could each be described in familiar terms.

“Then it was deliberate?”

“The revision was not mine. Its effect is not accidental. I won't name its author without evidence.”

There it was again: the refusal to let a strong conclusion become a larger one merely because I wanted it to.

Brynja explained that a measuring assembly could help establish the difference between the original specification and the substituted one. But the assembly lay within a part of the hold to which I had no independent right of entry. We would need the hold's permission and a safe route.

I asked whether I could go at once.

“You may ask,” she said. “That is not the same as being allowed.”

***

Rurik met me beside a maintenance passage, his expression dark with worry. A feeder gallery had been blocked and his crew was trapped beyond the obstruction. He did not seem interested in the records I carried.

“I don't care whose grand investigation brought you here,” he said. “Those workers need help.”

“I'll get them out.”

“Follow the maintenance markings. Don't touch a load-bearing brace unless you know what it does.”

The gallery was hot and close. I could hear water moving somewhere behind the stone and the uneven knocking of tools farther along the passage. The obstruction had made the route dangerous, but the evidence around it pointed to a neglected pressure fault and missed maintenance, not to the elaborate alterations Brynja had shown me.

For a moment I was disappointed by that ordinary explanation. The feeling shamed me almost immediately. Workers were trapped. Whether the cause was a secret enemy or a failure to maintain a passage made no difference to the people waiting beyond it.

I followed Rurik's markings, kept clear of the bracing he had warned me about and helped restore a route through which the crew could return. One worker emerged furious that the inspection had been delayed. Another said nothing until they were safely outside, then sat with their back against the wall and stared at their hands.

Rurik counted them before speaking to me.

“The crew is safe,” he said. “We can argue about the cause after they've had a meal.”

I stayed long enough to help return the equipment and record the pressure fault accurately. The gallery needed repair. No amount of speculation about the signal house would repair it.

The experience changed how I read Brynja's drawings. I had been looking at lines and numbers as clues in an investigation. Rurik had shown me what happened when a number became a support, a support became a passage, and people trusted that passage to carry them home.

***

The measuring assembly could not be released until another matter was settled. Grain had gone missing between two legitimate orders, and the hold's trade officer had little patience for outsiders who wanted technical equipment while ordinary deliveries remained disputed.

“Brynja says you can read a ledger without blaming the nearest porter,” the officer told me.

“I can try.”

“Good. Trade disputes don't improve when everyone reaches for a sword.”

The records were familiar in form, though not in detail. I compared the dockets, checked the receipt sequence and traced the shipment to the point at which two valid instructions had been treated as though they described a single delivery. No hidden enemy was needed to explain the shortage. The grain had not vanished into an Anchor network; it had been caught between competing claims that could be reconciled.

The officer reviewed the corrected account.

“The deliveries are reconciled,” they said. “The hold can release the engineering report.”

Only then did Brynja receive the limited authority needed to recover and inspect the assembly. She made me read the permission carefully. It named the work to be done and the people responsible. It did not authorise me to explore every chamber I passed.

When we compared the recovered measurements with the original drawing, the alteration became harder to dismiss. Brynja showed me where a broker's substituted specification diverged from the approved design. The change had been concealed within otherwise legitimate work.

“Someone knew enough to borrow the appearance of safety,” I said.

Brynja gave a short nod.

“And enough to rely on other people trusting my seal.”

I wondered how many hands the revised drawing had passed through. The people who cut the metal might have done exactly what they were instructed to do. The people who transported it might have seen nothing unusual. Even the people who installed it might have believed they were completing an ordinary contract.

The danger lay not in a single dishonest craftsman, but in a chain of work whose parts could be individually respectable and collectively misused.

I thought of the Grey Surveyor's insistence that a structure's limits mattered as much as its apparent success. Brynja had arrived at the same warning from another direction.

She prepared an authenticated engineering report. It established the discrepancy without claiming knowledge we did not possess. When she finished, she closed the original drawings and rested her hands upon them for a moment.

“People will ask whether my work caused this,” she said.

“What will you tell them?”

“That my work was changed. And that I intend to find out who benefited.”

***

The High King received the report through the hold's proper channels. I was nervous in a way I had not been before Aldren, perhaps because I had begun to understand how much could be lost when a ruler accepted a convenient explanation.

The High King read Brynja's findings and listened to the account of the altered contract. He did not treat the evidence as permission to demand answers from another sovereign people.

“Brynja's evidence warrants further inquiry,” he said, “but my word does not command Sylvaren. I can offer an introduction, nothing more.”

“That is enough,” I replied.

“Then carry my letter intact. Let the Aelari decide what they will share.”

He placed his seal upon the introduction. It was a different sort of seal from the broken one I had recovered near Millhaven. Yet I had learned not to trust a mark simply because it was well made. Its value lay in the authority honestly exercised behind it and the limits openly stated upon the page.

I left Kar Durn with the authenticated report, the introduction and a respect for its people that had little to do with the grandeur of their halls. Brynja's precision, Rurik's concern for his trapped crew and the trade officer's determination to settle an ordinary dispute had each taught me something about the responsibilities of a hold.

The route to Sylvaren took me away from the hard edges of the mountains and toward a landscape whose care I did not yet understand. I had heard descriptions of the Aelari capital, but I distrusted them by then. Every city I had visited had proved more complicated than the stories told about it.

Caelith received the High King's letter and Brynja's report. She examined both before speaking.

“The High King's seal is genuine, and Brynja's findings deserve examination. Neither gives you authority over our wards or records.”

“I'm asking you to review the evidence, not to surrender your archives.”

“Then I will register the report and the letter. If you seek access beyond this audience, we will discuss its limits separately.”

I felt relief at having reached the next part of the investigation. I also felt the familiar impatience of standing before another boundary.

“Why do the ward records matter?” she asked.

“We found altered designs elsewhere. We need to know whether the same materials were used here.”

“Then show me the originals and the changes. I will grant limited access if the evidence warrants it.”

I gave her the comparison Brynja had prepared. Caelith studied the lines with the same concentration the Dwarven engineer had shown, though she asked different questions: not only what the component had been designed to bear, but what it might disturb around it.

At length she agreed that the specified wards could be examined under supervision.

“Do not confuse permission with ownership,” she said.

I promised that I would not.

Outside the meeting place, I paused beneath the unfamiliar sounds of Sylvaren. I had followed the trail from a broken wagon to a royal court, from a harbour to a signal house, and from a signal house into the workshops of Kar Durn. Every step had shown me how a small alteration could travel through honest hands until it became part of something dangerous.

Now I stood among people whose work concerned living boundaries as much as constructed ones.

I wondered what might happen when a hidden design was carried into a place that had never consented to bear it.

I had come to Sylvaren asking for records.

Before long, I would have to learn what those records were meant to protect.


### Chapter Eight — What the Queen Will Share

I had imagined Sylvaren's wards as walls.

Perhaps that was because every other place I had visited had taught me to recognise protection in familiar forms: a gate at Millhaven, a guard at Valecrown, a reinforced gallery in Kar Durn. I knew how a wall could fail. I knew how a bridge could bear more weight than its makers intended. I knew how a seal could be copied and made to lend false authority to a dangerous order.

Caelith took me along a woodland path and showed me that protection could be harder to recognise.

There were no battlements where she stopped. A maintained boundary passed between living growth and carefully tended ground. Its purpose was not to forbid every crossing. It was to allow certain work to continue without exposing the people and places around it to risks they had not accepted.

“The ward tenders know this route,” she said. “You do not. Listen before you decide what needs changing.”

The first task she gave me had little to do with the altered designs I had carried from Kar Durn. A nursery route had been damaged, and workers were stranded beyond a stretch of neglected branch-ward where local fauna had become a danger.

“Is this part of the sabotage?” I asked.

“We have no reason to believe so. The forest has problems that exist without your investigation.”

I heard a trace of impatience in her voice, though it might have been my own embarrassment reflected back at me. I had spent so long looking for a common hand behind the stolen cargo that I had begun to expect it everywhere.

The route narrowed beneath branches that had grown across the maintenance markers. I followed the tenders' instructions, cleared the immediate danger and helped the workers return without disturbing the surrounding ward more than necessary. One of them insisted upon checking the nursery before accepting assistance for an injured arm. I waited while Caelith examined the boundary and confirmed that the workers' efforts had not been lost.

“The tenders are safe,” she told me afterward. “The nursery can recover.”

I watched the relief pass between the workers. No one asked whether their rescue would help uncover the mystery that had brought me to Sylvaren. It mattered because they were alive and their work could continue.

Only then did Caelith permit me to accompany her to the abandoned research ward.

***

She placed two records before me. One showed the ward as it had been approved; the other documented what remained in the field.

“Tell me what changed,” she said.

At first the differences seemed slight. A position altered. A measure adjusted. A relationship between parts that would have appeared harmless to anyone unfamiliar with the whole. I had seen the same deceptive neatness in Brynja's substituted drawing.

“The alterations resemble the other sites,” I said.

“Resemblance is not proof. Check the maintenance register as well.”

The register took longer to read than the design. Entries had been made by different hands at different times. Some recorded routine care, some repairs, and some the absence of work that ought to have been done. I had to separate neglect from intervention before I could identify what did not belong.

We compared the approved baseline with the altered ward and the surviving record of its maintenance. The differences were deliberate. They had not been authorised by the people responsible for the site.

Caelith read the conclusion twice.

“The changes were deliberate and unauthorised,” she said. “That I can attest.”

“Do you know who made them?”

“No. And I will not turn the uncertainty into an accusation because the answer would be convenient.”

She looked toward the living boundary beyond the research site.

“People will hear of this and begin to suspect every damaged branch-ward, every failed nursery, every frightened animal. We cannot let one real interference make us careless about everything else.”

I remembered Joss at Greyfall Quarry, counting workers while I searched for signs of a hidden enemy. I remembered Nessa refusing to call an ordinary Dominion attack part of the signal operation without evidence. The lesson had followed me across kingdoms and coasts. I wondered how many times I would need to learn it.

Mara had arrived in Sylvaren to examine the correspondence surrounding the restricted observations. She found me bent over the maintenance records and asked what Caelith had established.

“Unauthorised alteration,” I said. “Not a name.”

“Good. Keep those separate.”

The name we needed might lie with Veylan.

***

I expected Veylan to be defiant.

His correspondence showed that restricted observations had been sold or passed beyond their proper custody. Some of the letters carried the bitterness of a person who believed his work had been overlooked. I had read them before meeting him, and by the time I entered the authorised interview I had already begun to imagine the words he would use to justify himself.

Mara noticed.

“Speak to him before you decide he sold them willingly,” she said.

“What if he refuses?”

“Then document the refusal. We don't manufacture testimony to improve a case.”

Veylan was quieter than I expected. He did not deny the existence of the correspondence. He challenged the way it had been read.

“You see a sale,” he said. “I see years of work judged by people who never had to prove what I proved.”

“Did you release the observations?”

He looked away.

“I believed the work deserved to be seen.”

“That isn't the same as permission.”

“No.”

For a moment I thought he might say more. I waited, remembering the witness in Valecrown and Mara's insistence that silence should not be filled by the investigator.

Veylan spoke of resentment, of doors closed to him, of people who had praised his skill while refusing the conclusions he wanted them to accept. His grievance was real to him. So was the concealment shown by the letters.

I could understand the first without excusing the second.

Under Aelari authority, his statement was recorded and compared with the correspondence. Some details matched independently. Others remained his account alone. We did not need to decide whether he was a good man or a bad one to establish what he had done.

When we left, Mara held the authenticated copy beside the original letters.

“His account and the correspondence corroborate one another,” she said. “We can take this to the queen.”

I looked back toward the room where Veylan remained.

“Do you think he knew what his observations would be used for?”

“I think we should write down what he knew, and leave the rest open until we can prove it.”

I wanted a simpler answer. By then I no longer expected one.

***

Queen Seralyth received the findings in a chamber whose quiet made me more conscious of my own footsteps. I had carried a Human king's commission into Tidehold, a Dwarven High King's introduction into Sylvaren, and now an Aelari record before its sovereign. Each document had opened a conversation. None had granted me ownership of what I sought.

Seralyth listened while Caelith presented the ward comparison and Mara explained the recovered correspondence. I described the signal house at Northwatch, the controlled shutdown and the engineering differences Brynja had authenticated.

The queen asked about the limits of our measurements. She wanted to know which records were original, which were copies, and which conclusions depended upon testimony that could not be repeated.

I was grateful for the questions. They meant she was treating the evidence as something to be tested rather than as a story designed to win her favour.

At last she spoke.

“You have brought evidence rather than demands. I will release a limited inspection reference.”

She explained that the reference concerned a Dominion site. It did not authorise diplomacy with Dominion officers, nor did it open unrelated Aelari research to outsiders.

“We can investigate without their cooperation,” I said.

“Then understand the risk. They will see an intruder, not an invited scholar.”

I thought of the disciplined defenders on Rukhan's coast. They had not known why I watched their posts. They had only known that an enemy was near.

“Caelith will prepare the permitted copy,” Seralyth continued. “Do not expose our unrelated research.”

I accepted the conditions. The sample and the limited reference were not trophies. They were entrusted evidence, given for a purpose the queen had defined.

Outside the audience, Caelith secured the permitted material and checked the custody record.

“Does this make you part of our investigation?” I asked.

“It makes me responsible for what I have agreed to share,” she replied. “The rest of my work remains here.”

I had wanted her help because she understood the wards. I was beginning to value it for another reason: she would not allow my urgency to become authority over her people.

***

The return to Tidehold felt shorter than the first journey, though I knew the roads had not changed. I had travelled them with uncertainty before; now I carried more evidence and a sharper awareness of what it did not prove.

Nessa met us at the harbour. She read the coastal reference and the proposed extraction arrangements without showing surprise.

“We're returning to Northwatch for one document,” she said. “This is a hostile infiltration, not an embassy.”

“What's our extraction route?”

“The coastal path and the next authorised ship. If you lose contact, return to the agreed rally point.”

I asked whether there was any chance the Dominion engineers would speak with us.

“Not across this frontier,” Sera answered. “Recover their written findings. Don't approach Dominion authorities as allies.”

The inspection copy we needed had been prepared by Ilyss, a Vaelari engineer who had rejected a component before it was installed. The rejection mattered. It meant someone on the Dominion side had recognised that an alteration was dangerous, even if the two alliances had never shared the warning.

We approached the military observation post by a guarded route, keeping clear of civilian dwellings. The defenders were not waiting to help us. Their orders and ours were opposed. I could not pretend that recovering a useful document made the intrusion friendly.

Inside, I found the inspection copy among the post's records. The rejection was precise, its concerns technical rather than political. The engineer had identified a fault in the altered component and refused to approve it.

I thought of Brynja's anger at the misuse of her seal. Here, across a hostile frontier, another engineer had refused to accept dangerous work. The similarity did not make our peoples allies. It made the evidence harder to dismiss.

We withdrew by the agreed route and returned to the landing with the copy intact.

Sera examined it beside the earlier measurements.

“The report confirms the same pattern,” she said. “The people who rejected it may never know their evidence helped us.”

I wondered whether Ilyss would have believed me if I had been able to tell her. Perhaps she would have regarded the report as proof that her warning had been justified. Perhaps she would have seen only an enemy holding a document taken from a military post. I had no right to decide what she would think.

The next lead came from Mara's field agent. A courier was moving to destroy cached instructions that connected the altered materials to their route of distribution. We had already lodged a duplicate of the essential evidence, but the remaining archive might reveal more.

“The courier is destroying the cache,” the agent told me. “Intercept them before the archive burns.”

“Do we need the courier alive?”

“If it can be done safely, yes. But the record is essential.”

We reached the cache amid the smell of smoke and scorched paper. I had no time to save everything. I secured what could be recovered and made sure the surviving fragment was recorded against the duplicate already lodged elsewhere. The courier's attempt to erase the trail did not erase the evidence we had protected in advance.

The agent studied the damaged material.

“The fragment is enough to establish the route,” they said. “The rest must be corroborated.”

I was tired of that word, *corroborated*, and ashamed of being tired of it. Each time we had preserved an independent copy, the truth had become a little harder for someone else to destroy.

***

Mara spread the Dwarven drawings, Aelari ward records and Vaelari inspection copy across a table at Tidehold.

Three peoples. Different institutions. Different forms of authority. The same method of alteration.

I watched her compare the records without allowing any one of them to explain more than it contained. The Dwarven evidence established a substituted specification. The Aelari records showed deliberate changes to a protected ward. The Vaelari inspection proved that an engineer on the opposing side had recognised and rejected a dangerous component.

The courier's fragment helped connect the route by which the altered instructions had travelled.

“Are the Korrath responsible?” I asked.

Mara shook her head.

“Not on this evidence. Their older engineering may be what someone is copying.”

“Then we need to speak to them.”

“Yes. And we will ask them to judge the methods themselves.”

She prepared sealed copies for the next inquiry. We had found common suppression across different records, not proof that every office, every workshop or every people had been infiltrated. That distinction mattered. The world was already divided by war. We did not need to make every neighbour into a suspect as well.

Drom Kar would be our next destination.

I stood beside the table after Mara left, looking at the documents we had carried from one place to another. Each represented someone who had worked carefully enough for a record to survive: Brynja preserving her calculations, Caelith maintaining the ward baseline, Ilyss rejecting a dangerous component, the field agent lodging a duplicate before the cache could burn.

None of them had seen the whole pattern. Neither had I.

I had once believed the truth would be waiting at the end of the road, complete and unmistakable. Instead it seemed to be something people built together, one honest account at a time, while others tried to break the connections between them.

Beyond Tidehold, the ships were preparing to sail. The war continued. The altered designs had not ceased to exist because we had discovered them. Somewhere, people were still putting those designs to work.

I gathered the sealed copies and went to find Mara.

The next road would lead us to the Korrath, and to questions older than the contracts we had been following.

For the first time, I wondered whether the danger we were uncovering had begun long before any of us had been born.


### Chapter Nine — Water and Memory

The first thing I heard in Drom Kar was water.

Not the gentle sound of a stream passing through a valley, but a deep and patient movement beneath stone, carried through channels older than any road I had travelled. It was joined by the creak of freight tackle, the ring of tools and the calls of workers whose voices rose and fell between the walls. The city seemed to have learned to live with the weight of the mountains and the persistence of the water, neither pretending it could command the other.

Mara had given me sealed copies of the Dwarven drawings, Aelari ward records and Vaelari inspection. They had travelled with me across roads and sea routes, protected from rain and curious hands. I had become so accustomed to their presence that I felt their weight even when I set them down.

Urodan received me without treating the papers as a passport to the city's past.

“You have brought an inquiry,” he said after examining the seals. “That does not make our archives yours.”

“I came to ask what your people know about the methods in these drawings.”

“Then you will begin by learning what we must protect.”

He spoke of custodial duties, the maintenance routes that served the city and the records whose value depended upon their context. A damaged page could mislead as readily as a forged one. A diagram separated from the circumstances of its making could be turned into permission for work its authors had never intended.

I had heard variations of this warning in every place I had visited. Here it seemed to be built into the city itself.

Urodan gave me a work assignment connected to the existing water route. Completing it would allow us to recover records endangered in a maintenance store. It would not, he made clear, give me unrestricted archive access.

I accepted.

Tarek met me near the route entrance. He knew the channels and their service passages, and he spoke of them with the same practical concern Darian had once shown for the western bend outside Millhaven.

“The water does not stop because a passage is inconvenient,” he told me. “Neither do the people whose work depends upon it.”

We checked the maintenance markings, secured the route and reached the endangered store. A crew had been working to protect the records, and the danger to them was immediate enough that I stopped thinking about what the archive might reveal. We helped them withdraw safely before handling the material they had been trying to save.

The records were damp at their edges. I watched Tarek and the custodians separate what could be moved from what needed support where it lay. It was careful, unglamorous work. I had seen people rush toward evidence as though the truth could be seized by force. Here, saving a page meant knowing when not to touch it.

Among the recovered material was a warning whose significance had been obscured by omission. Its surviving context suggested that an earlier account had been copied without a crucial qualification.

I thought of the altered drawings and the false sequence in Valecrown. There was something especially dangerous about a record that appeared complete.

Dromak examined the warning with me. He required the original and the omitted margin to be authenticated independently before he would permit a restricted comparison.

“Who removed it?” I asked.

“That is a question,” he said. “Not an answer hidden in the damage.”

I had been ready to imagine a name. The missing words invited suspicion. Yet the omission itself could be established without deciding who had caused it.

When the evidence had been checked, Dromak granted the limited comparison. He did not offer a definitive judgment on the older grievances surrounding the Vorrath exile. The records before us could not carry such a verdict.

I was beginning to understand how a people might protect its history without agreeing on every part of it.

***

Sera arrived after the archival work had begun. She read the recovered warning, then asked me to walk with her beyond the busiest part of the freight works.

“There is something you should know about the people helping us,” she said.

I waited. I had learned that Sera rarely began a conversation this way unless the next words mattered.

She spoke of the Guardians: an order whose origins crossed the boundaries of the peoples now divided by alliances and war. Its work had been hidden, sometimes necessarily, sometimes at a cost that secrecy itself made difficult to measure.

I had met people who offered assistance without telling me why they possessed such knowledge. I thought of the Grey Surveyor, of his insistence upon limits and his unwillingness to turn a successful experiment into a universal claim.

“Why tell me now?” I asked.

“Because you have been asked to trust our instructions,” Sera said. “You deserve to know that those instructions come from people with obligations, not from an authority beyond question.”

She showed me what could be shared of the order's charter. I read it slowly. The words described cooperation and responsibilities that could not simply be imposed upon a stranger.

“You can work with us as an associate,” she said. “That is not a demand that you join.”

I thought of the Human warrant in my pack, the High King's introduction, and the limited access granted in Sylvaren. Each had been a means of doing necessary work without claiming another people's allegiance.

“I'll help,” I said. “But I want to understand what I'm being asked to do.”

Sera's expression softened.

“So do I.”

***

The Grey Surveyor was waiting beside a set of plans when we returned.

One drawing showed the arrangement of a Regional Anchor. Another contained Halden's proposed shutdown order. At first I could not understand why the Surveyor regarded the second with such unease. It appeared straightforward: remove the source of danger and the danger would end.

He placed a finger beside a missing part of the instructions.

“What happens to the load when the core is disconnected?”

I studied the diagram.

“It stops?”

“That is what the order assumes.”

“And what do you think happens?”

“I think the order does not account for what the supports are carrying.”

His answer frustrated me. I wanted him to tell me exactly what would fail. Instead he asked me to compare the plans, identify what had not been measured and find the missing stability material.

I remembered Brynja's changed specifications. The failure of a structure was not always located where the break first appeared. Sometimes it began with an assumption made long before anyone touched the machinery.

Sera read Halden's instructions and went still.

“He's preparing to destroy the installation.”

“Wouldn't that be safer?” I asked.

“Not if the disconnection causes the failure we're trying to prevent.”

We went to the freight works at once.

I remember the urgency of the journey more than the route. The sound of water followed us through the passages. Every worker we passed seemed to be carrying out an ordinary task in a place that might soon become dangerous for reasons they could not see.

Halden's team had prepared a charge. He was not a fool, and he was not indifferent to the lives around him. He believed destroying the Anchor was the responsible choice.

Sera confronted him before the attempt could begin.

“You haven't accounted for the supports,” she said.

“I have accounted for what happens if we leave the core operating.”

“And not for what happens when you break its connection.”

Their disagreement was not between courage and cowardice. Both feared the consequences of inaction. Both had reasons to distrust the other approach. The problem was that one of them had reached the point of acting before the necessary measurement had been made.

I showed Halden the missing stability information. The Grey Surveyor explained the untested load path without pretending the complete answer was already known.

For a moment Halden looked at the charge as though it had become something unfamiliar.

Then he ordered the premature attempt stopped.

I felt relief so sharply that my knees seemed weak. It lasted only until Sera reminded us that stopping a dangerous plan was not the same as making the installation safe.

We still had to enter.

***

The freight works were louder within than they had seemed from outside. Machinery passed its strain through the stone. The Regional Anchor stood at the centre of a system of supports and controls whose relationships I could barely follow even after studying the diagrams.

Halden's people were inside. Some were trapped beyond a damaged section. Others were trying to keep the equipment stable long enough for the crew to withdraw.

I had imagined the task as a confrontation with a machine. It became a series of decisions about people.

We reached the researchers first, opening a safe route and helping them clear the threatened chambers. I could hear Sera calling measurements as the Grey Surveyor checked the isolation sequence. Halden worked beside the team he had nearly endangered, no longer arguing that the core could simply be destroyed.

The apparatus shuddered when we began separating its supports. For an instant a change in the readings made me think the whole structure was failing. Sera ordered us to hold the sequence rather than improvise.

I kept my hands where I had been told to keep them.

That small act felt more difficult than any blow I had struck in the passage beneath Valecrown. There, an enemy had moved against me and I had moved in return. Here the danger invited action without telling me which action would make it worse.

The isolation held. The supports were secured. Only then did we proceed to the core shutdown.

When the machinery fell quiet, I did not cheer. I looked toward the passage through which the last of Halden's team had escaped and waited until someone confirmed that every person accounted for had reached safety.

Halden stood near the inactive controls, staring at the place where his charge would have been set.

“I thought I was preventing a disaster,” he said.

Sera did not answer immediately.

“You were trying to,” she said at last. “Next time, the warning has to reach everyone before the decision does.”

I thought of the missing margin in Dromak's records. An omitted qualification had travelled across years. An incomplete instruction had nearly become an explosion. The past and the present were joined by more than the shape of the machinery.

They were joined by the consequences of what people chose not to share.

***

The Grey Surveyor insisted that the full safety instructions be copied and given to the people who would continue working near the installation. A second set of measurements was lodged independently.

“Why so many copies?” I asked.

“Because the people at risk should not have to rely on one person remembering correctly.”

I wondered whether he was speaking only about the Anchor.

Brynja's experience at Kar Durn had taught me how a legitimate drawing could be altered after leaving its maker. Drom Kar taught me another danger: knowledge kept too narrowly could become useless at the moment someone most needed it.

The next proposal was smaller and more cautious. Instead of attempting another large intervention, the Grey Surveyor and Brynja's technical guidance would support a fully isolated trial using a Minor Resonator. The instruments, reserve limits and physical cutoff had to be checked before anyone authorised the experiment.

I helped prepare the apparatus. I had seen enough to distrust the words *small* and *safe* when spoken together, but the scale of the trial was deliberately bounded. Its purpose was to learn what a brief reversal might do, not to prove a solution to every Anchor.

When the preparations were complete, Sera reviewed the limits and authorised the test.

The reversal lasted only as long as the measured threshold allowed. The instruments recorded a change. At the agreed point we activated the cutoff, and the apparatus returned to its isolated state.

I waited for someone to explain what we had accomplished.

The Grey Surveyor studied the results.

“We have shown that a controlled reversal is possible under these conditions,” he said. “We have not shown that it will remain safe at a greater scale.”

“Then it might work?”

“It might. That is why we measure.”

The answer should have disappointed me. Instead I felt something like hope, though it was a cautious and uncomfortable kind.

Halden remained unconvinced that reversal was the safer answer. He believed severance could still offer a more dependable way to remove a dangerous working. Sera argued that cutting a connection without understanding its load might reproduce the very failure we had narrowly avoided.

They asked what I thought.

I considered the people we had rescued, the incomplete shutdown order and the brief reversal that had ended within its limits. I told them I favoured continuing to investigate bounded inversion, provided every trial remained subject to a safe cutoff.

Halden accepted that I had heard his argument. Sera recorded my preference as provisional, not as a binding choice for every decision to come.

I was grateful. I had begun the journey believing that good judgment meant reaching certainty. Now I was learning that a decision could be responsible precisely because it remained open to better evidence.

That evening I returned to the water channels. The city had resumed its work. Freight moved. Maintenance crews checked their routes. Somewhere beyond the public passages, custodians preserved records whose authors had never imagined that a stranger from Millhaven would one day need them.

I stood beside the moving water and thought about the missing margin, the copied instructions and the Surveyor's insistence that knowledge should reach those who bore its risks.

For the first time, I began to suspect that the danger we were following was not only a matter of machines altered in secret.

It was also a matter of who had been permitted to know how they worked, who had been trusted to question them, and who had been left to face their consequences without warning.

The water continued beneath the city.

I listened until I could no longer distinguish its sound from the distant machinery, and then went back to the others.


### Chapter Ten — The Harbinger's Design

The first pattern appeared because I had stopped looking for one.

For days we had been comparing the readings recovered from the signal house, the freight works and the smaller experiments conducted under the Grey Surveyor's supervision. Each set had its own uncertainties. Instruments had been built by different hands, measurements taken under different conditions, and the records had survived journeys that left their edges worn and their labels in need of careful checking.

I had expected another discrepancy, another date that did not fit, another altered drawing whose purpose could be established only after someone found the original.

Instead the readings began to agree.

Not in their magnitude, nor in the ordinary rise and fall of the instruments. Their agreement lay in the spacing of disturbances, in relationships that became visible only when the measurements were aligned against the same interval. Three signatures formed the beginning of a repeated arrangement. When we compared the remaining records, the arrangement persisted.

Sera stood beside the table while I shifted the last tracing into place.

“That's six,” I said.

“Six observed signatures,” she replied. “Don't give them a meaning we haven't established.”

I looked at the aligned marks. They seemed deliberate. That was the most I could honestly say.

The Grey Surveyor studied the comparison without speaking. His silence had become familiar, but this time it unsettled me. He had taught us to distinguish a useful result from a complete explanation. Now I wondered whether he was recognising something he had hoped never to see.

“Do they belong to the same system?” I asked.

“The readings support coordination,” he said. “They do not yet tell us the whole mechanism.”

“Someone is building something larger than the machines we've found.”

He did not contradict me.

Outside, Drom Kar continued its work. Water moved beneath the stone. Freight passed along the established routes. I had come to trust those ordinary sounds, yet the pattern on the table made them seem suddenly fragile. The people carrying supplies through the city did not know what our instruments had found. Neither, in any complete sense, did we.

Mara examined the report and authorised the next inquiry. The coordinated disturbances pointed toward the mainland Chainworks and an installation whose defences were associated with the Harbinger.

I had heard that title before, always at a distance from the people who suffered its consequences. It belonged to someone whose orders moved through hidden facilities and guarded routes, someone who had learned to make others carry out a purpose they might not have chosen for themselves.

The name beneath the title was Oma'vira.

I did not yet understand how old that name was.

***

We approached the Chainworks as observers before we approached them as combatants.

The perimeter had been chosen carefully. Patrols covered the most obvious routes, and the positions supporting the installation overlapped without leaving easy gaps. The defenders were not a disorderly band guarding stolen goods. Their movements showed planning, discipline and a willingness to sacrifice convenience for control.

I watched from concealed ground while Sera recorded the patrol changes. We marked the defended approaches and identified the places where a retreat could be made without leading danger toward nearby workers.

The orders we recovered carried the Harbinger's authority. They described security and movement in language that made people sound like components of a machine. A person might be transferred, isolated, reinforced or made compliant. I read the words twice before I could bear to read them aloud.

“Are these soldiers?” I asked.

“Some are willing,” Sera said. “Some have been altered. We cannot assume every person inside chose to serve.”

The distinction troubled me more than the armed perimeter. I knew how to defend myself against someone trying to kill me. I did not know how to recognise the boundary between conviction and a will that had been forced into obedience.

The Grey Surveyor asked us to preserve the orders with their context. The records might explain the installation's purpose, but they could not tell us what every captive or defender had experienced.

We returned with the patrol map and the authenticated instructions. It was enough to plan a careful approach. It was not enough to decide that every life within the Chainworks had been forfeited.

***

The next records led us to a Passage schematic.

I had heard of the ancient network in histories and half-remembered accounts, usually as something whose great routes had failed in the catastrophe that divided the world. The diagram before us showed a more specific connection: a route associated with Oma'vira's installation and a destination within the Shroud.

I could scarcely believe what I was reading.

“Someone used a Passage route into the Shroud?”

Sera studied the authenticated markings.

“That is what this evidence indicates.”

“How? I thought the network was lost.”

“The great network was broken,” she said. “That doesn't answer every question about what survived or what was attempted afterward.”

She did not offer another route. She did not tell me that a gate remained available to us. The schematic established a destination, not a safe means of reaching it.

We compared Oma'vira's signature with the recovered instructions and the destination record. The connections held. The Harbinger had not merely guarded an Anchor installation. Her work joined control of minds, control of movement and the concealment of a route that should not have been easy to use.

I asked whether the Guardians had known.

Sera's expression changed, but her answer remained narrow.

“We will answer what the evidence permits us to answer. First we must stop what is happening here.”

I did not like it. I had begun to recognise the sound of a door being kept closed. Yet people were still inside the facility, and every hour spent arguing over what Sera would not say was an hour in which the conditioning apparatus continued its work.

We prepared to enter.

***

The first chamber was quieter than I expected.

I had imagined screams, machinery roaring and guards shouting orders. Instead I heard measured footsteps and a voice repeating instructions from somewhere beyond a partition. The words were ordinary enough to be mistaken for training. Their repetition made them terrible.

We found captives whose movements had been constrained by the workings around them. Some watched us with open fear. Others barely reacted when we approached. I could not tell whether their stillness was courage, exhaustion or something imposed upon them.

Sera identified the conditioning relays. We had to disable them without making the people dependent upon the system suffer a sudden uncontrolled failure. The plan required care, and I felt the familiar impatience of knowing that danger was present while being told not to rush toward it.

I followed the agreed sequence. We isolated the relays, cleared the route for the captives and protected them as the controls were disabled. Some could move immediately. Others needed help. No one demanded that they explain what had been done to them before being allowed to leave.

I remember one captive looking at me as though waiting for permission to speak.

“You can go,” I said.

They did not move.

Sera came beside me. Her voice was gentle, but she did not promise what she could not know.

“The route is open. We will help you reach it.”

The captive took a step. Then another.

I had thought the greatest cruelty of the installation would be visible in its instruments. Instead it lay in the hesitation of someone who had been taught not to trust their own choice.

We secured the evacuation before pressing deeper.

Oma'vira waited within the defended heart of the facility. I knew her by the authority with which the remaining workings answered her, and by the terrible composure of someone who had spent centuries refining a purpose.

She did not appear surprised that we had reached her.

“You have broken the instruments,” she said, looking toward the silenced relays. “You do not understand what they preserved.”

“I saw the people they held.”

“You saw what they could not bear without guidance.”

The words angered me. They also frightened me, because she spoke them without the hesitation of someone inventing an excuse. She believed her authority over other minds was justified by what she claimed to know.

I thought of the Grey Surveyor's warnings, of Sera's refusal to promise safety, and of the captives waiting at the exit. Every person who had helped me understand the danger had admitted limits. Oma'vira treated limits as defects in those who resisted her.

“You made their choices for them,” I said.

“I gave them a purpose beyond their fear.”

“They should have been allowed to refuse it.”

Her expression hardened. Whatever she had once been, she would not yield the work before her.

The confrontation was not clean or noble. The remaining defenders fought to protect the controls, and Oma'vira used the workings of the chamber to keep us from reaching her. I moved between the prepared safe positions, striking when an opening appeared and retreating when the apparatus threatened to turn the room itself against us.

I remember the noise of failing relays and the effort of keeping my attention on the people still making their way out. I remember Sera calling a warning before a field changed. I remember the Grey Surveyor's instructions, precise enough to follow even when fear made every movement seem urgent.

At last Oma'vira fell.

I waited for her to rise.

She did not.

The Harbinger was dead, and the facility's conditioning controls were disabled. The captives who had escaped remained alive beyond the danger. We preserved the Passage records and the authenticated evidence of her work.

I had expected to feel triumph at the death of someone who had caused so much suffering. Instead I felt the exhaustion of a struggle whose end could not return the stolen years to the people she had held.

Sera stood beside the recovered records.

“We will not let the account become simpler than what happened,” she said.

I looked toward the evacuation route.

“No,” I answered. “We won't.”

***

The captured Major Anchor remained a danger after Oma'vira's death.

We had proved a brief reversal possible in a fully isolated Minor Resonator. That did not make the larger working safe. Brynja and Sera reviewed the safeguards with the qualified specialists, checking the supports, the reserve limits and the physical cutoff before authorising a bounded inversion.

My task was not to perform the technical work. I helped defend the specialists and the cutoff while they carried out the procedure.

It was an uncomfortable kind of responsibility. I could see the people doing the most important work, but I could not take their place. I had to trust them to make judgments I did not fully understand, and they had to trust me to keep the route clear if the situation changed.

The inversion began.

For a moment the readings rose in a way that made me think of the signal house at Northwatch. Then the specialists adjusted the sequence within its measured limits. The Major Anchor's behaviour changed. The bounded reversal held long enough to be recorded, and the team brought the apparatus back under control.

No one declared the entire network defeated.

Maintenance duties were assigned. The measurements were copied. The result was significant because it had been achieved without pretending that success at one installation guaranteed safety at another.

Brynja studied the final figures.

“A larger working,” she said, “and still only one measured result.”

I smiled despite my exhaustion.

“I'm beginning to think that's the only sort of result you'll ever accept.”

“The sort we can defend,” she replied.

The Grey Surveyor looked at the records for a long time. I wondered what he saw in them that I could not.

***

The Passage evidence remained.

We copied the destination records and compared them with the surviving schematic. The route had pointed into the Shroud. Its associated gate was destroyed. We verified that state from independent evidence rather than treating the damaged structure as proof of everything that had happened to it.

I asked when it had been destroyed.

The records did not give us a complete account. We could establish that the gate no longer functioned; we could not honestly turn the evidence into a scene we had not witnessed.

Sera accepted the report.

“So Oma'vira had a way into the Shroud,” I said.

“She had access to a route whose destination lay there,” Sera replied.

“And now it's gone.”

“That gate is destroyed.”

I waited for her to say more.

She did not.

The silence between us felt different from the quiet after the Minor Resonator shut down. There, the absence of sound had marked a danger brought under control. Here it marked knowledge that someone was not yet prepared to share.

I thought of the Guardians' charter and the Grey Surveyor's insistence that people at risk deserved complete instructions. I thought of the captives who had been denied the freedom to choose, and of the people who had risked their lives to release them.

“Is there another way?” I asked.

Sera looked toward the sealed records.

“We need to talk about what the Guardians have kept.”

I felt anger rise, sharp and immediate. Then I remembered how often my anger had made a difficult truth seem simpler than it was.

I sat down opposite her.

“Then tell me,” I said.

Outside, the Chainworks had fallen quiet enough for the ordinary sounds of labour to return. Workers moved through the recovered approaches. The rescued captives were being cared for. The Major Anchor remained under guarded maintenance, its bounded reversal recorded but its future still uncertain.

I had come to the installation believing that killing the Harbinger might end the danger. Instead her death had opened a question older than the facility itself.

Someone had crossed into the Shroud.

Someone else had known enough about that crossing to fear what its discovery would mean.

And I was beginning to suspect that the Guardians' greatest secret was not what they had fought, but what they had preserved.


### Chapter Eleven — The Gate They Kept

The evidence from Oma'vira's facility was harder to carry than the records that had led us there.

I had brought drawings and ledgers across half the known roads of the Covenant. Now I carried an authenticated account of a destroyed Passage gate whose destination lay inside the Shroud, and of people who had been made to obey the Harbinger's will. I could not separate the two discoveries in my mind. The gate had been a means of movement; the conditioning had been a means of control. Both had served someone who believed other lives existed to be directed.

Sera asked me to bring the gate records to Drom Kar. We met in a quiet room where the sound of the city's water channels reached us only as a distant movement beneath the floor.

I laid out the copied destination marks and the evidence confirming the gate's destruction.

“This route led into the Shroud,” I said.

“Yes.”

“And Oma'vira used it.”

“The records support that.”

I waited for her to say more. She looked at the papers without touching them.

“How was access possible?” I asked.

Sera drew a slow breath.

“Not every Passage was lost in the same way.”

I felt the old anger rise. It was not the anger I had felt toward Rusk or Cassian, whose actions I could name. It was the anger of discovering that someone I had trusted had allowed me to act without knowing something that might have changed the risks I took.

“You knew there could be a way through.”

“I knew of a separate route,” she said. “Restricted, low-capacity, and kept under guard. It is not Oma'vira's destroyed gate.”

“Then why didn't you tell me?”

“Because it was not ours alone to disclose.”

I wanted to accept that as an answer. I also wanted to reject it as an excuse.

“You asked me to trust the Guardians.”

“I did.”

“And you kept this from me.”

“Yes.”

She did not soften the admission. That made it more difficult to argue with, though no easier to forgive.

I thought of the Grey Surveyor insisting that instructions be given to everyone at risk. I thought of Halden nearly destroying a Regional Anchor because the necessary warning had not reached him. If secrecy could protect people, it could also leave them standing beside a danger they did not understand.

“Who decided the route should remain hidden?” I asked.

“More than one custodian. And not all of them were Guardians.”

She would say no more until we had reviewed the surviving records. I was beginning to understand that there was a difference between refusing to answer and refusing to claim an authority one did not possess. I could not yet tell how much of Sera's silence belonged to each.

***

The historical crossing records were not a map to be followed at will.

They described small movements made under restrictions, the capacity of a route that could not support armies or ordinary trade, and the consent required before anyone passed through it. Some entries were incomplete. Others had been deliberately kept apart from the operational records used by the Guardians.

I had expected a secret highway. What I found was a record of journeys made sparingly, with people on both sides responsible for deciding whether they should happen.

Sera showed me the limits without naming every custodian.

“Why conceal even this?” I asked.

“Because knowledge of a passage can become a demand to use it. A demand can become a threat. And a threat can become an army at the gate.”

“Then the Guardians decided no one else could know?”

“We helped keep the secret,” she said. “That decision protected some people. It also cost us trust.”

I had been prepared for her to defend every choice. Instead she acknowledged the harm without pretending the danger had disappeared.

The records established that crossings had occurred with custodial consent. They did not make the route public, nor did they give the Covenant a right to command it. The surviving gate remained a guarded means of passage, not a replacement for roads, ships and flight paths.

I remembered the voyage to Northwatch, the wet deck beneath my boots and the long wait for a ship that could carry only so much. I had complained then about the distance between places. Now I understood why distance sometimes protected them.

Tarek helped examine the route's practical limits. He treated the old records as instructions that might fail if followed without fresh measurements.

“A small group might cross and return,” he said, “if the path is still within its tested limits. That doesn't mean we can send a company through it.”

We surveyed what could be inspected without exceeding the existing permission. The return path mattered as much as the outward journey. A gate that admitted people but could not bring them home would be another form of trap.

Dromak reviewed the custodial conditions. I watched him reject a proposed shortcut because it assumed permission where none had been given.

“You cannot measure consent with an instrument,” he told me.

“No,” I said. “But we can record whether it was asked for.”

He regarded me for a moment, then returned to the documents.

I prepared an expedition proposal for Corven, describing the capacity, the safety limits and the fact that any journey depended upon approval beyond the Guardians themselves. Corven received it as a request to be considered, not as an order he could issue to another people.

That distinction had become familiar. It was no less important for being familiar.

***

The petition went through the proper custodians.

I had imagined that gaining access to a secret gate would feel like a triumph, the final reward for carrying evidence across so many territories. Instead it felt like standing before another door and explaining why I wished to enter without pretending the door belonged to me.

The request was bounded: a small expedition, an identified purpose, restrictions on movement and research, and an obligation to return by the approved route. The Guardians could prepare the crossing. They could not supply consent on behalf of the people beyond it.

The answer did not come quickly.

During the wait I thought of the ruined gate at Oma'vira's facility. That route had been used to serve a hidden operation. The surviving passage, by contrast, existed within a web of restrictions intended to prevent precisely such misuse. Yet the difference between them was not simply that one had been used by an enemy and the other by allies. It was the difference between movement taken without regard for those affected and movement governed by the people who would bear its consequences.

At last permission was granted under specific conditions.

Sera read the restrictions aloud. We recorded them. No one described the approval as a permanent opening of the route.

Nessa joined the small crossing party. She checked the return arrangements with the same practical discipline she had shown at Northwatch.

“If the route becomes unsafe,” she said, “we return by the agreed procedure. We don't improvise because someone wants to see what lies farther ahead.”

I looked toward the ancient gate.

“Do you think it will work?”

“It has been tested for this crossing,” she said. “That's the answer you get.”

I smiled despite myself. I was beginning to recognise the language of people who had learned to survive by refusing promises they could not keep.

***

The gate did not resemble the great roads I had imagined when I first heard of the Passage network.

Its workings were old and constrained. The space around it had been prepared for a small group, not a caravan. I could see the care with which the custodians had maintained what remained functional, and the signs of limits they had no intention of testing for my curiosity.

We verified the permission once more before entering.

I stepped forward with the others.

For an instant the familiar measures of distance seemed to fail me. There was no road beneath my feet in the ordinary sense, no horizon against which to judge how far I had travelled. Then the crossing ended, and I stood in a place whose air and light I did not recognise.

We had reached Lantern Redoubt.

The first people who met us did not announce themselves as subjects of the Covenant, nor as servants of the Guardians. They received us as visitors whose permission had been recorded and whose conduct would be judged by the terms of that permission.

I had expected another hidden military post. The Redoubt was more than that. It was a maintained place, with people carrying out work that had continued long before my arrival. Its existence did not depend upon my discovering it.

An outpost custodian checked our arrival. Nessa confirmed the crossing party and the return arrangements. I listened while the terms of access were reviewed, trying to understand the names and customs that were still unfamiliar to me.

Then Aru'ven came to meet us.

I had heard fragments of old accounts about the Kel'vari, usually spoken as though they belonged entirely to a vanished age. Standing before Aru'ven, I understood how incomplete those accounts had been.

The Kel'vari were not merely a people preserved in damaged histories.

They were alive.

I found that I had no prepared words.

Aru'ven regarded me without surprise.

“You have come a long way,” he said.

“Longer than I understood.”

“That is often true of visitors.”

He explained the Redoubt's guest rules and the limits of our access. The settlement was not an invitation to wander through every enclave or archive. It was a place with its own custodians, maintenance duties and decisions to make.

I looked around at the people moving through their work. Some watched us openly. Others paid us little attention. I had travelled so far to uncover a secret that I had almost forgotten the secret might be someone's ordinary home.

Sera stood a little apart.

“You knew,” I said quietly.

“I knew they were here.”

“And you let me think they were gone.”

“I allowed you to know less than I did,” she answered. “I won't pretend that has no cost.”

I looked back toward Aru'ven and the Redoubt.

The anger had not left me. But it had changed. I no longer wanted only an explanation for why the Guardians had kept their gate hidden. I wanted to understand what the Kel'vari themselves had chosen, what they had endured, and what they might fear from the people who had now learned of them.

***

Our first work beyond the Redoubt was not an expedition into forbidden history.

Aru'ven asked for help with a missing work party at an authorised site. Equipment had been left behind, and the people responsible had not returned when expected.

The request was painfully familiar. Darian's missing worker. Joss's quarry crew. The stranded supply party at Northwatch. I had crossed an ancient gate into a land I scarcely understood, and someone still needed help bringing neighbours home.

We went only where permission allowed. We found the workers, protected them from the immediate danger and recovered their equipment. When we returned, Aru'ven checked the account without treating our assistance as a reason to widen our access.

“Thank you,” he said. “The work mattered to those who depend upon it.”

I thought of Elara's first assignment in Millhaven. Speak to the people who know the place. Learn what they need. Do not promise more than you can do.

The distance between those two moments seemed immense. The responsibility felt much the same.

Aru'ven later explained the conditions under which our party might remain for further research. The boundaries were specific. Some places could be visited under supervision. Others were not open to us. Records belonged to their custodians, and the presence of a Guardian did not erase that authority.

I accepted the terms.

I had once resented every permission as another delay between me and the truth. Now I was beginning to see that the truth I wanted was inseparable from the people who had kept it alive.

That evening I stood where I could see the Redoubt's lights and the ancient gate by which we had arrived.

Beyond the permitted approaches lay the remains of routes older than the Covenant and Dominion, and histories that had survived without our knowing them. I wanted to see everything. I wanted to ask every question at once.

But the people here had not endured centuries of secrecy so that I could satisfy my curiosity in a single night.

I had crossed the Shroud's boundary and found a living people where I had expected only ruins.

For the first time, the great mystery before me was not how to enter a hidden place.

It was how to be worthy of being allowed to remain.


### Chapter Twelve — The Names Beneath the Dust

I had expected the ruins to be silent.

They were not.

Wind moved through the broken stones of the old Passage approach, and water found its way through channels whose builders had long since abandoned them. Somewhere beyond the permitted path, a bird called from cover. The sounds were ordinary. That was what unsettled me. I had crossed a guarded gate into a land almost erased from the histories I knew, and the world had not stopped living while the records of it were lost.

Aru'ven met our party at the edge of the authorised route. He reviewed the boundary markers with the patience of someone who had explained them to visitors before and did not expect curiosity to make them less necessary.

“The remains are unstable,” he said. “You may examine the marked approach. You may not attempt to restore the gate.”

“I only want to know how Oma'vira reached it.”

“Then look for evidence of her passage. Do not make a second intrusion in the name of understanding the first.”

The distinction struck harder than I expected. At the Chainworks I had found captives whose choices had been taken from them. Here I was being asked to respect the choices of people who had spent generations protecting what remained of their homeland.

We followed the marked approach.

The ancient stones bore traces of work that had once been carefully maintained. Some channels had collapsed. Others ended in broken fittings that no longer carried any useful load. The Passage itself was not merely dormant, waiting for a clever hand to wake it. Its failure was part of the history we had come to investigate.

I found marks that did not belong to the original construction. Their placement matched details from the records recovered at Oma'vira's facility. We copied their positions, compared them with the authenticated schematic and recorded the intrusion without disturbing the ruined gate.

Sera examined the result.

“Her route reached this place,” she said.

“And the gate is still broken.”

“Yes.”

I looked at the stonework, trying to imagine the movement of people through a passage that no longer existed. I thought of the caravans I had followed from Millhaven and the ships that had carried me to Northwatch. Every road I knew could fail in a storm or be closed by war. The old Passage had failed on a scale that had changed the lives of entire peoples.

Aru'ven watched me study the ruins.

“You're wondering what it was like before,” he said.

“I am.”

“So are we, sometimes. But we live with what remains, not only with what was lost.”

He led us back by the authorised path. I did not ask to go farther.

***

The damaged warning came from a protected store whose custodians had been trying to stabilise records threatened by age and neglect. I had seen paper burned, ledgers falsified and instructions stripped of their essential qualifications. This record was different. Its damage had not been made to deceive. Time and exposure had done their work without intention.

The surviving text was fragile enough that I was afraid to breathe too close to it.

A custodian showed me how to support the material before copying it. We worked slowly, preserving the words that remained and leaving gaps where the surface had been lost. I wanted to supply the missing phrases. Some seemed obvious from the lines around them. The custodian stopped me before I could turn a guess into ink.

“Copy what survives,” they said. “If you have an interpretation, place it beside the record, not inside it.”

I remembered Lysa's instruction in Valecrown: *Don't tidy the entries to fit a theory.*

The warning was copied and deposited under the proper authority. It did not explain the entire catastrophe that had broken the Passage network. It preserved one more piece of the caution that earlier generations had tried to leave behind.

That evening I read the copy again. I could not shake the thought that the people who wrote it had expected someone to listen.

***

The first reference to the Sixth Scholar appeared among records I had been permitted to review.

I had heard of the ancient project before, in fragments of history and in the guarded language of the Grey Surveyor. I knew that the First Anchor had involved work whose consequences reached far beyond its makers. I did not yet know enough to separate every name in the surviving accounts from the titles that later generations had attached to them.

The reference described a verifier whose role had been to test the stability of a combined working. The surviving notation was distinctive, but the identity behind it was not supplied in the material before me.

I brought the reference to Sera.

“There was a sixth?” I asked.

“There was,” she said.

“Someone who checked the others?”

“Someone whose work mattered to the project. The surviving records deserve a proper comparison before you draw more from that.”

I wanted to ask whether the Grey Surveyor knew the name. I almost did. Instead I asked how I could see the records that might answer the question.

Sera told me that an audience would require permission from the custodians of a more restricted archive. We could petition for a supervised meeting. We could not demand one.

The request took us toward Oth'irun.

I had begun to recognise the rhythm of this journey: a discovery, a boundary, a request to those who bore responsibility for what lay beyond it. Yet each boundary was different. At Valecrown, Mara had protected the integrity of testimony. At Kar Durn, Brynja had protected the meaning of a design. Here the custodians protected a history that outsiders had nearly forgotten existed.

I wondered whether they would regard my questions as useful, dangerous or merely tiresome.

***

Sel'ora met us at a protected approach to the archive.

She did not begin by asking what I wished to know. She began by explaining what I was permitted to do.

“The records here are not prizes for whoever reaches them first,” she said. “Some concern people still living. Some preserve testimony whose meaning can be damaged by careless copying. You will work under supervision.”

“I understand.”

“Then show me that you do.”

A copyist had failed to return from an assigned route near the archive stores. Sel'ora asked us to help recover the missing person before we proceeded with the inquiry. The request was not a bargain in which a life was exchanged for access. It was work that needed doing, and we were present with the means to help.

We followed the authorised approach, found the copyist and brought them back with the records they had been protecting. Sel'ora checked the person's condition before examining the material.

“Thank you,” she said. “Now we can speak about the limits of your request.”

I had expected her to be impressed by the evidence I carried from the Covenant. Instead she was interested in whether I could distinguish a source from an interpretation, and whether I understood that a record might remain restricted even after I had helped preserve it.

I told her about the damaged warning, the altered drawings and the independent copies that had survived attempts to destroy them.

She listened carefully.

“You have learned why records matter,” she said. “That is a beginning.”

***

One of the archive stores had been exposed to danger, and Sel'ora permitted us to help rehouse the threatened material. We worked beside custodians who knew which records could be moved together and which had to remain separate. I carried cases, checked labels and followed instructions. The labour was less dramatic than fighting through the Chainworks. It felt no less important.

When the work was complete, Sel'ora authorised a bounded comparison of records concerning the ancient project.

The first document was a list of six names associated with the First Anchor. The second was a preserved image of the verifier's notation. The third contained a stability sequence whose structure reminded me of the Grey Surveyor's corrections during the inversion trials.

I set them side by side.

The names belonged to a world I had not known existed when I first came to Millhaven. Vaer'uun. Thes'var. Su'vareth. Oma'vira. Khor'vun. And a sixth whose place in the records had not been explained to me.

I knew Oma'vira now as the Harbinger we had defeated. Seeing her name among the original scholars made the past feel suddenly close. She had not always been a title spoken with fear. She had once worked beside others on a project whose legitimate purpose had been presented to their people as worthy of approval.

I looked at the verifier's mark again.

“Someone here understood how the working could fail,” I said.

Sel'ora did not answer for the record.

“Compare the sequence,” she said. “Then ask the person who can speak to it.”

The stability notation matched features I had seen in the Grey Surveyor's instructions. I had watched him correct measurements at Drom Kar, refuse unsafe assumptions and insist that people at risk receive complete information. The resemblance was too precise to ignore.

Yet I had been wrong before when I allowed resemblance to become identity.

I asked Sel'ora for an independent comparison.

She directed us to Leth'ari, who held instrument records preserved apart from the project list. We checked the signature against those records without relying upon the Surveyor's own account. The agreement was real.

I felt the answer approaching and resisted the urge to name it before the evidence did.

***

The Grey Surveyor received the authenticated comparisons without surprise.

That unsettled me more than a denial would have.

I placed the project list before him, then the verifier image and the independently preserved instrument sequence.

“These are the same corrections you taught us to make,” I said.

“They are related.”

“Related how?”

He looked at the records for a long time. I had seen him silent beside dangerous machinery and incomplete instructions. This silence was different. It seemed to belong to memory rather than calculation.

“I have asked you to trust measurements,” he said at last. “You have done the work to test them without trusting me.”

“Then tell me who made the original mark.”

He raised his eyes to mine.

“I did.”

The words were quiet. For a moment I thought I had misunderstood them.

“You were there?”

“Yes.”

I looked again at the six names. The final one seemed to stand apart from the others, though the ink was no different.

“Oth'varen,” I said.

He did not deny it.

“I am Oth'varen.”

I had imagined the Sixth Scholar as a figure preserved in stone, perhaps dead for centuries, perhaps remembered only through arguments about what he had known. I had travelled beside him, followed his instructions and argued with his caution. I had asked him questions he had answered only in part.

Anger came first.

“You knew what I was looking for.”

“Yes.”

“And you let me search for someone standing in front of me.”

“I did.”

“Why?”

He looked toward the archive records.

“Because I feared what the knowledge would bring. Because I believed concealment could protect people who had already lost too much. And because I was wrong to let that fear decide everything.”

I wanted to accuse him of making me a fool. I wanted to ask how many people had suffered while he kept his name hidden. I also remembered the lives saved by his warnings, the careful experiments and the insistence that the truth survive any single witness.

“You could have told us,” I said.

“I could have.”

He did not ask me to forgive him.

Instead he invited me to compare his account with the independent records once more. He would speak of what he had witnessed, but he would not claim that memory alone was sufficient proof of every event.

I sat opposite him.

For the first time, the history of the First Anchor was not merely a set of names and damaged instructions. One of the people who had worked upon it was alive, carrying centuries of knowledge and the consequences of having kept so much of it to himself.

I thought of the captives at Oma'vira's facility, of the workers saved from the freight works, of the ruined Passage and the living Kel'vari who had endured beyond the world's knowledge.

I had come seeking the Sixth Scholar.

I had found someone whose greatest struggle was not to remember the past, but to decide how much of it he could bear to share.

“Then begin,” I said.

Oth'varen looked down at the records.

And he began to tell me what the six had tried to build.


### Chapter Thirteen — The Six Reunited

Oth'varen did not begin with the catastrophe.

He began with a table.

“There were six places,” he said. “And six people who believed that if we worked carefully enough, we might learn something no one had learned before.”

The records lay between us in Sel'ora's archive. The room was quiet except for the small sounds of pages being turned and the measured movements of the custodians. I had imagined that a survivor of the First Anchor would speak in the language of divine power and lost civilisations. Instead he seemed to be remembering a room in which colleagues argued over where to put their instruments.

“Was it always dangerous?” I asked.

“All work that reaches beyond what is known carries danger. That is why we had procedures, independent verification and limits. We were not supposed to abandon those things when the results became interesting.”

He touched the verifier's mark on the preserved image.

“That was my work. Thes'var designed structures that made new arrangements possible. I tested whether those arrangements could remain stable. We disagreed often. It was useful to disagree.”

“Did you trust him?”

“I did.”

He said it without qualification, and for a moment I saw the cost of the answer.

Oth'varen spoke of Vaer'uun's ability to give difficult questions a shared purpose, of Thes'var's restless ingenuity, of Su'vareth's skill in guarding sensitive exchanges, of Oma'vira's study of consciousness and perception, and of Khor'vun's concern for safeguards. They had been scholars before their names became titles spoken with fear.

I had seen Oma'vira die. I had seen what she made of other people's minds. Hearing that she had once tried to understand how perception could be tested safely did not soften what she had done. It made her choices harder to dismiss as the actions of a creature that had never been anything else.

“What changed?” I asked.

Oth'varen looked toward the instrument records.

“The results began to exceed our explanations. We were attempting work whose stated purpose was communication with Ascendant. The six-field experiments produced effects that seemed to point beyond what we expected. We could repeat some of them. We could not account for them.”

“And the others thought they had found something greater.”

“Some came to believe that ordinary verification was an obstacle rather than a protection.”

He told me how his objections grew more urgent. The five concealed changes to the First Anchor's working, persuaded that the apparent intelligence beyond their measurements offered knowledge worth the risk. He had challenged their assumptions, but he had not known the full extent of what they had altered before activation.

I wanted to ask why he had not stopped them.

I did ask.

“I tried,” he said.

“Was that enough?”

“No.”

There was no defence in the word.

He described the rupture only within the bounds of what he could attest. The Anchor opened a breach it could not contain. The devastation that followed destroyed the world the scholars had known. The six were transformed by the forces released. Five surrendered to the corrupting influence that had reached through the breach. Oth'varen resisted, though the struggle did not leave him untouched.

He did not describe resistance as purity.

“I felt the pull,” he said. “I still remember how close I came to accepting it.”

I looked at his hands resting beside the records. They were steady.

“Does it ever leave you?”

“No.”

I had wanted the last uncorrupted scholar to be safe from what had taken the others. His answer made the years of his survival seem less like a miracle and more like an effort repeated each day.

***

Sel'ora brought another record, one whose preservation had required independent authentication. It concerned the years after the Binding, when the Shroud had enclosed the ruined homeland and Nhal's guidance had fallen silent.

Oth'varen studied the record before speaking.

“I found them again,” he said.

“The five?”

“Yes.”

He did not ask me to imagine the reunion. The archive held a reconstruction prepared from surviving testimony and records, with its uncertainties documented. Sel'ora explained that the sequence could be viewed without treating every gesture as independently proven. Its purpose was to preserve an event, not to make a memory infallible.

I agreed to witness it.

The chamber darkened around the reconstruction. Ruined walls took shape, and a fractured sanctuary emerged from the archive's preserved account. I could see the outlines of instruments damaged beyond their original use. Five figures waited among them.

Oth'varen stood beside me as the record unfolded. He was watching a younger version of himself enter a place he had once known.

Vaer'uun spoke first.

“You survived.”

The Oth'varen in the reconstruction looked from one transformed colleague to another.

“What have you done?”

Vaer'uun answered as though survival itself justified everything that had followed. He spoke of powers the gods had not comprehended and of knowledge that ordinary limits could never have allowed.

“Five,” Oth'varen said when Vaer'uun counted them together as six.

The word seemed to divide the chamber.

I watched Oma'vira step forward. I remembered the conditioning relays and the captives who had hesitated when told they were free. Here she spoke of the transformation as something chosen, a glimpse of truth that Oth'varen refused to accept.

He answered that something had tried to claim them.

Thes'var stood beside a broken instrument. Even in the damaged reconstruction, his attention returned to the machinery. He argued that the Anchor's catastrophe proved the failure of its structure, not the failure of the principle behind it.

“The breach destroyed our civilisation,” Oth'varen told him.

“Then the structure failed,” Thes'var replied. “Not the principle.”

I felt the terrible familiarity of the argument. At Kar Durn, Brynja had shown me how a small change in a drawing could turn a trusted structure toward disaster. Thes'var had seen the greatest disaster in his people's history and concluded that the next structure must be made more capable of reaching the same forbidden end.

Su'vareth spoke of records and accusations. She had already learned to describe concealment as protection and deception as necessary courage. Khor'vun remained near the entrance, guarding the people he had once been charged to keep safe. His loyalty had endured while the meaning of safety had been turned inside out.

The figures were not strangers to one another. That was what made the reconstruction unbearable. They spoke with the intimacy of people who remembered old arguments, old work and old trust. Each had found a way to make the catastrophe support the conviction that had led toward it.

Vaer'uun offered Oth'varen a place among them.

The reconstruction showed the five drawing closer. Their altered power resonated with the corruption Oth'varen still carried. The invitation was more than speech. For a moment the pressure within the sanctuary seemed to turn upon him.

The Oth'varen in the record resisted.

I watched him force his working into a stable configuration despite the pain it caused. The five did not accept his refusal. Khor'vun moved to bar his departure.

The reconstruction ended without offering reconciliation.

The archive returned around me.

I realised that my hands were clenched.

***

For a while neither of us spoke.

I had seen a battle against the Harbinger. This had been worse in a different way. There were no captives to free and no relay to disable. There was only a meeting in which six people who had once trusted one another discovered that they no longer agreed on what the destruction of their world meant.

“Did you believe you could bring them back?” I asked.

“At first.”

“And afterward?”

“I knew I could not persuade them by repeating the warnings they had already rejected.”

His gaze remained on the place where the reconstruction had stood.

“I was not blameless because I resisted. I had trusted them. I had failed to understand what they were doing soon enough. And afterward I let fear teach me to keep too much knowledge in too few hands.”

I thought of Halden's incomplete shutdown instructions, the threatened archive store, and the Guardians' secret gate. Oth'varen had spent centuries trying to prevent another catastrophe. Some of his caution had saved lives. Some had left people dangerously ignorant.

“You told Sera to share the full safety instructions at Drom Kar,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Would you have done that before?”

He considered the question.

“I hope I would have. I am no longer certain.”

It was the first answer he had given me that felt less like a scholar's correction than a confession.

Sel'ora asked whether we wished to preserve the verified reconstruction with the independent records. Oth'varen agreed, but insisted that the reconstruction remain distinguishable from the original evidence and from his personal testimony.

I helped lodge the account.

It was not forgiveness. It was not a verdict on everything the six had done. It was a record that could be checked by people who had not been present, and whose right to question it did not depend upon Oth'varen's approval.

***

In the days that followed, the archive offered more than one history.

I read an early account of the years after the Sundering, then another preserved independently. They agreed on the devastation and the collapse of the Passage network. They differed in what their authors had witnessed, what they had been told, and what they had chosen to emphasise.

I had once been eager to find a single complete account and discard the rest. Sel'ora made me record the differences instead.

“Two histories may both preserve truth,” she said, “without either possessing all of it.”

One set of records led toward the Shattered Delegation, an event much closer to my own lifetime than the First Anchor. Its dates and dispatches did not agree cleanly. The official accounts had helped harden the Covenant–Dominion war, yet the evidence before us suggested that some messages and movements deserved a fresh inquiry.

I remembered Aldren speaking of Edric.

*I have never been certain whose hand struck first.*

The uncertainty had followed me from Valecrown into a history older than the kingdoms themselves. I did not want to return to Aldren with another suspicion dressed as proof.

Mara and Oth'varen reviewed the proposed inquiry separately. Oth'varen could explain the historical context of the records, but he would not claim to know every act committed during the Delegation. Mara insisted that any modern investigation would require its own witnesses and authorisations.

Sera brought a request from custodians whose work had become vulnerable as our inquiries drew attention. We helped protect the archive frontier through an authorised route, without treating the need for security as permission to expose restricted records.

When the immediate danger passed, Oth'varen asked to speak with me.

“The Delegation's records may contain a trail worth following,” he said. “But I will not ask you to accept my conclusions in place of the evidence.”

“What do you want me to do?”

“Take the bounded inquiry to those who can authorise it. Let the Covenant examine its own records. Let the custodians decide what may be shared. And remember that the people across the war are not merely pieces of our history.”

I thought of the Vaelari engineer whose rejected inspection had helped us, though she had never agreed to work with us. I thought of Garrick, who had opposed peace without murdering Edric. I thought of Cassian, who had used a position of trust to help destroy the possibility of that peace.

“Do you think the war began because someone wanted it to?” I asked.

“I think people made choices that helped it continue. We must learn which choices were theirs before we assign the blame.”

It was not the answer I had wanted. It was the answer I could carry without betraying what Mara had taught me.

I left the archive with authorised copies and an inquiry that would lead back toward the living politics of Theia.

Behind me, Oth'varen remained among the records of the five people he had once called friends. I no longer believed that his survival made him wiser than everyone who had died. I knew only that he had lived long enough to see how much harm could be done by certainty, and how much courage it might take to share knowledge without controlling what others did with it.

I had come to Oth'irun looking for the Sixth Scholar.

I left knowing his name, but with more questions about him than before.

That seemed, at last, like the beginning of trust.

# THEIA — THE INTERWOVEN CHRONICLE

Before there was a world, there was no sky to divide above from below, no distance to cross, and no moment by which one event could be measured against another. Neither the Physical Realm nor the Void existed. There were no realms at all, no established laws of nature, and no boundary separating what could exist from what could not. Yet two primordial essences endured beyond every condition that later beings would call reality.

**Aethyr** was the supreme creative will: not merely a god of life or light, but the potential for existence itself, the power by which something might arise where nothing had been. **Nhal** was its opposite: not a creature of darkness dwelling in some already formed abyss, but the supreme will to negate, to undo whatever might be made, until even the possibility of its existence was extinguished. Their power was older and more fundamental than the gods who would one day emerge. Where Aethyr sought to bring forth creation, Nhal sought to unmake it.

Their struggle had no battlefield, because space did not yet exist. It had no beginning that could be recorded, because time had not yet begun. Later minds might describe it as lasting eons, but even that word borrows a measure from a universe that had not been born. Every expression of Aethyr's creative purpose met Nhal's opposing will; every act of unmaking was resisted by the possibility of becoming. Neither could overcome the other. Their contest settled into an immeasurable stalemate, and no creation could be assured of enduring while its destruction remained within Nhal's reach.

In that impasse Aethyr conceived a different kind of victory. To defeat Nhal outright had proved impossible. But perhaps Nhal did not need to be destroyed. If creation could be given a realm of its own, and Nhal confined to another realm from which it could not freely reach what Aethyr made, existence might finally be allowed to unfold. The answer was not greater force in the old struggle; it was to establish the very conditions under which that struggle could no longer proceed as before.

The cost was Aethyr itself.

Aethyr understood that creating such a separation would require an act beyond ordinary creation: the breaking of its own primordial unity. The power that had resisted Nhal for an immeasurable existence would be spent in a single deliberate sacrifice. Aethyr would not remain whole to govern the result, to witness its future, or to defend each new life personally. Yet if the plan succeeded, what came afterward would have something Aethyr had never been able to guarantee—**the chance to exist without being immediately unmade**.

And so Aethyr chose.

In an instant beyond the measure of any clock, Aethyr shattered itself.

This was the **First Sundering**. The eruption of primordial energy did not merely fill an empty universe; it **brought two distinct realms into existence**. One was the **Physical Realm**, in which space, time, matter and the fundamental laws of magic could arise and endure. The other was the **Void**, not a second world of mountains and cities, but a realm of negation beyond the conditions of ordinary physical existence.

The force of the shattering drove Nhal into the newly formed Void. Aethyr's own shattered energies passed into the Physical Realm. In its final creative act, Aethyr established a **Veil between the two realms**—a fundamental separation intended to prevent Nhal from reaching across and unmaking the existence now taking shape.

It was not a prison built around an existing abyss. The prison, the world it protected, and the boundary between them were consequences of the same sacrifice. **Aethyr had changed the terms of the primordial conflict by creating the separation that neither essence had previously possessed.**

Nhal survived. Its nature was primordial and could not be extinguished merely by being cast beyond the new physical order. But survival was not freedom. Nhal was confined beyond the Veil, unable simply to reach into the Physical Realm and erase whatever emerged there. Aethyr's purpose had been achieved, at a price no later god could fully comprehend.

The Veil had denied Nhal the power to intervene, **not the ability to observe**. From its confinement Nhal could witness the Physical Realm unfold: the Seven awakening, their differing ambitions, the emergence of mortal races and the increasingly intricate workings of magic. It could learn from what it perceived, but it could not reach across, change a mortal's thoughts, alter a spell, send a warning or touch the smallest stone. Its perception was not omniscience; hidden motives and future choices remained uncertain. For ages it watched Aethyr's fragments build a world it could not unmake. This long enforced observation would later give Nhal knowledge of the ambitions it might exploit, should any means of influence ever become possible.

Aethyr, as a single consciousness, was gone.

Within the Physical Realm, seven great concentrations of its shattered essence awakened as independent beings. They inherited extraordinary power, but not the whole mind, memory or intention of the one who had sacrificed itself. They did not know the long struggle, the revelation that had ended it, or the reason the Veil existed. None was appointed to rule the others. These seven would become known as **the Seven**.

And so creation began with a paradox that would haunt every later age: the first act that made lasting life possible was an act of self-destruction, undertaken to prevent a still-living enemy from destroying all that might follow. The gods would inherit the world made by that choice without understanding its price. Nhal, beyond the Veil, would remember what they had forgotten.

**Aethyr chose sacrifice so that creation could finally have a future.**

In the ages that followed, the Seven felt a strange familiarity with one another but could not explain it. No one was their king. They discovered their powers gradually, learned magical laws rather than inventing them, and developed independent personalities, ambitions and loyalties.

The seven did not awaken together. They emerged into the young Physical Realm at different moments and came to know themselves before they knew one another. No god was appointed sovereign over the others; no voice explained that they were fragments of Aethyr. Their awakenings were separated by stretches of solitude in which each began to understand the world independently. What mattered was that relationships formed through experience rather than being inherited fully made.

Among the earliest and most consequential was the bond between **Guardian and Warrior**. It did not begin in agreement. The young realm was still turbulent, its first enduring formations threatened by the forces that shaped them. Guardian regarded their fragility as a reason to intervene; Warrior questioned whether constant shelter could produce anything capable of standing on its own. Guardian answered that a thing destroyed before it had a chance to grow could never learn strength. Warrior argued that strength granted entirely by another's intervention was not strength discovered for oneself. Neither wanted pointless destruction. Neither was prepared to surrender the principle at stake.

They returned to the disagreement, observed the consequences of their choices and slowly learned to respect what the other understood. Guardian came to recognize the dignity of resilience and independent will. Warrior came to see that protection could make strength possible rather than diminish it. Disagreement became conversation, conversation became cooperation, and cooperation became the closest friendship among the Seven. They would not always agree—especially when honour and sacrifice later came into conflict—but they learned to trust that the other acted from a serious conviction.

Elsewhere, **Seeker** investigated the patterns underlying the newborn realm. The existence of matter and energy did not explain why particular forms endured or why a magical arrangement responded to intention. Seeker learned to distinguish reproducible law from coincidence and to value an unanswered question more than an elegant but untested certainty. **Shaper**, working independently, approached the same world from the opposite direction. Where Seeker wished to understand a phenomenon, Shaper wished to make something with it. Shaper's first works were experiments in form, construction and transformation, each one an argument that reality could become more than it had been.

Their meeting began with an anomaly. Seeker encountered one of Shaper's formations and found that it remained stable despite violating an accepted model. Shaper did not know the full reason it worked; Seeker had not imagined that arrangement. Seeker proposed an adjustment and Shaper gave it form. A structure that had survived by inspired trial became something they could understand, refine and reproduce. Then Shaper tried a variation Seeker had not anticipated and exposed another principle. Each discovered that the other's gift could carry their own further than solitary work.

The collaboration grew into affection and intellectual trust. Seeker admired Shaper's courage to attempt the seemingly impossible. Shaper admired Seeker's ability to turn invention into dependable knowledge. Their investigations transformed the young Physical Realm: stable structures, carefully governed energies and increasingly sophisticated patterns became possible. Keeper would later recognize that the stability of such works depended on rules neither partner always respected; Judge would see that powerful creators needed responsibilities as well as freedom. Yet these concerns had not yet broken the extraordinary confidence of the early age.

The first truly transformative question emerged from Seeker and Shaper's work: **could the principles that sustained a magical form also permit an independent living consciousness?** Not merely a moving construct, nor a servant echoing a maker's intentions, but a being able to experience the world, choose, learn and become something its creator had never planned.

Seeker and Shaper made the foundational discovery that independent mortal life was possible. But possibility was not proof of safety. They could model complex self-sustaining patterns and describe the conditions under which individuality might arise, yet they could not confidently establish what would separate a free consciousness from an extension of divine will, or how such a being could endure without constant intervention. **They chose not to attempt mortal creation yet.** This was a deliberate pause because their knowledge remained incomplete, not a failure of imagination. Seeker believed further understanding was owed to any life they might bring forth. Shaper accepted the pause but felt the frustration of seeing an extraordinary possibility left unrealized.

Their findings reached Guardian and Warrior, who saw in them questions different from those the two researchers had asked. Guardian wanted life that could flourish for its own sake, even if its maker could not predict what it would become. Warrior wanted life that could choose, strive and overcome difficulty through its own will. Their old disagreement—protection against the danger of overprotection—became the source of two complementary approaches to the unresolved problem. Rather than wait for a complete theoretical account, they took Seeker and Shaper's research further through their own understanding of living independence and will.

**Guardian created the elves, the first of the mortal races.** They were not instruments fashioned to worship or obey. Their capacity for life, relationship and adaptation gave them a future their creator could nurture but not wholly command. **Warrior created the orcs**, likewise free to grow beyond their maker's expectations, with the potential for endurance, courage, communal honour and the strength to face adversity. Their creations followed close enough in the earliest history that each god could witness the other's achievement. Neither regarded the other people as a failed version of its own. For Guardian and Warrior, the difference between elves and orcs confirmed the lesson their friendship had taught them: independent life could flourish through more than one set of virtues.

The new races changed the other gods as profoundly as they changed the world. Their lives revealed that the creation of a person was not the completion of a work but the beginning of a history. They made choices, formed bonds and altered their surroundings. Even their divine makers were surprised. Guardian learned the pain of watching a beloved creation risk harm. Warrior discovered that honour freely chosen by mortals meant more than honour simply imposed. Seeker found a new field of inquiry in the unpredictable development of mortal minds. Shaper watched beings transform the world around them and saw the possibility of creators who were themselves creations.

**Shaper then created the dwarves.** Inspired by the capacity of elves and orcs to act independently, Shaper envisioned people who would not merely inhabit a formed world but transform it through craft, construction and enduring works. Their aptitude for making was not a command to reproduce divine designs. It was an invitation to surpass them. When dwarves began inventing forms and techniques Shaper had not taught, the god experienced an extraordinary pride. Mortal admiration for Shaper's visible contributions would grow from these achievements, later becoming one source of Ascendant's envy.

Seeker did not create a separate one of the original six mortal races. Instead, Seeker became increasingly fascinated by mortal discovery itself. The elves, orcs and dwarves could observe, test and challenge even divine explanations. Seeker's role as investigator and teacher would later make the god a principal patron of halflings and Vaelari, though neither people had been created by Seeker. The distinction between **creator** and **patron** began to matter long before the modern religions took shape.

As mortals formed relationships and communities, **Judge** confronted questions that had scarcely existed before free races appeared. What law could govern beings who could refuse their makers? How should competing claims be reconciled? What obligations did power create? Judge created **humans**, whose adaptability and capacity to form intricate institutions reflected those questions. Their later principal devotion to Guardian would show that an origin did not determine a people's permanent faith.

**Keeper** observed another aspect of mortality. A person could be free and still fragile. A community could flourish and still collapse when its sustaining patterns failed. Keeper studied how living systems could remain stable through change without being held motionless by a divine hand. From this concern came **halflings**, whose inherited magical resilience would later express itself in sophisticated guild traditions and engineering. Like humans, halflings would eventually choose a principal patron other than their original creator: Seeker.

By this point **five mortal races existed: elves, orcs, dwarves, humans and halflings**. Their early communities were developing identities, knowledge and relationships independent of their gods. The five creators and Seeker had shared discoveries, disagreements, celebrations and the first anxieties of responsibility. This was the divine family and its living legacy into which Ascendant would enter.

**Ascendant had not only just awakened.** He had spent a long period independently exploring the realm, developing an understanding of adaptation, amplification and synthesis, and shaping a philosophy that emphasized the potential to go beyond an original design. When he finally found the others, he encountered equals who nevertheless possessed a history together that he could not share. Their friendships were established. Their work was visible in five living races. The other gods did not have to reject him deliberately for their ease with one another to feel like exclusion.

He admired much of what they had made. He also wondered whether they had left room for anything truly new. Seeker and Shaper's celebrated collaboration suggested a place for insight and invention, but Shaper was already beloved for the dwarves and other great works. Guardian and Warrior had proved that life could arise from different philosophies. Judge and Keeper had each contributed a people. Ascendant had extraordinary power, but no comparable legacy and no people who knew him as their maker.

His desire to contribute could still have become an uncomplicated source of creativity. Instead, recognition slowly became entangled with identity. He wanted to demonstrate that the world could not merely be preserved, understood or shaped: it could be **improved beyond the limits its first makers had accepted**. Keeper possessed knowledge of magical stability that Ascendant's independent experiments could not fully reproduce. Their future collaboration in creating the trolls therefore offered both a practical partnership and a chance for a lasting legacy. The later Seventh project would carry that same desire to a far more dangerous scale.

Ascendant's creative gifts were genuine, but recognition had become entangled with his sense of worth. Each new achievement offered the possibility of belonging, and each comparison with the others made that belonging feel more uncertain.

Keeper and Ascendant had not been close friends before their collaboration. Ascendant had come late to the divine community, discovering five mortal races already alive and the other gods' achievements already celebrated. Keeper possessed knowledge Ascendant needed: the understanding of how to give living magical structures stability without extinguishing their independence. Ascendant offered a complementary gift, the capacity to amplify and adapt what already existed. Together they began creating the trolls, the sixth mortal people.

Keeper wished to give them a stable magical constitution and the ordinary capacity to endure the changing world. Ascendant contributed an adaptable nature, but privately wanted something more personal from this creation. He had seen elves honour Guardian, orcs revere Warrior and dwarves celebrate Shaper. The new people might finally be a living testament to his own significance. And so he considered a subtle alteration to the sensitivity to primordial energy that each created race inherited through its ancestry.

Through the creation process, **each mortal race inherited traces of Aethyr's primordial energy**, the same fundamental power from which the Seven themselves had emerged. As the gods shaped their creations, that energy manifested in different ways, giving rise to distinct racial affinities and magical potential—not fragments of the gods' personal essences. Over time, studying these manifestations would lead to the discovery and understanding of the **six fundamental fields of magic**. **Ascendant secretly amplified the trolls' sensitivity to this primordial energy and its divine resonance**, hoping they would feel the presence of divinity more keenly and, in time, choose to honour him above the others. He did not give them the ability to heal any wound, survive any blow or evolve new powers when threatened. The alteration concerned their **spiritual receptivity**, not their combat strength. It offered no guarantee of direct conversation with a god, superior spellcasting, exceptional magical reserves or obedience.

The trolls emerged as free mortal beings. They could be injured, fall ill, age and die, as other races could. Their heightened sensitivity to Aethyr's primordial energy could express itself through experiences that their descendants interpreted as spiritual, through devotion to ritual, and through an unusually rich religious imagination. But it could not choose their beliefs for them. A troll could question a divine teaching, reject a priest's interpretation or follow a different patron. A people created with heightened awareness was not a people created without free will.

Keeper eventually recognized that Ascendant had altered the agreed design. The immediate danger was not an army of invulnerable beings; it was the attempt of one creator to shape the religious inclinations of independent lives for personal recognition. Keeper had consented to a stable, resilient people, not to an undisclosed amplification of their sensitivity to the gods. Removing the alteration after the trolls existed risked interfering with living beings who had committed no wrong. Keeper chose to preserve them, but the decision could not restore trust.

Ascendant defended the work: the trolls remained free, and their heightened spiritual awareness could be a genuine gift. Keeper did not deny either point. What Keeper could not accept was that Ascendant had decided, alone, to change the conditions under which those beings would develop their beliefs. The distinction mattered. **Ascendant had not forced the trolls to worship him, but he had tried to influence the path by which they might come to do so.**

The irony would unfold across generations. Troll religious traditions flourished, but devotion did not converge upon Ascendant. Some communities revered the promise of change and growth associated with him. Others increasingly honoured Keeper's restraint, stability and guardianship. Ascendant had helped create a people unusually attentive to divine questions, only to discover that their independence included the freedom to prefer his collaborator. His desire for recognition had produced an achievement he could neither claim exclusively nor control.

The discovery troubled the other gods as well. They admired the lives Keeper and Ascendant had made, yet the concealed alteration raised questions none could easily set aside: whether a creator could change an agreed design without consent, and how the freedom of a people could be protected from the ambitions of those who made them.

The consequences for Keeper and Ascendant were enduring. Keeper became more wary of undisclosed modifications to living magical structures, especially those that might influence the freedom of their inhabitants. Ascendant increasingly believed that his finest achievements would be judged by the manner of their making rather than their results.

Mortals were never confined to their creators' intentions. The orcs developed scholars as well as champions; dwarves became healers as well as masons; elves could choose lives that required no magical training. The traces of Aethyr's primordial energy inherited by mortal bloodlines expressed themselves differently across families and generations. In most, the inheritance was subtle. In rare lineages it could produce unusual aptitude, but neither ancestry nor divine patronage removed the need for training, discipline and choice.

The six peoples spread through the lands that became their ancestral homes. Human settlements followed rivers across inland plains, where dependable harvests made permanent institutions possible. Dwarven communities worked the mountains, opening mines and underground passages before expanding the great defensive and civic structures for which they became renowned. Orc Great Clans established communities across the grasslands, combining martial traditions with permanent settlements, sacred places and councils of their own. Halflings adapted underground valleys into networks of dwellings, waterways, workshops and mechanisms. Elves cultivated settlements within the primeval forests, developing traditions of stewardship alongside their understanding of living magic. Trolls established strongholds in rugged highlands, where chronicles, stonework and protective wards preserved the memory of earlier generations.

These were beginnings, not rigid boundaries. Traders crossed the distances between peoples; scholars travelled to places where unfamiliar methods might answer their questions. A dwarven engineer could learn from a halfling regulator without becoming halfling, and a human settlement might adopt techniques first developed by elves. Geography shaped customs, but contact and migration ensured that no people possessed a single unchanging way of life.

The gods observed these developments with different kinds of pride. Shaper saw mortals extend the possibilities of craft beyond anything directly taught to them. Seeker valued the questions they asked, especially when their experiments challenged accepted explanations. Guardian protected communities beyond the elves, while Judge watched human institutions develop interpretations of law that were not simply repetitions of divine instruction. Their appearances remained rare enough to matter. Much more commonly, mortals solved problems through the knowledge and relationships they built for themselves.

As communities grew, their uses of magic became more deliberate. Practitioners distinguished **six disciplines: Elements & Energy, Matter, Life, Form, Mind and Passage**. No discipline belonged exclusively to a particular race. Inherited affinity could make one field easier to apprehend, but sustained skill required practice and understanding. A working drew upon the practitioner's finite reserves, shaped by intention and precision. Those reserves recovered through rest, ambient energy and disciplined meditation; exhausting them imposed real limits. More complex workings required understanding several disciplines and the relationships between them. A gathering of untrained practitioners could not simply combine their strength into the skill of a master.

These limits encouraged cooperation. A settlement seeking protection might employ a specialist in Form to devise a stable pattern, another in Matter to prepare its supporting structure, and trained ward-keepers to maintain the finished work. Passage experts could establish connections between distant centres, but the undertaking required careful design, substantial resources and continuing maintenance. Life practitioners refined methods of healing while debating the limits of intervention; engineers discovered ways to renew charged objects and preserve complex workings. Extraordinary resurrection, stable matter created from nothing and far-ranging passages remained rare achievements, not ordinary conveniences.

The need to preserve such expertise gradually changed the nature of scholarship. Teachers who had once trained a handful of apprentices began exchanging records with distant colleagues. Schools grew around successful methods, disputed theories and shared ethical obligations. From these traditions emerged **three or four great independent magical orders**, alongside many smaller institutions. They were not divided one-to-one among the six disciplines. Their identities arose from differing philosophies, specialties and public responsibilities, and their members could challenge divine teaching as well as one another.

The orders became places where knowledge survived beyond an individual practitioner. Libraries preserved failed experiments alongside successful ones, allowing later researchers to recognise errors that might otherwise be repeated. Visiting scholars carried methods between cities. Councils debated the ownership of discoveries, the risks of experimental workings and the duties owed to communities that depended upon them. Divine founders, rare appearances and trusted intermediaries influenced their development, but the orders possessed their own authority and could disagree openly with the gods.

Their work increasingly entered daily life. A halfling regulator might maintain the flow of a dwarven defensive system. Elven and human healers could compare treatments across institutions, while Passage engineers joined markets and archives separated by great distances. A city ward created by exceptional practitioners could be maintained by specialists who had never met its designers. Such arrangements allowed communities to benefit from achievements far beyond the abilities of any one resident.

This was the **Age of Wonders**: not a time of limitless magic, but an age in which accumulated knowledge, patient engineering and cooperation made extraordinary achievements dependable. Conventional labour and magical craft remained interwoven. Fields still required cultivation, buildings needed repair, and every complex system depended upon people trained to sustain it.

The prosperity also changed what communities expected from the world around them. A city supplied through Passage routes could let older roads fall into disuse. A settlement protected for generations by a ward might retain fewer conventional defences. An intricate structure could continue operating long after the death of its designer, while the theory needed to rebuild it passed into the care of an increasingly narrow circle of specialists. The achievements of the age were real, and so was the dependence they created.

The greatest danger of the Age of Wonders did not begin as a council of seven gods agreeing to fashion a new people. It began with a private wound in the life of one god.

**Ascendant had never forgotten the trolls.** Their creation with Keeper had been a genuine achievement, and the trolls had become a free people with elaborate spiritual traditions. But Ascendant's concealed amplification of their sensitivity to divine essence had not produced the devotion he desired. Many honoured Keeper. Others honoured both creators, or drew their own conclusions about the gods. Ascendant had attempted to cultivate recognition without abolishing free will; their freedom had answered him in a way he could not control.

He had also learned something about Keeper. Beneath that god's caution lay an extraordinary command of how living magical patterns could endure without collapsing. Their estrangement was real, but Keeper did not wish every future exchange to be poisoned by the first betrayal. In the years that followed, Keeper cautiously permitted Ascendant to examine **limited portions of advanced stability research**. It was an attempt to restore a scholarly relationship under conditions both could understand. The permitted work concerned complex relationships among divine essence, living autonomy and the six disciplines. Certain incomplete experiments, whose effects Keeper could not explain or safely contain, remained restricted.

Ascendant accepted the arrangement. At first the exchange was honest. His insights into adaptation genuinely improved some of Keeper's theoretical models, and Keeper's methods corrected failures in Ascendant's own. But Ascendant noticed absences in the research: references without their underlying proofs, experiments whose conclusions were withheld, and patterns whose permitted forms implied more powerful variants. Keeper had drawn a boundary around knowledge for which no reliable safeguard yet existed. Ascendant began to regard that boundary as the very obstacle that had always prevented his genius from being recognized.

**Using the research Keeper had willingly granted him access to, Ascendant devised his own methods to reconstruct the gaps in the knowledge Keeper had deliberately withheld.** He then extended those recovered principles through his own mastery of amplification and synthesis. He knew he had exceeded the agreement. He did not tell Keeper. He was acting from conviction, wounded pride and the desire to create something undeniably his own.

His ambition had a precise object. The six original mortal races possessed distinct inherited affinities and could all learn magic through training, finite reserves, discipline and effort. Ascendant believed it possible to create a seventh race whose consciousness could apprehend the relationships among magical disciplines with an ease other mortals rarely achieved. Such beings would not possess limitless energy or immunity to death. They would still have to learn, practice, eat, build, argue and live. But they might see structures in Form and Passage that even accomplished scholars overlooked, and advance the arts of the Physical Realm far beyond their makers' expectations.

A second quality arose from the same radical design. The six original species could not ordinarily have children across species boundaries. **Ascendant's seventh race alone would be compatible with any of the six.** Whether he foresaw all the social consequences is not established; the compatibility expressed his central philosophy that creation should be able to cross limitations its predecessors accepted. 

Ascendant worked in secrecy, without the other gods contributing to the act of creation. He tested patterns, discarded failed structures and developed his own methods to combine the six magical fields with a precision previously unimagined in mortals. Many trials failed. Others succeeded because of his persistence and ingenuity. **Nhal neither guided these experiments nor contributed knowledge to the Kel'vari's creation.** The race's extraordinary aptitude was Ascendant's achievement, born of his determination to outshine the other gods. The unintended consequence was a possibility so remote that even Ascendant did not perceive it: in the hands of sufficiently gifted, trained and ambitious descendants, an exceptionally precise combination of all six fields might one day resonate with the Veil. This was not a flaw implanted by Nhal, an inevitable trait of every member of the race, or an open breach. It was an unrealized possibility within a creation whose potential exceeded its creator's foresight.

Beyond the Veil, Nhal watched. It had witnessed the birth of the first six races and the gods' growing relationships; now it saw Ascendant create beings capable of bringing the six expressions of Aethyr's primordial energy together at a depth mortals had never reached. Nhal recognized the significance because it remembered the primordial power that had formed its prison. But **recognition was not intervention**. It could not whisper to Ascendant, improve his results or change the Kel'vari's design. It could only hope that one day these new mortals would discover a configuration capable of disturbing the boundary.

Then the first of the **Kel'vari**, the seventh mortal race, awakened. The living consciousness was its own. Ascendant could not command its choices simply by having made it. More followed. They learned and formed attachments, developed preferences and disagreements, and began a history that did not belong to Ascendant alone. **He was their sole divine creator**, but he was not the author of every act they would undertake. At last he possessed a creation whose divine origin no other god could claim as shared labour.

He revealed them only after their independence and unusual aptitude could no longer be dismissed as speculation. Seeker was astonished by their capacity to understand magical relationships. Shaper admired the scale of Ascendant's accomplishment, even as the speed of its achievement unsettled old assumptions. Guardian saw free persons entitled to protection. Warrior respected their agency but warned that rulers might seek to possess their gifts. Judge questioned the secrecy of a creation whose consequences could affect every people. **Keeper recognized elements of research Ascendant had been forbidden to extend** and knew their agreement had been broken again.

Yet the other gods could not simply condemn the Kel'vari for their creator's conduct. They were alive, independent and, at first, flourishing. Keeper could identify unauthorized methods without proving that the new lives were themselves dangerous. Seeker insisted that a discovery could be examined without punishing its discoverers. Guardian refused to allow the people to become hostages in a dispute among gods. Ascendant heard the reservations as yet another refusal to acknowledge what he had accomplished.

The Kel'vari did not remain a collection of individuals gathered around their creator. As generations passed, they established families, settlements and institutions of their own. Their extraordinary capacity to recognise relationships between magical fields gave them unusual opportunities, but it did not supply food, shelter, governance or the labour upon which any lasting society depended. Farmers, builders, artisans, healers and civic officials were as necessary to their survival as the scholars whose discoveries attracted the attention of other peoples.

Their settlements developed into cities whose institutions reflected both their gifts and their disagreements. Some centres grew around archives and teaching halls; others became important through craft, cultivation, protective research or connections to the wider Passage network. Their ancestral language, **Oth'vaeri**, carried a distinct melodic cadence, with apostrophes marking meaningful sound boundaries in formal names. Shared language and ancestry did not prevent the cities from developing different customs, priorities and political traditions.

The Kel'vari's growing expertise brought them into close contact with the six older races. Visiting scholars exchanged methods with the great magical orders, while merchants and craftspeople carried practical discoveries between settlements. Kel'vari research contributed to improvements in healing, construction, defensive wards and Passage engineering. In return, their institutions drew upon the accumulated experience of civilizations that had existed before their own. They were unusually gifted at understanding complex relationships, but their achievements still depended upon training, cooperation and access to knowledge.

The cities learned that independence and shared responsibility could not always be separated. A local council might govern land, resources and disputes within its own territory, yet a Passage connection joining several cities required common standards and continuing cooperation. Research undertaken in one centre could affect the safety of distant communities. Disagreements over who should approve experiments, maintain shared works or control access to discoveries encouraged the development of broader institutions.

Over time, these arrangements became a **federation of largely autonomous Kel'vari cities**. Civic councils retained authority over local affairs. Scholarly assemblies coordinated research and the exchange of knowledge, while a federal assembly considered shared infrastructure and undertakings whose consequences extended beyond any one city. The federation did not remove rivalry or political ambition, but it provided a means of pursuing achievements that no individual centre could sustain alone.

For Ascendant, the civilization's growth was a source of pride. The people he had created had surpassed the limits he believed the older gods had accepted. Yet their independence meant their achievements belonged to them. Their scholars challenged inherited assumptions, their councils made decisions without seeking divine approval for every undertaking, and their ambitions did not always match those of their creator.

Among the questions that increasingly occupied Kel'vari scholars was the relationship between the six magical fields. Their studies suggested that the disciplines were distinct expressions of a deeper unity, though the principles connecting them remained difficult to reproduce. As their methods improved, the desire to understand that unity became associated with an even greater ambition: establishing a lasting means of communicating with Ascendant.

Such communication could not be achieved merely by possessing unusual aptitude. The gods' appearances were rare, and even the greatest mortal workings remained constrained by the laws of the Physical Realm. Kel'vari scholars therefore approached the undertaking through research, testing and debate. What began as a question of magical theory gradually became a subject of federation-wide interest, bringing together institutions whose cooperation would be essential to any attempt on such a scale.

As generations passed, Kel'vari researchers explored the apparent unity beneath the six magical fields. In one exceptionally difficult experiment they brought all six into a fleeting configuration and observed an unexpected result they could study but not fully explain. Unknown to them, that configuration had briefly **resonated with the Veil**, giving Nhal its first minute opportunity to influence a magical working in the Physical Realm.

Encouraged by their results, the researchers continued combining the six fields in increasingly complex experiments. Whenever they reproduced the particular alignment, the Veil resonated again. Nhal could not command minds, control all magic or speak openly as a god, but through those fleeting opportunities it could favour possible outcomes, help unstable patterns settle and encourage discoveries that seemed to justify further investigation.

The research eventually supported a publicly authorised undertaking: a permanent connection with Ascendant. The Kel'vari federation and its scholarly authorities approved the legitimate objective after debate over cost, safety and the limits of existing knowledge. The undertaking became known as **the First Anchor**.

Six principal scholars guided the project. **Vaer'uun** led resonance and communication research; **Thes'var** designed its complex structures; **Su'vareth** coordinated restricted records and external relationships; **Oma'vira** studied consciousness and perception; **Khor'vun** directed protective workings and containment; and **Oth'varen** independently verified the stability of the combined six fields. They were celebrated colleagues, and Oth'varen counted the other five among his closest friends.

Over years of repeated resonance, five of the scholars came to suspect an intelligence behind their extraordinary results and willingly pursued it. Nhal cultivated their ambitions and gradually distorted their judgement, subtly guiding the project towards an objective known only to itself. Vaer'uun interpreted discoveries as confirmation of a greater purpose. Thes'var incorporated concealed structural alterations. Su'vareth restricted records and concealed discrepancies. Oma'vira encouraged confidence in what she regarded as a revelation. Khor'vun adjusted safeguards to accommodate the altered workings. Their legitimate reputations and divided institutional oversight allowed the deception to continue.

Oth'varen noticed discrepancies between approved designs and operational configurations. Other Kel'vari investigators also uncovered missing records, inconsistent results and evidence of unauthorised alterations. They preserved copies and demanded further scrutiny, though they could neither identify Nhal nor demonstrate the ultimate danger. Oth'varen increasingly feared that his friends were following an influence they did not understand.

As the First Anchor neared completion, the five presented their final findings to the scholarly assemblies and federal authorities. Years of successful experiments, independently celebrated discoveries and the extraordinary reputation of the project's leading researchers gave the assembly confidence in their work.

Concerns had been raised by Oth'varen and a small number of investigators, but the evidence remained incomplete. Discrepancies in the research records and unexplained alterations had been attributed to the complexity of the undertaking, while the five carefully concealed the true extent of their modifications.

The final authorisation was granted. Across the Kel'vari federation, anticipation grew for what promised to be the greatest achievement of their civilization: a lasting connection with Ascendant.

Oth'varen continued his examination. In the activation chamber, with the six principal scholars present, he recognised that the actual configuration was directed towards an unknown destination rather than the promised stable channel to Ascendant. He confronted his five colleagues and pleaded with them to stop. He appealed to their shared work, their friendship and the lives depending on their judgement. They refused. They believed the hidden intelligence had led them to a discovery greater than the original project, and their years of exposure had made them increasingly able to justify what they concealed.

The First Anchor activated.

For a fleeting moment, the six magical fields converged in a configuration of extraordinary precision. The immense structure responded as its creators had anticipated, drawing together energies that no mortal working had ever sustained on such a scale.

Then something changed.

The convergence shifted beyond the boundaries of its intended design. The six fields, brought together through the concealed alterations, began to resonate with something the scholars had never understood. The Anchor reached beyond the Physical Realm.

And **Aethyr's Veil ruptured.**

Through the breach surged a primordial force unlike anything the Kel'vari had encountered. The carefully balanced magical fields convulsed as the power of unmaking entered the working, overwhelming its protective structures and spreading through the vast network of energies sustaining the Anchor.

Within the central chamber, the six scholars stood at the heart of the rupture.

Beyond the Veil, Nhal recognised the opportunity it had cultivated through years of subtle influence. The five who had willingly pursued its guidance were exposed to the immense primordial energies pouring through the breach. Nhal seized upon them, deliberately directing those energies into their living forms.

Vaer'uun, Thes'var, Su'vareth, Oma'vira and Khor'vun were transformed. Their bodies and magical structures were altered beyond the limits of ordinary mortal existence. They gained extraordinary longevity, immense magical capacity and resilience far beyond what any Kel'vari could naturally survive. Yet they had not become gods. Their transformation did not render them invulnerable or incapable of death.

Nhal's attention then turned to the sixth.

Oth'varen had resisted the hidden influence behind the Anchor's research. He had recognised the manipulation and desperately attempted to prevent the activation. Now Nhal sought to claim him as well.

The primordial energies entered his body, altering his physical form and magical structures just as they had transformed his colleagues. But where the five had willingly embraced the influence that guided them, Oth'varen resisted. The power threatened to overwhelm him. His perceptions expanded, his body changed, and within the transformation he felt the same terrible invitation that had drawn the others towards Nhal.

For a moment, he understood the temptation. The power offered possibilities beyond anything he had imagined, an understanding of magic that seemed to transcend the limitations of mortal existence. But beneath that promise, he recognised the same influence that had manipulated their research.

He refused it.

Drawing upon everything he understood about the stability of magical structures, Oth'varen fought to preserve the independence of his own consciousness. He could not prevent the primordial energies from transforming him, but he resisted their attempt to claim his will. The struggle nearly destroyed him. Yet his resistance held.

Like the five, Oth'varen emerged fundamentally changed. His mortal body possessed extraordinary longevity, resilience and magical capacity. But Nhal had failed to secure his allegiance. The corruption remained within him, a persistent influence that would test his resolve throughout his long existence.

**All six had been transformed. Only five had surrendered to Nhal.**

The rupture continued to widen.

The energies flowing through the First Anchor overwhelmed its protective structures. The six magical fields fell out of balance, and the containment wards surrounding the central chamber failed in succession.

**A tremendous discharge of primordial energy erupted from the heart of the First Anchor.**

The shockwave shattered the chamber and devastated the surrounding research complex. Towers fell, laboratories collapsed and the conduits linking the complex to its supporting structures were torn apart. Yet at the centre of the devastation, the First Anchor remained. Its outer mechanisms were ruined, but its core continued to resonate with the breach.

All six transformed scholars were caught in the discharge. Their extraordinary resilience allowed them to survive forces that would have killed ordinary mortals, but they were injured and scattered. Some disappeared into the collapsing galleries; others were thrown into the surrounding devastation. Oth'varen lost sight of his former colleagues as the chamber fell around him. Their precise destinations remained unknown.

When the discharge subsided, the research complex was gone. The six were separated by ruined structures, unstable passages and a landscape beginning to break apart.

None knew which of the others had survived.

Yet the First Anchor continued to operate.

The breach remained open, and the primordial energies flowing through it spread beyond the ruined complex.

**The Kel'vari homeland was the first to suffer.**

Cities were struck by forces their scholars could neither predict nor contain. Buildings collapsed as the magical structures sustaining them failed. Protective wards twisted into dangerous configurations, and great Passage connections broke apart or became unstable. Across the homeland, mountains fractured, valleys collapsed and immense stretches of land were torn apart.

The catastrophe spread beyond the Kel'vari's borders.

The interconnected magical infrastructure of the ancient world carried disturbances far beyond the original breach. Passage networks failed, protective structures collapsed, and systems upon which entire cities depended ceased to function.

Yet the destruction was not confined to magic.

Immense forces tore through the Physical Realm, reshaping the land itself. Great fractures opened across the ancient world. Regions that had once been joined were separated by widening seas, while mountains rose or collapsed and entire coastlines were transformed.

**The continents of the ancient world were breaking apart.**

Civilizations that had flourished through centuries of cooperation found themselves divided by distances that had not previously existed. Ancient roads ended at shattered coastlines. Cities lost access to their surrounding settlements, agricultural regions and distant trading partners.

The devastation was uneven.

Some ancestral cities were destroyed outright. Others survived the immediate catastrophe but lost the infrastructure, resources or knowledge upon which their continued existence depended. Communities that had once relied upon the Passage network for food, trade, protection and scholarship were suddenly isolated.

Across Theia, survivors struggled to understand what had happened. The Kel'vari, whose extraordinary discoveries had once been celebrated throughout the ancient world, now found their homeland devastated and their greatest achievement associated with a catastrophe of unimaginable scale.

But the Kel'vari had not been extinguished.

Some survived among the ruins of their homeland, sheltered by ancient wards, fortified structures or the few places that had escaped the worst destruction. Others had been travelling, studying or living among the six older races when the catastrophe struck.

Those beyond the homeland faced growing suspicion.

As reports of the disaster spread, rulers and communities searched for explanations and someone to hold responsible. Some demanded access to surviving Kel'vari research, believing it might explain the destruction or provide the means to repair it. Others regarded the Kel'vari themselves as dangerous and sought to drive them from their lands.

Among the survivors were scholars who had questioned the First Anchor's research before its activation. They carried records of unexplained experimental results, concealed modifications and discrepancies between the approved designs and the workings that had ultimately been constructed.

The catastrophe had confirmed the danger they feared, but it had not revealed the full nature of the intelligence behind the manipulation. They did not know its name or primordial origin. Nor did they understand the full extent of what had been unleashed.

What they knew was that the First Anchor had been deliberately altered, that their warnings had gone unheeded, and that the resulting catastrophe had devastated the world.

They began seeking allies among the scholars, witnesses and protectors of the six older races, whose own civilizations were struggling with the consequences of the disaster. Their evidence became increasingly important as the gods and mortal peoples attempted to understand the catastrophe and determine who bore responsibility.

The devastation left the gods confronting a catastrophe unlike anything they had previously witnessed. Across Theia, their mortal creations suffered amid ruined cities, fractured lands and the collapse of magical structures that had sustained civilizations for generations.

As the scale of the destruction became clear, attention turned towards the Kel'vari and the research that had brought about the disaster.

Keeper recognised elements of the forbidden stability principles Ascendant had used in creating the Kel'vari. The discovery deepened his suspicions, and he demanded access to the research behind the First Anchor, determined to understand how such a catastrophic working had been constructed.

Ascendant resisted.

He had watched his greatest creation suffer devastation on an unimaginable scale. His people were being blamed for a disaster most had played no part in causing, and he feared that surrendering their knowledge would expose the survivors to further persecution.

To Ascendant, Keeper's demands seemed less an attempt to uncover the truth than another effort to condemn the achievement he had never fully accepted.

The other gods were divided over how to respond.

Judge demanded accountability for those responsible, but insisted that judgement must rest upon evidence. Guardian opposed the persecution of innocent Kel'vari and sought to protect the displaced. Warrior condemned the destruction and demanded that those responsible answer for it, while refusing to treat an entire people as guilty.

Seeker argued that the First Anchor's workings must be understood before their knowledge was suppressed or destroyed. Shaper, witnessing the collapse of the ancient world's infrastructure, feared that indiscriminately dismantling advanced magical structures would condemn even more mortals to suffering.

None of these positions was without merit. Yet each god increasingly regarded the others' priorities as a threat to the people and principles they had sworn to protect.

Their disagreements deepened as the surviving Kel'vari research was examined.

Records were incomplete. Experimental results contradicted established theory, and evidence emerged that the First Anchor's construction had differed from its approved design.

Among those who possessed such evidence was Oth'varen.

Having survived the destruction of the Anchor complex, he sought out the Kel'vari investigators who had questioned the project before its activation. Together, they gathered surviving records, reconstructed the concealed alterations and attempted to establish how their colleagues had been led towards the disastrous working.

Oth'varen knew more than any of them about the final moments of the activation. He had witnessed the transformation of his five colleagues and experienced the same primordial influence attempting to claim him.

Yet even he could not explain its true nature.

He knew that an intelligence had manipulated their research and that the five had willingly pursued it. He did not know its name, its origin or the full extent of its purpose.

The rupture of the Veil had changed more than the physical world. Before the First Anchor's activation, Nhal could influence only particular magical experiments through fleeting resonances. With the breach open, its influence reached those who had willingly followed it with greater clarity.

The five transformed scholars experienced this most strongly. What had once appeared as unexpected insight now took the form of thoughts not wholly their own, impressions that arrived with sudden meaning and, at times, whispers that seemed to speak from beyond their minds. **Nhal had found a means to communicate with its chosen followers.**

The guidance was neither constant nor absolute control. Through the corruption within them, Nhal offered direction, fragments of knowledge and interpretations that reinforced convictions already distorted by years of influence. The five increasingly understood even the destruction of their homeland through the explanations it provided, treating the catastrophe as an incomplete achievement rather than a betrayal.

Other willing followers received weaker impressions, particularly near corrupted structures or during forbidden workings. Their experiences varied, but the communications helped them recover research, conceal evidence and exploit the gods' growing distrust. Nhal did not direct every action. Its followers remained capable of choices, even as their judgement and ambitions grew more closely aligned with its purpose.

Oth'varen also sensed the intelligence through the corruption that had reached him. Sometimes its presence came as a faint whisper or an invitation to stop resisting, particularly when he drew deeply upon his altered power. He never accepted it as an authority. The five, by contrast, increasingly treated the guiding presence as inseparable from their own purpose.

As the investigation continued, those responsible for the First Anchor's secret alterations began working to prevent the truth from emerging.

The surviving followers of the hidden intelligence exploited the growing distrust among the gods. They circulated selected records alongside forged evidence, mixing genuine accounts of Ascendant's concealed methods with false claims that he had deliberately designed the Kel'vari as instruments of destruction.

Other documents suggested that Keeper had known more about the dangers of the research than he had admitted, while conflicting testimony cast doubt upon the intentions of those demanding access to the surviving magical knowledge.

The accusations found willing audiences.

Ascendant, already distrustful of Keeper's motives, withheld further details of his creation of the Kel'vari. Keeper restricted access to dangerous stability principles, fearing that their disclosure might enable another catastrophic working.

Seeker challenged those restrictions, arguing that concealed evidence could neither be tested nor trusted. Judge's attempts to establish responsibility were frustrated by contradictory accounts, while Guardian and Warrior increasingly found themselves protecting communities threatened by reprisals and the seizure of their knowledge.

Each act of concealment strengthened suspicion. Each demand for disclosure seemed to threaten another god's responsibilities.

What had begun as a disagreement over the cause of the catastrophe became a struggle over who could be trusted with the knowledge and power necessary to prevent another.

Mortal rulers, academies and magical orders were drawn into the conflict. Some supported the gods whose principles they shared. Others sought protection for their people, access to surviving infrastructure or control of knowledge that might determine whether their civilizations recovered.

The first attempts to seize forbidden research provoked resistance. Resistance brought harsher measures, and the resulting confrontations deepened the divisions among gods and mortals alike.

**The Divine Schism had become the Divine War.**

Amid the spreading conflict, Oth'varen and the surviving Kel'vari investigators recognised that their evidence could no longer be entrusted to any single divine authority, kingdom or magical institution.

They had already witnessed how respected scholars could be manipulated and how dangerous knowledge could be concealed beneath apparently legitimate research. Now they saw the same pattern of secrecy, accusation and mistrust spreading through the wider world.

They began seeking trusted allies among scholars, witnesses and protectors of the six older races.

Some had already questioned the accounts used to justify the war. Others had protected Kel'vari refugees, preserved records of the catastrophe or resisted attempts to seize dangerous magical knowledge.

Together, they established **the Guardians**.

From their founding, the Guardians were a multiracial organisation, united not by ancestry or allegiance to a particular god, but by a shared purpose: to preserve evidence of the manipulation behind the First Anchor, investigate the unknown intelligence responsible and prevent another catastrophe.

They established hidden repositories and independent cells throughout the surviving civilizations, including within the devastated Kel'vari homeland.

Their members sought to safeguard knowledge without allowing it to be exploited, protect surviving witnesses and preserve the truth against those who would destroy or distort it.

Oth'varen became one of their principal founders and their most important living witness to the First Anchor's activation.

But the Guardians were not his personal order. Their strength came from the different peoples and disciplines brought together by a common mission.

As the Divine War spread, their work became increasingly dangerous.

The followers of the hidden intelligence continued manipulating the conflict, while the gods' own decisions brought further destruction to a world already struggling to survive.

And despite everything Oth'varen had witnessed, the true nature of the power behind the First Anchor remained unknown.

The Divine War brought further destruction to a world already struggling to recover from the Second Sundering.

Surviving Passage routes became strategic objectives, fought over by forces seeking to move armies and supplies through the fractured continents. Fortified cities were besieged for their remaining protective structures, while magical institutions were attacked for the knowledge and artifacts they possessed.

As the war continued, the loss of skilled practitioners and irreplaceable records made the surviving infrastructure increasingly difficult to maintain. Entire communities were forced to abandon settlements that had survived the original catastrophe, while others became isolated as the last dependable routes between them were destroyed.

The Guardians attempted to preserve what knowledge and evidence they could, but their hidden repositories and independent cells could not prevent the wider destruction. Meanwhile, followers of the unknown intelligence continued exploiting the conflict, recovering fragments of the First Anchor's research and pursuing experiments whose true purpose remained concealed.

Amid the devastation, Keeper began noticing disturbances that could not be explained by warfare or the failure of damaged magical structures.

Some protective workings collapsed despite remaining structurally sound. Others behaved unpredictably only when particular magical configurations were attempted. Accounts from distant regions described similar anomalies, even where no direct connection between the affected structures could be established.

Keeper began comparing these incidents with surviving records of the First Anchor's research.

He recognised a troubling similarity. Certain configurations appeared to produce results that favoured further instability or encouraged repeated experimentation, much as the unexplained discoveries preceding the First Anchor's activation had done.

At first, he considered whether the anomalies reflected an undiscovered interaction between the six magical fields. Yet the pattern persisted across different workings and circumstances, and some disturbances appeared to respond to the actions of those investigating them.

The evidence increasingly suggested something beyond ordinary magical instability.

Keeper extended his investigation towards the devastated Kel'vari homeland, where the most severe disturbances continued to originate.

There, studying the corruption surrounding the First Anchor, he recognised that the rupture had created more than a passage for destructive energies.

**Something beyond the Physical Realm was exerting influence through it.**

Keeper could not identify the intelligence or reconstruct the primordial history of Aethyr and Nhal. But he recognised deliberate behaviour in the disturbances, and understood that the breach offered an opportunity for that influence to grow.

The possibility that it might eventually establish a lasting presence within the Physical Realm became his greatest concern.

As Keeper investigated the damage surrounding the First Anchor, a terrible possibility emerged. The breach could not simply be abandoned, nor could the Anchor be safely destroyed. It had become entangled with the rupture in the Veil, and removing it risked tearing away what little still constrained the intrusion. The damaged region would have to be isolated from the rest of the Physical Realm.

Keeper began developing a means of containment. An immense magical boundary, later called **the Shroud**, might preserve what remained beyond the epicentre. But no ordinary spell, nor even the power of a single god, could sustain it. The containment would require a contribution from **all Seven**, their combined divine essences forming the foundation of a barrier capable of resisting the pressure from beyond creation.

As Keeper refined the workings, the true cost became clear. The Seven would not merely expend their power to establish the Shroud. Their essences would have to remain bound to it, continuously sustaining the containment. Once the ritual took hold, none could simply withdraw and resume their former lives.

**Keeper knew the price with certainty.**

Keeper also understood that the others might never willingly accept such a fate. They were already divided by suspicion, grief and war. Some would question the evidence, others would search for alternatives, and still others might refuse to sacrifice their freedom without knowing whether the threat could be contained by less terrible means. Yet every delay risked allowing the breach to grow beyond their ability to restrain it.

Keeper made a deliberate choice. Rather than reveal the permanent nature of the Binding, Keeper would present the containment as a great but temporary expenditure of divine power. Each god would surrender a small portion of their essence to establish the Shroud, accepting exhaustion, weakness or a long recovery as the price of protecting creation. **Keeper knew this was not the truth.** The contributions would not be temporary, and the essences surrendered would become the Shroud's enduring foundation.

When Keeper finally brought the proposal before the other six gods, the warning was met with resistance. Judge demanded proof of the intelligence behind the catastrophe and questioned the authority to impose such a sacrifice. Seeker wanted to reproduce the observations and understand the workings before committing divine power. Shaper feared the proposed containment would condemn what remained of the Kel'vari homeland. Warrior refused to abandon the innocent lives still caught within the devastation. Ascendant, already suspicious of Keeper's judgment and secrecy, resisted the suggestion that his people's greatest achievement had opened a danger beyond their understanding.

Guardian, however, came to accept Keeper's evidence and the urgency of the threat. Guardian's support did not erase the others' doubts, but it gave the proposal a voice they trusted. The debate continued as the war consumed more of the ancient world. Each god weighed the danger against their own principles, the fate of their creations and the consequences of doing nothing.

Eventually, the Seven reached an agreement. Their trust was wounded, and their reasons for accepting the plan were not identical, but they recognized that the breach threatened everything they had made. **They consented to contribute what they believed was a limited portion of their power**, trusting Keeper's assurance that their sacrifice would end once the Shroud had been established.

The final containment would come to be remembered as **The Binding**.

The Seven joined their power in one last act of cooperation. The boundary rose around the devastated homeland. The breach was contained. The spread of Nhal's influence was checked.

Then they tried to withdraw.

They could not.

The structure that held the Shroud was not merely a thing the Seven had built. Their own continuing essences had become its living foundation. They remained conscious and distinct, but bound to a task that would outlast their freedom. Keeper could no longer hide the truth: this had been the intended outcome all along.

**The Binding saved creation through a sacrifice the other gods had not knowingly chosen.**

For Warrior, the deception violated the honour of a freely given promise. For Judge, it violated the conditions under which agreement had been obtained. Seeker would search the structure for a means of release; Shaper would confront the terrible fact that the makers themselves had become the mechanism of their creation. Guardian could recognize the need to protect mortal lives and still be appalled that their right to choose had been taken. Ascendant, whose own earlier concealment had broken Keeper's trust, now faced a betrayal committed on a far greater scale.

The Seven remained bound, and the knowledge that their consent had been obtained through concealment deepened the divisions The Binding had been meant to overcome.

### The Immediate Aftermath of The Binding

The Binding had contained the breach, but the world beyond the Shroud remained devastated.

The Second Sundering had fractured the continents, and the Divine War had brought further destruction to civilizations already struggling to survive. The gods who had once guided their mortal creations were now bound within the great magical barrier surrounding the ruined Kel'vari homeland.

Across Theia, survivors faced a world profoundly changed. Cities that had endured the catastrophe were separated from their former territories. The great Passage network that had connected distant peoples was largely broken, and much of the knowledge needed to restore it had been lost.

Yet not every connection had failed.

A small number of ancient Passage gates remained operational, though at greatly reduced capacity. Where vast numbers of travellers and supplies had once moved between cities, only individuals, small groups or limited consignments could now pass.

These surviving routes provided rare connections between otherwise isolated regions, but their limited capacity made them incapable of restoring the trade and movement upon which the ancient world had depended.

#### Within the Shroud

The Kel'vari homeland lay enclosed within the barrier, its ancient cities shattered and its landscape scarred by the forces unleashed through the First Anchor.

The Shroud restrained the spread of corruption beyond its boundaries, but it did not cleanse the land within.

Around the First Anchor, primordial influence continued to distort magical structures and the surrounding environment. Regions of the homeland remained dangerous or inaccessible, and many surviving settlements were separated by corrupted territory and ruined infrastructure.

The Kel'vari who had survived the catastrophe gathered wherever protection remained possible.

Some found shelter within fortified research complexes, ancient defensive structures or settlements whose protective wards had survived. Others occupied damaged cities or established small refuges around sources of water, cultivable land and surviving magical infrastructure.

Their extraordinary knowledge offered advantages, but it could not replace destroyed food supplies, lost craftspeople or the countless ordinary activities required to sustain a community.

The Guardians maintained cells within the devastated homeland, protecting survivors, recovering evidence and attempting to prevent the dangerous research that had caused the catastrophe from being repeated.

Their work was increasingly difficult. The Shroud had restricted access to the wider world, while corrupted territories and unstable passages made movement between surviving communities hazardous.

Not all Kel'vari within the Shroud were aligned with the Unmade. Most were ordinary survivors, struggling to preserve their families and what remained of their civilization.

#### Beyond the Shroud

Outside the containment, the six older races faced the consequences of the Second Sundering and Divine War without the direct guidance of their creators.

The human communities of the inland plains began restoring their settlements and agricultural lands. The dwarves retained their ancient mountain and subterranean capital, though its surviving infrastructure required extensive repair.

Across the grasslands, the orc Great Clans gathered displaced families and defended the territories that remained habitable. Halfling communities struggled with damaged passages and failing systems within their ancestral underground valleys.

The elves and trolls likewise confronted the destruction of their ancient world. Their civilizations remained culturally connected, though religious and political disagreements were becoming increasingly difficult to reconcile.

The ancestral troll highland capital had survived. Elsewhere, settlements and institutions faced different degrees of destruction, isolation and displacement.

Among the populations beyond the Shroud were Kel'vari who had been travelling, studying or living among the older races when the catastrophe occurred, together with those who had escaped their homeland before The Binding.

Their circumstances varied.

Some found protection among former colleagues and trusted communities. Others faced suspicion or persecution from those who associated their people with the destruction of the ancient world.

The Guardians' cells beyond the Shroud sought to protect vulnerable Kel'vari, preserve surviving evidence and maintain contact with their counterparts inside the containment wherever possible.

The followers of the hidden intelligence had also been scattered.

Some remained within the Shroud, while others had been operating among distant institutions when the catastrophe occurred. Those outside the barrier retained fragments of research, concealed loyalties and whatever contacts they had established before the war.

But the surviving Unmade faced a crisis that none of their preparations had anticipated.

#### The Silence of Nhal

The Binding did more than restrain the corruption spreading from the First Anchor. By enclosing the breach within the Shroud, the Seven disrupted the channels through which the intelligence beyond the Veil had communicated with its followers.

For the five transformed scholars, the change was immediate. The whispers and guiding impressions that had accompanied them through the Divine War ceased. No further instructions came. The presence they had learned to trust was suddenly silent.

They attempted to recover the connection through surviving Anchor workings and familiar configurations. Nothing answered. Their transformation, extraordinary power and distorted convictions remained, but the voice that had reinforced them was gone.

**The voice was gone, but its influence had become part of who they were.**

Across the Unmade, within and beyond the Shroud, dependable communication ceased. Corruption still pressed upon the land around the First Anchor, but pressure was not instruction. Their laboratories and records had already been devastated by the Sundering and war, leaving them with fragments of knowledge and remembered guidance whose source no longer answered.

Oth'varen felt the silence too. For him it brought relief rather than abandonment, although the corruption within him remained and he knew that the intelligence had not been destroyed.

For some followers the silence brought uncertainty; others believed the gods had separated them from their patron. None could know how long it would last.

### The First Years of Silence

The five transformed scholars, however, had survived.

Scattered by the destruction of the First Anchor complex, they had endured the Divine War and the devastation of their homeland. In the years following The Binding, they began gathering the remaining followers of the intelligence they had served.

Their extraordinary longevity, magical power and knowledge of the original research placed them beyond the authority of ordinary Unmade practitioners.

Yet the corruption that had transformed them had also become deeply rooted in their thoughts and ambitions.

They did not interpret the destruction of their civilization as a reason to abandon their pursuit. Instead, they saw the catastrophe as evidence that their greatest undertaking had been incomplete.

The loss of Nhal's guidance made their continued work more difficult, but it did not diminish their conviction.

From the surviving followers and fragments of their former research, they established **the Corrupted Conclave**.

Vaer'uun assumed the role of **the First Voice**, interpreting the remembered intentions of the intelligence they served.

Thes'var became **the Architect**, determined to recover the lost workings of the First Anchor.

Su'vareth, **the Veiled Hand**, began restoring covert connections with followers beyond the Shroud.

Oma'vira, **the Harbinger**, preserved devotion and recruited new adherents.

Khor'vun, **the Warden**, secured the remaining strongholds and protected the approaches to the First Anchor.

Together, the five assumed leadership of the Unmade.

Their authority did not come from a new instruction from Nhal. It arose from their surviving knowledge, extraordinary abilities and shared determination to continue the work they had begun.

#### The First Reunion

Several years after The Binding, Oth'varen followed traces of familiar but altered magical workmanship to a ruined Kel'vari research sanctuary. Its protective patterns had been distorted, yet beneath the new arrangements he recognised the precision of the scholar who had once worked beside him. Thes'var's designs remained unmistakable.

Oth'varen had not known which of his five former colleagues had survived the discharge that destroyed the First Anchor complex. He had searched for evidence through the war and the first years of containment, fearing what their survival might mean. Within the sanctuary he found all five alive, their altered forms still bearing the consequences of the breach. They had gathered surviving followers and established authority over the Unmade. Their long exposure to Nhal's influence had not faded with the loss of its voice.

The reunion confronted him with the distance between the people he remembered and those who stood before him. Vaer'uun, now the First Voice, regarded their transformation as evidence that they had reached beyond the gods' limitations. Thes'var, the Architect, treated the catastrophe as proof that the Anchor's construction had been insufficient, not that its purpose was wrong. Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand, justified the concealment of their research as protection from those who would have suppressed it. Oma'vira, the Harbinger, spoke of their transformation as a revelation, while Khor'vun, the Warden, saw the defence of the five and their followers as the duty that remained to him.

Oth'varen had once trusted each of them for different reasons. He had argued with Thes'var over the stability of their designs, relied upon Khor'vun's safeguards and believed Vaer'uun capable of recognising the limits of a discovery. Their answers revealed that those very strengths had been bent towards Nhal's purpose. The destruction of their homeland had not restored their judgement. They had instead learned to interpret it as an incomplete achievement, a sacrifice justified by what they still believed could be attained.

When they invited him to rejoin them, the corruption that had entered Oth'varen during the breach responded to their combined presence. He felt the attraction of the power they had embraced, not as a fresh command from Nhal, whose reliable communication had ceased, but as a resonance among beings transformed by the same primordial force. He drew upon his understanding of magical stability to resist it. The effort caused him pain, and he recognised how vulnerable he remained even after years of refusing the influence.

His refusal ended the encounter. The sanctuary's protective workings turned against him, and Khor'vun barred his departure. Oth'varen disrupted the wards while Thes'var attempted to restore them, breaking their containment long enough to escape into the ruins. He did not defeat the five, nor could he bring himself to destroy them. But he had seen enough to understand that the danger they represented had survived the catastrophe and The Binding.

He returned to the Guardians knowing that their task extended beyond preserving the evidence of the First Anchor. The five still sought to continue their work, and their corrupted convictions made another disaster possible. For Oth'varen, that knowledge carried the grief of an old friendship as well as the responsibility of preventing its consequences.

### The First Generations — Survival Becomes Inheritance

Children born after The Binding inherited a world of broken passages, separated lands and absent gods. For their parents these were fresh wounds; for their descendants they became the conditions of everyday life.

#### Within the Shroud

In the generations following The Binding, the surviving Kel'vari struggled to rebuild their shattered civilization. The corruption spreading across their homeland, together with the destruction of ancient roads and Passage networks, left many communities isolated from one another.

Without the means to restore their former federation, survivors established independent settlements around the few locations where ancient infrastructure, protective wards and essential resources remained intact.

Over time, these refuges developed into **distinct Kel'vari enclaves**, each shaped by the surviving institutions and resources upon which its people depended.

Among the most significant was **Veyr'athun**, a fortified enclave established around an ancient centre of defensive research. Its surviving protective workings provided shelter against the corruption surrounding it.

**Oth'irun** emerged around one of the civilization's great archives, where scholars and custodians dedicated themselves to preserving the knowledge and records that had survived the catastrophe.

In the sheltered lands of **Oru'sava**, ancient cultivation and restorative workings allowed its inhabitants to sustain agriculture and provide for communities whose ancestral farmlands had been lost.

**Thuma'rei**, once a major centre of Passage research and transportation, became an important refuge through its surviving gates, maintaining limited connections between otherwise isolated regions.

At **Soru'vaeth**, a former centre of material research and reconstruction, survivors began recovering resources, artifacts and knowledge from the ruins of their ancestral civilization.

These five enclaves were among the most significant surviving Kel'vari settlements, but they were not the only ones. Smaller refuges remained scattered throughout the Shroud, some maintaining limited contact with their neighbours while others became increasingly isolated.

Although the enclaves shared a common ancestry and the memory of their lost civilization, **they remained independent communities rather than reforming a single Kel'vari kingdom**.

As generations passed, their different circumstances shaped distinct traditions and priorities. Archive custodians feared repeating forbidden research, while ward-keepers needed to investigate failing defences. Passage custodians could move only limited supplies and travellers. Reclamation parties sometimes returned with indispensable tools and sometimes disturbed dangers that had lain untouched. Families worked, learned and aged within these refuges, preserving fragments of a civilization no enclave could reproduce in full.

Not every corrupted inhabitant served the Unmade. Some transformed Kel'vari and ancient constructs continued duties they barely understood, defending deserted gates or tending workings that had become dangerous. Others willingly followed the Unmade or were exploited by them. Guardian cells protected particular communities and repositories, but securing one refuge or route could expose another.

As the enclaves struggled to preserve their independence, the Unmade strengthened their remaining strongholds. **Khor'vun, the Warden**, secured routes towards the First Anchor, while **Thes'var, the Architect**, recovered research from ruined laboratories. The teachings of **Oma'vira, the Harbinger**, reached new generations of followers; **Vaer'uun, the First Voice**, enforced his interpretation of their silent patron's purpose. Through **Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand**, contacts beyond the Shroud became more organised, though none of these efforts restored Nhal's voice.

Oth'varen continued to aid the Guardians and protect evidence of the catastrophe. His reunion with the five had shown him that their ambitions survived. He remained a living witness to their transformation, though he could not explain the primordial intelligence's full nature, and the corruption within his own altered body still demanded resistance.

#### Beyond the Shroud

Human communities restored farms and institutions across the inland plains. The dwarves repaired their surviving mountain and subterranean capital, relying upon human food and secure routes while offering engineering, fortification and craft in return. Repeated cooperation created the foundations of a lasting treaty.

Across the grasslands, the orc Great Clans defended their people and sheltered halflings displaced from their underground valleys. The partnership that followed was neither conquest nor subordination: halfling engineering, supply systems and guild organisation complemented the protection and territory offered by the orcs.

Elven communities disputed the responsibilities attached to magical knowledge. Among trolls, the traditions honouring Keeper and Ascendant developed increasingly distinct religious authorities. These differences deepened through institutions, decisions and generations rather than dividing either people in a single moment.

Kel'vari refugees established families among the six older races. **Only the Kel'vari could have children with members of any of those six species**, which could not ordinarily reproduce across species boundaries. Their descendants were not thereby immortal or extraordinarily powerful. Some families preserved their ancestry; others concealed it or gradually lost the records. Guardian cells sought to protect vulnerable descendants and maintain limited contact with the Shroud. Unmade adherents beyond the barrier likewise preserved hidden networks and fragments of research, without the capacity to move armies through the surviving gates.

### The Age of Reconstruction — New Peoples and New Powers

The first generations had built refuges. Their descendants faced a different task: deciding what kind of world those refuges would become. A protected road could bind communities together, but it could also give one settlement power over another. A restored archive could teach a new generation, or reignite an argument over knowledge that had once brought catastrophe. The arrangements made to survive were becoming laws, borders and institutions.

Human settlements spread again across the inland plains. As harvests became more dependable, councils and local rulers coordinated roads, defence and the disputes of growing towns. A renewed monarchy gathered these communities around a postwar racial capital, while the dwarves rebuilt the ancient mountain metropolis that had endured the Divine War. Neither people could prosper alone: human grain and routes supported dwarven holds, while dwarven engineering secured bridges, fortifications and vital works. Their cooperation deepened into a royal treaty without extinguishing either people's independence.

On the grasslands, the orc Great Clans faced their own questions of shared authority. Protection of displaced kin had brought them into lasting partnership with the halflings, whose guilds organised supplies and whose engineers helped settlements survive. As the halflings established a surface capital and new trading centres, their interests extended beyond the immediate refuge that had first united them. Orc leaders and halfling institutions increasingly found that agreements over roads, protection and exchange could endure where temporary obligations could not.

Among the elves, rebuilding reopened arguments the catastrophe had never settled. Scholars who sought to recover the ancient world's knowledge faced those who believed its unrestrained use had contributed to disaster. At first these disputes concerned the control of archives, the restoration of wards and the authority to approve experiments. They gradually divided communities over the obligations of magical power itself. Their original capital had been lost, leaving survivors to disagree over which traditions should guide the future.

Those who became the **Aelari** built a woodland monarchy in the primeval forest, placing greater trust in Guardian's principles of protection, Life and restraint. Those who became the **Vaelari** gathered around a mountain-lake capital under arcane leadership, honouring Seeker and the pursuit of knowledge. Their choices arose from a shared history, but each increasingly understood the other's path as a danger to what remained of that inheritance. Separation became hostility, sustained by competing institutions and memories.

The trolls also carried an old disagreement into the reconstruction. Traditions honouring Keeper's stability and Ascendant's promise of change had long existed side by side. As religious authorities gained influence over sacred places, customs and political decisions, those differences became harder to reconcile. The **Korrath** retained the ancestral highland capital and its elders' councils, chiefly honouring Keeper. The people who became the **Vorrath** were exiled amid the conflict and established a sacred storm-coast capital under religious leadership, chiefly honouring Ascendant. The circumstances of that exile remained contested in the histories their descendants preserved.

The growing divisions drew the older partnerships into wider commitments. The human–dwarf treaty offered a foundation upon which the Aelari and Korrath could join in mutual protection. Together they formed **Covenant**, an alliance of four sovereign civilizations. Its hereditary High Ruler came from a separate branch of the human royal dynasty; its multicultural coastal alliance capital was distinct from the human kingdom's inland capital. The arrangement allowed common defence while preserving separate laws, traditions and rulers.

The orc–halfling partnership likewise broadened as the Vaelari and Vorrath sought allies whose interests and grievances differed from those of Covenant. Their union became **Dominion**, also comprising four sovereign civilizations. Authority over the alliance was vested in a High Ruler chosen through trials involving champions nominated by all four member peoples. Its multicultural highland capital stood apart from the orc racial capital. These political forms emerged through negotiations and competing needs, not because the older races had always been destined to divide into two camps.

Within the Shroud, Kel'vari enclaves followed a different course. Distance, corruption and fragile passages continued to prevent a reunited federation. A settlement that preserved cultivation could not simply share its abundance with every isolated refuge; an archive that survived might possess knowledge another enclave had lost. The Guardians protected particular communities and repositories, but the loss of couriers and the increasing difficulty of contact meant that knowledge travelled less reliably with every generation.

The five original Conclave founders endured while their followers aged and died. Their unnatural longevity allowed them to maintain ambitions across lifetimes, but their power could not restore the guidance lost at The Binding. Beyond the Shroud, Kel'vari descendants increasingly identified with their adopted societies. Much of the old world's scholarship survived only in fragments. Of the three or four great magical orders of the Age of Wonders, **one secretly endured**, while new academies, guild colleges and religious schools developed practices suited to a changed world.

### The Long Age of Silence — Memory Becomes Secrecy

The first Unmade to lose Nhal's voice had expected the silence to end. They remembered the whispers that had followed the First Anchor's rupture, the impressions that seemed to answer questions before they were spoken and the guidance that had given scattered followers a shared direction through the Divine War. Their successors knew those experiences through testimony. Later generations knew them through records, ritual and the insistence of the five transformed scholars who had lived through them.

The silence lasted longer than the lifetimes of ordinary followers. A generation passed, then another, without a reliable answer from beyond the Veil. What had begun as an interruption became a condition around which the Unmade organised their existence. In time, they called those centuries **the Age of Silence**. The name did not mean that the Shroud had become peaceful. The corruption surrounding the First Anchor remained dangerous, and the five founders still possessed formidable power. It meant that the voice which had once guided their undertaking no longer spoke.

The Conclave had to decide how an organisation built around obedience to an absent intelligence could continue. **Vaer'uun, the First Voice**, preserved the authority of the instructions he remembered and insisted that their purpose could be understood even without fresh guidance. **Oma'vira, the Harbinger**, sustained devotion among followers who had never heard Nhal themselves. She gathered accounts, shaped teachings around them and treated doubt as a test of conviction. The memories on which those teachings rested were real, but repetition and interpretation changed how later adherents understood them.

**Thes'var, the Architect**, pursued another answer to the silence. He recovered fragments of the Anchor's research, tested surviving structures and sought the configurations that had once allowed the hidden intelligence to reach them. Failure did not persuade him to abandon the work; he regarded each incomplete reconstruction as evidence of knowledge still missing. **Khor'vun, the Warden**, defended the strongholds, routes and materials on which such experiments depended. He protected the organisation against threats from the corrupted land as well as from surviving Kel'vari and Guardians who opposed it. **Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand**, cultivated connections beyond the Shroud, where distance and political change required patient recruitment rather than direct force.

The five did not always agree about what their absent patron intended. Some followers believed Nhal had withdrawn from them; others regarded the Shroud as an obstacle that must eventually be broken. The Conclave's founders could not appeal to a fresh command to settle every disagreement. Yet their shared corruption, extraordinary longevity and knowledge of the original research kept the Unmade from dissolving. Their followers changed, their interpretations multiplied, and their purpose endured.

The Conclave's long research programme also changed during these centuries. **Thes'var, the Architect**, first attempted to recreate the six-field alignments of the First Anchor. Most workings failed, but a few produced faint, repeatable resonances with the original breach even beyond the Shroud. These **Minor Resonators** disturbed local magical conditions without restoring Nhal's voice. Their value was proof that the damage in the Veil could still be influenced from a distance.

Successive generations improved the designs, preserved measurements and established concealed laboratories. **Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand**, obtained materials and protected distant contacts; **Khor'vun, the Warden**, secured sites; **Oma'vira, the Harbinger**, maintained devotion through repeated failure; and **Vaer'uun, the First Voice**, interpreted progress as fidelity to Nhal's remembered purpose. The work was the Conclave's own, pursued without new instructions from its patron.

Eventually the Architect developed **Regional Anchors**, larger and more stable workings that linked the resonances of multiple installations. Their combined effects extended beyond individual sites, redistributing the accessibility of existing primordial energy and placing a faint cumulative strain upon the Shroud. They created no new magic. Some installations remained active for generations; others failed, fell dormant or were abandoned. Disturbances appeared as isolated anomalies to outside observers. The network remained too weak to restore communication with Nhal, but its foundations had been laid.

Within the Shroud, the Kel'vari enclaves faced a very different passage of time. The survivors who had first sheltered behind functioning wards gave way to children who had never seen the federation's cities intact. A ward-keeper might teach a successor the sequence required to maintain a protective structure while lacking the lost instruments needed to explain its complete design. An archivist might preserve a damaged record for decades, only for its meaning to become disputed after the last reader trained in the old methods died. Families cultivated protected ground, repaired shelters and negotiated over supplies whose movement depended on unreliable routes.

These were living communities, not relics frozen at the moment of catastrophe. Their inhabitants formed attachments, argued over responsibilities and made decisions that shaped later generations. Some communities guarded knowledge jealously because a mistake could endanger everyone behind their walls. Others risked expeditions into corrupted territory to recover resources their neighbours could no longer provide. The five major enclaves retained distinct purposes, while smaller refuges sometimes disappeared or survived with little contact beyond their immediate surroundings.

The Guardians had been founded to preserve evidence and protect those endangered by the First Anchor's manipulation. In the earliest generations, secrecy had served that purpose. A hidden repository could keep forbidden research out of the hands of the Unmade; a concealed refuge could shelter Kel'vari who faced persecution; a courier who knew only part of a route could not betray the whole network if captured.

Over centuries, those same precautions carried a cost. Couriers died before passing on their knowledge. Routes became unusable. A cell might receive instructions to protect a sealed archive but no longer possess the testimony explaining what it contained. Other cells lost contact with their counterparts inside the Shroud and came to regard reports of surviving enclaves as unreliable traditions. The organisation did not choose ignorance in a single decision. **Its protective secrecy gradually became an obstacle to its own memory.**

A few custodians retained the truth. Oth'varen remained among them, carrying firsthand knowledge of the Anchor and the five scholars who had betrayed their people. His extraordinary lifespan did not make the Guardians a single, perfectly informed institution. His continued existence and identity were themselves closely guarded, and no one witness could personally sustain every hidden route, refuge and archive across the world. He watched an order founded to prevent concealed knowledge from causing another catastrophe struggle with the consequences of concealment.

Beyond the Shroud, the Veiled Hand's networks developed the opposite advantage. They could survive without preserving a complete account of their origins. A contact within an academy might pass forbidden research to a successor; a guild intermediary could arrange a shipment without knowing its destination; an officer might justify a concealed operation as service to the alliance they publicly supported. Some agents knowingly served the Unmade. Others were manipulated through ambitions and obligations that required no understanding of Nhal. The remaining low-capacity Passage gates permitted limited covert journeys, but not armies or renewed direct communication.

During the same centuries, Covenant and Dominion grew into powers whose political identities were shaped by the choices of their ancestors. The human–dwarf treaty and orc–halfling partnership had begun in the necessities of survival; the elven division and Vorrath exile carried unresolved grievances into the alliances that followed. Successive rulers inherited claims to territory, sacred places, trade routes and ancient artifacts. Their peoples increasingly interpreted the divine age through separate traditions, even as much of the evidence that might have reconciled those accounts disappeared.

By the end of the long silence, the world contained two increasingly hostile alliances, a hidden order whose members knew unequal portions of its own history, and an Unmade conspiracy whose founders had outlived generations of followers. None of these developments required Nhal to speak. They were the accumulated consequences of choices made while its voice was absent.

The Architect's next ambition was to coordinate the scattered Regional Anchors through more powerful central workings. Early attempts at **Major Anchors** demanded rare artifacts, elaborate protections and cooperation among distant cells. Some remained incomplete or dormant. The Conclave had not yet restored Nhal's voice, and no single installation could accomplish that goal alone.

### The Modern Age — War and the Shattered Delegation

By the modern age, Covenant and Dominion remained hostile alliances of four sovereign civilizations each. The seas separating their continents did not prevent conflict. Warships supplied landing bases, garrisons, resource camps and research outposts on enemy shores. No ordinary public peace or cross-alliance cooperation united them. The Guardians and Unmade operated covertly within both societies.

Covenant's hereditary High Ruler balanced the human monarchy, independent dwarf holds, Aelari royalty and Korrath elders. Dominion's trial-chosen High Ruler answered to orc Great Clans, halfling guilds, Vaelari arcane institutions and Vorrath religious authorities. **Queen Seralyth**, **First Archon Selrion** and **First Elder Dromak** were among the established leaders whose peoples carried these histories into modern government.

Generations of conflict had taken their toll upon Covenant and Dominion.

Along their contested frontiers, settlements had been abandoned, trade routes disrupted and resources diverted towards the demands of war. Maintaining fortified outposts upon distant enemy shores placed an increasing burden upon both alliances, while repeated campaigns brought victories that seldom endured.

For the rulers of both powers, the cost of continued conflict was becoming increasingly difficult to justify.

Yet generations of hostility had left little room for trust. Old grievances remained unresolved, and neither alliance was willing to appear weak before its enemies or its own people.

Despite these obstacles, influential voices within both alliances began advocating for an end to the fighting.

What began as cautious exchanges through intermediaries gradually developed into the possibility of formal negotiations.

The prospect was extraordinary.

For the first time in generations, representatives of Covenant and Dominion were preparing to meet with the authority to explore terms that might bring their long conflict to an end.

The delegations faced considerable opposition. Military commanders questioned the intentions of their enemies, political leaders feared concessions that might weaken their standing, and communities that had suffered through generations of warfare struggled to imagine reconciliation.

Nevertheless, preparations continued.

For the Unmade, the prospect of peace presented a serious threat.

The continuing war had provided their hidden networks with opportunities that would be far more difficult to exploit in a stable world. Missing supplies could be attributed to enemy raids, forbidden research justified as military necessity, and covert operations concealed beneath the suspicion that surrounded every unexplained incident.

Peace threatened to remove that protection.

Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand, recognised the danger. Through agents embedded within the political, military and commercial institutions of both alliances, she began preparing an operation intended not merely to disrupt the negotiations, but to destroy the fragile trust upon which they depended.

The Unmade exploited existing suspicions, arranged the movement of agents and materials, and ensured that carefully selected evidence would implicate both alliances in the violence to come.

When the delegations finally assembled, the attack struck.

**The gathering was devastated, and the hope of peace collapsed with it.**

Among those lost was someone deeply cherished by the orc leader, whose death transformed an already difficult political decision into a profoundly personal tragedy.

The human monarchy suffered an equally devastating loss. The human ruler's most trusted adviser, a figure whose counsel had helped make the negotiations possible, was killed in the attack.

For both leaders, the destruction of the delegation was no longer simply an assault upon their alliances. It was a personal betrayal, made all the more bitter by evidence that appeared to implicate the very people with whom they had sought peace.

In the confusion that followed, evidence emerged suggesting that Covenant had orchestrated the attack. Other accounts implicated Dominion. Witnesses offered contradictory testimony, while documents and materials recovered from the site appeared to confirm the accusations each side was already inclined to believe.

The surviving representatives returned to their peoples carrying different accounts of the same catastrophe.

Covenant condemned what it regarded as an act of Dominion treachery.

Dominion answered with accusations of betrayal by Covenant.

The negotiations were abandoned.

Across both alliances, outrage overwhelmed those who had advocated peace. Leaders who had supported the gathering found their judgement questioned, while military authorities demanded retaliation.

The event became known as **the Shattered Delegation**.

Its consequences spread rapidly.

Armies mobilised, coastal garrisons were reinforced and expeditions pushed deeper into contested territories. Resources that might have supported recovery were once again committed to warfare.

The fragile possibility of reconciliation had been replaced by renewed hostility, strengthened by the conviction on both sides that their enemies had deliberately betrayed an offer of peace.

Behind the conflicting accusations lay the work of the Unmade.

Agents of Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand, had orchestrated the attack and planted evidence designed to ensure that neither alliance would accept the other's account. Their purpose had never been to secure victory for Covenant or Dominion, but to preserve the conflict that allowed their hidden operations to flourish.

The Guardians began investigating the inconsistencies surrounding the attack.

Certain details suggested deliberate manipulation, and fragments of evidence pointed towards individuals whose activities extended across both alliances. Yet the Guardians' own secrecy complicated their efforts. Revealing what they knew risked exposing cells embedded within hostile institutions, while incomplete accusations might provoke further reprisals.

Unable to establish the full truth publicly, they continued their investigation from the shadows.

For Covenant and Dominion, the Shattered Delegation became another enduring grievance, reinforcing generations of suspicion.

For the Unmade, it was a calculated success.

**Two alliances had come closer to peace than they had in generations. Through a single act of manipulation, the Unmade had ensured that their war would continue.**

### The Awakening — The Network Reaches Its Threshold

The Conclave's centuries of experimentation had followed a difficult progression. Minor Resonators established that distant workings could resonate with the original breach. Regional Anchors extended those effects across connected installations. Now the first successful **Major Anchors** could synchronise several Regional Anchors, amplifying the influence of a network rather than a single site.

The first Major Anchor did not awaken the world. Its importance lay in allowing the Conclave to align more of the installations it had built over generations. As further nodes were completed and brought into resonance, their combined pressure upon the Shroud grew. The same workings altered the distribution and accessibility of primordial energy already present in the Physical Realm. They did not create magic, and Nhal was not the source of the energy becoming more readily available.

The earliest effects seemed unrelated. A ward began holding a pattern more easily, while another became dangerously unstable. A healer sustained a working that had previously exceeded their reserves. Dormant instruments responded to new practitioners, and ancient structures produced unfamiliar disturbances. Some communities benefited, others suffered, and scholars disagreed over the cause. Those near active Anchors often faced greater instability, but the cumulative network affected regions far beyond any individual device.

The Guardians investigated incidents without immediately recognising their shared origin. Centuries of compartmentalised records and lost couriers made comparisons difficult. Meanwhile, the Conclave measured the effects more carefully. Its five founders had heard Nhal before The Binding, but they were wary of mistaking chance, corrupted patterns or a rival cell's deception for a returning voice.

Eventually enough Major Anchors and supporting Regional Anchors operated in alignment to cross a critical threshold. The cumulative resonance strained the containment sufficiently for a narrow channel of influence to reach corrupted workings again.

**After generations of silence, Nhal began to communicate again.**

At first the responses came as impressions, brief whispers and knowledge that seemed to arrive without an ordinary source. They were selective and intermittent, not an unrestricted voice or command over every mind. The founders tested independent results until they recognised the same guiding presence they had known during the Divine War. Nhal remained confined beyond the Veil, but the network built without its guidance had restored a limited connection.

The return transformed the Conclave's operations. **Su'vareth, the Veiled Hand**, coordinated agents and recovered caches across both alliances. **Thes'var, the Architect**, could refine the network with new guidance. The other founders mobilised followers and protected installations as the Unmade sought to extend the system further. Their objective was to strain the Shroud and eventually permit more than whispers to pass through the breach.

The Guardians began to recognise that the lesser Anchors were not all equally dangerous to disable. A Minor Resonator could sometimes be destroyed directly. A Regional Anchor might need its supporting wards and conduits disconnected first. A Major Anchor could influence multiple linked installations, so careless destruction risked unpredictable effects. Stopping one node might protect a community without ending the wider awakening or healing the original breach.

Yet the same discovery suggested another possibility. The Anchors operated through arrangements of the six magical fields, not through an energy that belonged to Nhal. Their redistribution of accessible magic was not necessarily inseparable from the harmful resonance directed against the Shroud. **In principle, their configurations might be inverted to reinforce the containment rather than weaken it.**

This was not a simple polarity switch. Inversion would require deep understanding of the interdependent fields and the safe reconstruction of supporting structures. A Minor Resonator might be reconfigured locally; a Regional Anchor would require coordinated adjustment of its connections; a Major Anchor might demand control over several linked installations. **Oth'varen's expertise in stabilising six-field workings** and the Guardians' preserved evidence could prove vital, but a complete method had not yet been established.

If successful, inverted Anchors might preserve some of the awakening's beneficial redistribution of primordial energy while reducing corruption and strengthening the Shroud. They might even lessen the burden borne by the Seven, though neither their release nor a permanent replacement for their divine essences was assured. Whether inversion could be achieved safely, and what its ultimate costs might be, remained for later discovery.

The Conclave had spent centuries creating a network to weaken Nhal's prison. Its own achievement might yet become a means of strengthening that prison—but only if others learned to change the workings' purpose before the network was driven further towards rupture.

### Theia at the Threshold

Across the two alliances, the awakening entered lives already shaped by local obligations and inherited conflict. Human frontiers faced betrayal and disputed authority; ancient dwarf mechanisms stirred beneath surviving halls. Aelari sanctuaries struggled with unstable wards, while Vaelari researchers pursued unfamiliar discoveries. Korrath wardstones failed, Vorrath pilgrims disputed strange signs, orc Great Clans contested claims of honour, and halfling engineers reopened sealed ancestral works.

These troubles did not all originate with Nhal. Each people possessed its own history. Yet some incidents concealed Unmade operations, while others drew Guardian investigators towards connections that ordinary rulers could not see. A stolen artifact could travel through a military supply route; a legitimate scholar might unknowingly aid an infiltrator; a ruined Passage chamber might preserve evidence that altered the meaning of a familiar dispute.

Covenant and Dominion continued to act separately and to regard one another as enemies. Their investigations could uncover different fragments of the same conspiracy without producing a public alliance. The Guardians remained cautious about revealing themselves, and the surviving Kel'vari enclaves were still largely unknown beyond the Shroud.

Within that barrier, the First Anchor continued to sustain the wound that the Seven had bound themselves to contain. Beyond it, the descendants of their creations faced a renewed threat whose origins lay in the trust broken during the Age of Wonders. The Corrupted Conclave had endured through the silence. Now its purpose was moving again.

The world had begun to awaken, and the old catastrophe was no longer merely a matter of history.

### Canon and open questions

**Established canon includes:** Aethyr and Nhal as the primordials; Aethyr's deliberate sacrifice creating the realms and Veil; the Seven's independent identities; the six magical disciplines and finite-reserve principles; the six original mortal species and Ascendant's creation of the **Kel'vari**, whose ancestral language is **Oth'vaeri**; the authorised First Anchor project and its six principal scholars; Nhal's deliberate transformation of the five willing scholars and failed attempt to claim **Oth'varen**; the Second Sundering's continental fractures; the Divine War, multiracial Guardians and The Binding; the five founders of the **Corrupted Conclave**; the five named Kel'vari enclaves; the silence and later selective return of Nhal's communication; the eight sovereign civilizations and their Covenant/Dominion memberships; the Shattered Delegation's Unmade authorship; and the hostile alliances' overseas military incursions.

**Still open:** the exact dates of the elven and troll schisms and alliance formation; the cause and timing of the original elven capital's destruction; the precipitating event of the Vorrath exile; the initiating crisis of the halfling migration; the names and detailed histories of additional rulers and enclave leaders; the complete linguistic structure of Oth'vaeri; Oth'varen's modern public identity and precise powers; the costs and limitations of the five founders' transformations; the identity of the surviving magical order; the details of modern Anchor placement and encounters; and the ultimate fate of Nhal and the Seven.

The full dramatic scene **Memory I — The Six Reunited** is preserved in [Theia Memory Scenes](THEIA_MEMORY_SCENES.md). The Chronicle records its historical place and consequences without reproducing its dialogue.
